ID: 0080

SOURCE: IMO Shortlist

YEAR: 2012

TOPIC: Geometry

SUBTOPICS: Radical Axis, Coaxal Circles, Projective Geometry, Directed Ratios, Inversion in Space, Orthocenter

STATEMENT:
Let $ABC$ be a triangle with circumcircle $\omega$, and let $\ell$ be a line having no common point with $\omega$. Let $P$ be the foot of the perpendicular from the center of $\omega$ to $\ell$. The side-lines $BC,CA,AB$ meet $\ell$ at $X,Y,Z$, respectively, where $X,Y,Z$ are all different from $P$. Prove that the circumcircles of triangles $AXP$, $BYP$, and $CZP$ have a common point different from $P$, or are mutually tangent at $P$.

SOLUTION 1:
Let
\[
\omega_A=(AXP),
\qquad
\omega_B=(BYP),
\qquad
\omega_C=(CZP).
\]
We shall prove that these three circles are coaxal.

Let $A'\ne A$ be the second intersection of $\omega$ and $\omega_A$. Define $B'$ and $C'$ analogously. We claim that the lines
\[
AA',\qquad BB',\qquad CC'
\]
are concurrent.

Let
\[
X'=AA'\cap\ell,
\qquad
Y'=BB'\cap\ell,
\qquad
Z'=CC'\cap\ell.
\]
These points exist. For example, if $AA'\parallel\ell$, then the common chord $AA'$ of $\omega$ and $\omega_A$ would be parallel to $\ell$, which would force $\omega_A$ to be tangent to $\ell$ at $P$; since $X\in\omega_A\cap\ell$, this would give $X=P$, contrary to the hypothesis.

Let $O$ and $r$ be the center and radius of $\omega$. All segments on $\ell$ are directed. Taking powers of $X'$ with respect to $\omega_A$ and $\omega$, we get
\[
\overline{X'P}\cdot\overline{X'X}
=\overline{X'A'}\cdot\overline{X'A}
=X'O^2-r^2.
\]
Since $OP\perp\ell$,
\[
X'O^2=X'P^2+OP^2.
\]
Also
\[
\overline{X'X}=\overline{X'P}+\overline{PX}.
\]
After cancellation,
\[
\overline{X'P}\cdot\overline{PX}=OP^2-r^2.
\]
Analogously,
\[
\overline{X'P}\cdot\overline{PX}
=
\overline{Y'P}\cdot\overline{PY}
=
\overline{Z'P}\cdot\overline{PZ}
=k^2,
\tag{1}
\]
where
\[
k^2=OP^2-r^2>0
\]
because $\ell$ does not meet $\omega$.

Let
\[
U=AA'\cap BC,
\qquad
V=BB'\cap CA,
\qquad
W=CC'\cap AB.
\]
We use directed ratios. Menelaus' theorem for the transversal $XYZ=\ell$ of triangle $ABC$ gives
\[
\frac{BX}{CX}\cdot\frac{CY}{AY}\cdot\frac{AZ}{BZ}=1.
\tag{2}
\]
By projecting the line $BC$ to $\ell$ from $A$, the four points
\[
B,C,U,X
\]
correspond to
\[
Z,Y,X',X.
\]
Invariance of cross-ratio therefore gives
\[
\frac{BU/CU}{BX/CX}
=
\frac{ZX'/YX'}{ZX/YX}.
\tag{3}
\]
The analogous projections from $B$ and $C$ give the two cyclic versions of (3). Multiplying all three equalities and using (2), we obtain
\[
\frac{BU}{CU}\cdot\frac{CV}{AV}\cdot\frac{AW}{BW}
=-\frac{ZX'}{YX'}\cdot\frac{XY'}{ZY'}\cdot\frac{YZ'}{XZ'}.
\tag{4}
\]

Choose a directed coordinate on $\ell$ with origin $P$, and denote the coordinates of $X,Y,Z$ by $x,y,z$. From (1), the coordinates of $X',Y',Z'$ are
\[
x'=-\frac{k^2}{x},
\qquad
y'=-\frac{k^2}{y},
\qquad
z'=-\frac{k^2}{z}.
\tag{5}
\]
Now
\[
\begin{aligned}
\frac{ZX'}{YX'}\cdot\frac{XY'}{ZY'}\cdot\frac{YZ'}{XZ'}
&=
\frac{z-x'}{y-x'}
\cdot
\frac{x-y'}{z-y'}
\cdot
\frac{y-z'}{x-z'}\\
&=
\frac{xz+k^2}{xy+k^2}
\cdot
\frac{xy+k^2}{yz+k^2}
\cdot
\frac{yz+k^2}{xz+k^2}\\
&=1.
\end{aligned}
\tag{6}
\]
Equations (4) and (6) yield
\[
\frac{BU}{CU}\cdot\frac{CV}{AV}\cdot\frac{AW}{BW}=-1.
\]
By directed Ceva's theorem, the lines $AA',BB',CC'$ are concurrent; denote their common point by $Q$. Equation (1) gives $X',Y',Z'\ne P$, so this concurrency point cannot be $P$; hence $Q\ne P$.

The line $AA'$ is the radical axis of $\omega$ and $\omega_A$, so $Q$ has equal powers with respect to these two circles. The same is true for the pairs $(\omega,\omega_B)$ and $(\omega,\omega_C)$. Hence $Q$ has the same power with respect to all four circles
\[
\omega,\omega_A,\omega_B,\omega_C.
\]
The point $P$ lies on each of $\omega_A,\omega_B,\omega_C$. Therefore every pair among these three circles has the same radical axis, namely the line $PQ$. Thus they are coaxal.

Since all three circles already pass through $P$, a coaxal family of this form either has a second common point different from $P$, or the second intersection coalesces with $P$, in which case all three circles have the same tangent at $P$. This proves the required conclusion.

SOLUTION 2:
We give a spatial inversion proof.

Let $QR$ be the diameter of $\omega$ whose extension beyond $Q$ passes through $P$, and let $\Pi$ be the plane of the original configuration. Choose a point $S$ outside $\Pi$ such that
\[
SQ\perp\Pi
\qquad\text{and}\qquad
\angle PSR=90^\circ.
\]
Apply an inversion in space with center $S$. For every original object $T$, denote its image by $T^*$.

The plane $\Pi$ is transformed into a sphere $\Sigma$ through $S$. Every line in $\Pi$ is transformed into a circle on $\Sigma$ through $S$, while every circle in $\Pi$ is transformed into a circle on $\Sigma$.

The line $\ell$ and the circle $\omega$ are both perpendicular to the plane $SPQ$. Consequently, their images $\ell^*$ and $\omega^*$ lie in parallel planes.

Consider the three circles
\[
(A^*X^*P^*),
\qquad
(B^*Y^*P^*),
\qquad
(C^*Z^*P^*)
\]
on the sphere $\Sigma$.

The point $X^*$ is the second common point, other than $S$, of the circles $(B^*C^*S)$ and $\ell^*$. Thus the lines $SX^*$ and $B^*C^*$ are coplanar. The first lies in the plane of $\ell^*$ and the second in the parallel plane of $\omega^*$, so
\[
SX^*\parallel B^*C^*.
\tag{7}
\]
Similarly,
\[
SY^*\parallel C^*A^*,
\qquad
SZ^*\parallel A^*B^*.
\tag{8}
\]

Let $A_1$ be the second intersection, other than $A^*$, of the circles $(A^*X^*P^*)$ and $\omega^*$. The plane of $(A^*X^*P^*)$ cuts the two parallel planes of $\ell^*$ and $\omega^*$ in the lines $X^*P^*$ and $A^*A_1$, respectively. Hence
\[
A^*A_1\parallel X^*P^*.
\tag{9}
\]
The segment $SP^*$ is a diameter of the circle $\ell^*$, so
\[
SX^*\perp X^*P^*.
\tag{10}
\]
From (7), (9), and (10),
\[
A^*A_1\perp B^*C^*.
\]
Thus $A^*A_1$ is the altitude from $A^*$ in triangle $A^*B^*C^*$.

Define $B_1$ and $C_1$ analogously. Then
\[
B^*B_1
\quad\text{and}\quad
C^*C_1
\]
are the other two altitudes. Let $H$ be the orthocenter of triangle $A^*B^*C^*$.

Let $W$ be the second intersection of the line $P^*H$ with the sphere $\Sigma$, other than $P^*$. The plane of the circle $(A^*P^*X^*)$ contains the line $A^*A_1$, hence it contains $H$; it also contains $P^*$. Therefore it contains the whole line $P^*H$, and so
\[
W\in(A^*P^*X^*).
\]
The same argument gives
\[
W\in(B^*P^*Y^*)
\qquad\text{and}\qquad
W\in(C^*P^*Z^*).
\]
Thus the three image circles have the second common point $W$.

If the line $P^*H$ is tangent to the sphere $\Sigma$, then $W=P^*$ with multiplicity two, and $P^*H$ is the common tangent of the three image circles at $P^*$. Inverting back, the original three circles either have a common point different from $P$ or are mutually tangent at $P$.
