ID: 0102

SOURCE: IMO Shortlist

YEAR: 2013

TOPIC: Combinatorics

SUBTOPICS: Combinatorial Games, Invariants, Dyadic Intervals, Greedy Strategy

STATEMENT:
Players A and B play a game on the real line. Player A has a pot containing four units of black ink. A quantity $p$ of ink can blacken a closed interval of length $p$.

In every round, A chooses a positive integer $m$ and provides $2^{-m}$ units of ink. Player B then chooses an integer $k$ and blackens the interval
\[
\left[\frac{k}{2^m},\frac{k+1}{2^m}\right].
\]
Parts of this interval may already be black.

Player A wants, after finitely many rounds, to have emptied the pot while the interval $[0,1]$ is not completely black. Determine whether A has a winning strategy.

SOLUTION 1:
The answer is no. We describe a strategy for B under which the pot cannot become empty before $[0,1]$ is completely black.

At the beginning of a round, suppose $[0,1]$ is not yet completely black, and let $x$ be the largest number such that
\[
[0,x]
\]
is completely black. Thus $0\le x<1$. If A chooses $m$, put
\[
y=\lfloor 2^m x\rfloor
\]
and define the two consecutive dyadic intervals
\[
I_0=\left[\frac{y}{2^m},\frac{y+1}{2^m}\right],
\qquad
I_1=\left[\frac{y+1}{2^m},\frac{y+2}{2^m}\right].
\]
The interval $I_0$ is the leftmost interval of length $2^{-m}$ containing an unblackened point.

B uses the following rule:

\begin{itemize}
\item if $I_1$ contains an unblackened point, blacken $I_1$;
\item otherwise, blacken $I_0$.
\end{itemize}

For convenience, regard $[1,2]$ as already black before the game, without using ink. Thus if $I_1$ begins at $1$, B chooses $I_0$.

We prove the following invariants. Before every round in which $[0,1]$ is not completely black:

\begin{enumerate}
\item the total ink spent on painted intervals contained in $[0,x]$ is at most $3x$;
\item for every $q\ge1$, at most one painted interval of length $2^{-q}$ lies to the right of $x$; if such an interval exists, it is the interval immediately to the right of the level-$q$ dyadic interval containing $x$.
\end{enumerate}

They hold initially. Suppose first that B paints $I_1$. The interval $I_0$ still contains an unblackened point, so $x$ does not change and the first invariant is unaffected. The second invariant is also preserved: at level $m$, any previously painted interval still lying to the right of $x$ would already have to be this same interval $I_1$; at all other levels nothing changes.

Now suppose B paints $I_0$, and let the new black prefix be
\[
[0,x'],
\qquad
x'=x+\alpha.
\]
If $[0,1]$ is still incomplete, then $I_1\subseteq[0,x']$, so
\[
\alpha>2^{-m}.
\tag{1}
\]
Any interval painted before this round that meets $(x,x')$ is in fact contained in $[x,x']$: otherwise a black interval would cross one of the maximal endpoints $x$ or $x'$. By the second invariant, these previously painted intervals have pairwise distinct dyadic lengths. Since each is contained in an interval of length $\alpha$, the sum of their lengths is less than
\[
2\alpha.
\tag{2}
\]
Indeed, the sum of distinct powers of $1/2$ not exceeding $\alpha$ is less than twice the largest one.

The newly painted interval $I_0$ costs $2^{-m}<\alpha$ by (1). Therefore the total ink spent on intervals contained in $[0,x']$ is less than
\[
3x+2\alpha+\alpha=3x',
\]
so the first invariant is preserved. The second invariant is preserved as well: as $x$ moves to the right, a previously painted dyadic interval either becomes part of the black prefix or remains the unique interval immediately to the right of the dyadic cell containing the new $x$.

Finally, consider any stage at which $[0,1]$ is not completely black. No painted interval can straddle $x$, since then the black prefix would extend past $x$. Thus every used interval is either contained in $[0,x]$ or lies to its right. By the first invariant, the former intervals used at most $3x$ units of ink. By the second invariant, the latter intervals have distinct dyadic lengths and are contained in $[x,1]$; their total length is at most
\[
2(1-x).
\]
Hence the total amount of ink used is at most
\[
3x+2(1-x)=2+x<3.
\]
In particular, the original four-unit pot is not empty. Therefore A can never empty the pot while leaving part of $[0,1]$ unblackened, so A has no winning strategy.
