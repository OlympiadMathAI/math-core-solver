ID: 0183

SOURCE: IMO Shortlist

YEAR: 2016

TOPIC: Combinatorics

SUBTOPICS: Binary Strings, Hamming Distance, Pigeonhole Principle, Counting

STATEMENT:
The leader of an IMO team chooses positive integers $n$ and $k$ with $n>k$, and announces them to the deputy leader and a contestant. The leader then secretly tells the deputy leader an $n$-digit binary string, and the deputy leader writes down all $n$-digit binary strings that differ from the leader's string in exactly $k$ positions. The contestant may look at the strings written by the deputy leader and then guess the leader's string.

What is the minimum number of guesses, in terms of $n$ and $k$, needed to guarantee the correct answer?

SOLUTION 1:
The answer is
\[
\boxed{
\begin{cases}
2,&n=2k,\\
1,&n\ne2k.
\end{cases}}
\]

For binary strings $U,V$, let $d(U,V)$ denote their Hamming distance. Let $X$ be the leader's string, and let $\overline X$ be its bitwise complement. The family of strings at distance $k$ from $X$ is exactly the family of strings at distance $n-k$ from $\overline X$. Thus the cases $k$ and $n-k$ are equivalent, and we may assume
\[
k\ge\frac n2.
\]

If $n=2k$, then the information written by the deputy leader is identical for $X$ and $\overline X$, because
\[
d(Z,X)=k\iff d(Z,\overline X)=n-k=k.
\]
Therefore at least two guesses are necessary.

We now show that no other ambiguity is possible. Let $Y\ne X$, and put
\[
m=d(X,Y).
\]
Assume that
\[
0<m<2k.
\]
After relabelling the coordinates, suppose that $X$ and $Y$ differ in their first $m$ positions. Form a string $Z$ by changing the first $k$ digits of $X$. Then
\[
d(Z,X)=k,
\]
so $Z$ appears on the deputy leader's list. On the other hand,
\[
d(Z,Y)=|m-k|<k.
\]
Hence $Z$ would not appear if $Y$ were the leader's string. The contestant can therefore rule out $Y$.

If $k>n/2$, then every $Y\ne X$ satisfies
\[
d(X,Y)\le n<2k,
\]
so every false candidate is ruled out and one guess suffices.

If $n=2k$, every string other than $X$ and $\overline X$ has distance strictly between $0$ and $2k$ from $X$, and is therefore ruled out. Thus only $X$ and $\overline X$ remain, so two guesses suffice.

SOLUTION 2:
Suppose first that
\[
n\ne2k.
\]
Fix one coordinate. If the leader's digit in that coordinate is $1$, then among the
\[
\binom nk
\]
listed strings, exactly
\[
\binom{n-1}{k}
\]
have digit $1$ there, while exactly
\[
\binom{n-1}{k-1}
\]
have digit $0$ there. If the leader's digit is $0$, these two counts are interchanged.

Since
\[
\binom{n-1}{k}\ne\binom{n-1}{k-1}
\]
when $n\ne2k$, the contestant can determine the leader's digit in that coordinate. Repeating this for every coordinate recovers the entire string, so one guess suffices.

Now suppose
\[
n=2k.
\]
The strings $X$ and $\overline X$ give the same list, so at least two guesses are needed. We prove that the contestant can determine $X$ up to complementation.

Take any two coordinates. Suppose first that the two corresponding digits of $X$ are equal. Among the listed strings, each of the two unequal two-digit patterns occurs
\[
\binom{2k-2}{k-1}
\]
times, while each of the two equal patterns occurs
\[
\binom{2k-2}{k}
=
\binom{2k-2}{k-2}
\]
times. If the two digits of $X$ are different, these two frequencies are interchanged. For $k>1$,
\[
\binom{2k-2}{k-1}\ne\binom{2k-2}{k},
\]
so the contestant can determine whether the two hidden digits are equal.

Comparing every digit with the first one determines all digits relative to the first. Hence the only two possible strings are $X$ and $\overline X$. The exceptional case $n=2$, $k=1$ is immediate. Therefore exactly two guesses are sufficient when $n=2k$.
