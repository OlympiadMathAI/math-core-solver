ID: 0368

SOURCE: IMO Shortlist

YEAR: 2022

TOPIC: Algebra

SUBTOPICS: Functional Equations, Rational Slopes, Fractional Parts, Counterexample Construction

STATEMENT:
Let $\mathbb R$ be the set of real numbers, and let $\mathcal F$ be the set of all functions
\[
f:\mathbb R\to\mathbb R
\]
satisfying
\[
f(x+f(y))=f(x)+f(y)
\tag{1}
\]
for all real numbers $x,y$.

Find all rational numbers $q$ such that, for every $f\in\mathcal F$, there exists a real number $z$ satisfying
\[
f(z)=qz.
\]

SOLUTION 1:
The required rational numbers are
\[
\boxed{
q=\frac{m+1}{m}
\quad\text{for some }m\in\mathbb Z\setminus\{0\}
}.
\]

Let $\mathcal Z$ be the set of rational numbers having the required property, and put
\[
\mathcal S=
\left\{
\frac{m+1}{m}:m\in\mathbb Z\setminus\{0\}
\right\}.
\]
We prove both inclusions.

\textbf{Step 1: $\mathcal S\subseteq\mathcal Z$.}

Fix $f\in\mathcal F$ and write
\[
c=f(0).
\]
Putting $(x,y)=(0,0)$ in (1),
\[
f(c)=2c.
\]
Putting $(x,y)=(0,c)$ then gives
\[
f(2c)=3c.
\]
Inductively,
\[
f(mc)=(m+1)c
\qquad(m\ge1).
\tag{2}
\]
Indeed, if (2) holds for $m$, then equation (1) with $(x,y)=(0,mc)$ gives
\[
f((m+1)c)=c+f(mc)=(m+2)c.
\]

Putting $(x,y)=(-c,0)$ in (1), we obtain
\[
f(-c)=0.
\]
If, for some $k\ge1$,
\[
f(-kc)=(1-k)c,
\]
then equation (1) with $(x,y)=(-(k+1)c,0)$ gives
\[
f(-kc)=f(-(k+1)c)+c,
\]
and therefore
\[
f(-(k+1)c)=-kc.
\]
Induction yields
\[
f(mc)=(m+1)c
\qquad(m\le-1).
\tag{3}
\]

Let
\[
q=\frac{m+1}{m}\in\mathcal S.
\]
If $c=0$, then $z=0$ satisfies $f(z)=qz$. If $c\ne0$, take
\[
z=mc.
\]
Equations (2)--(3) give
\[
f(z)=(m+1)c=qmc=qz.
\]
Thus $q\in\mathcal Z$.

\textbf{Step 2: $\mathcal Z\subseteq\mathcal S$.}

Let $p\in\mathbb Q\setminus\mathcal S$. We construct a function $f\in\mathcal F$ for which
\[
f(z)\ne pz
\qquad(z\in\mathbb R).
\]

We first need a simple lemma.

\textbf{Lemma.} For every $\alpha\in[0,1)$, there is an integer $m$ such that
\[
m+n\ne p(\alpha+n)
\qquad\text{for every }n\in\mathbb Z.
\tag{4}
\]

If $p=1$, choose any integer $m\ne\alpha$, and (4) is immediate. Suppose $p\ne1$. If no such $m$ existed, then for every integer $m$ the number
\[
\frac{m-p\alpha}{p-1}
\]
would be an integer. Taking two consecutive values of $m$ would imply
\[
\frac1{p-1}\in\mathbb Z.
\]
Thus
\[
p=1+\frac1t=\frac{t+1}{t}
\]
for some nonzero integer $t$, contrary to $p\notin\mathcal S$. The lemma follows.

For each $\alpha\in[0,1)$, choose an integer $g(\alpha)$ satisfying (4), and define
\[
f(x)=g(\{x\})+\lfloor x\rfloor,
\tag{5}
\]
where $\{x\}=x-\lfloor x\rfloor$ is the fractional part of $x$.

The value $f(y)$ is always an integer. Hence
\[
\{x+f(y)\}=\{x\}
\]
and
\[
\lfloor x+f(y)\rfloor=\lfloor x\rfloor+f(y).
\]
Using (5),
\[
\begin{aligned}
f(x+f(y))
&=g(\{x\})+\lfloor x\rfloor+f(y)\\
&=f(x)+f(y).
\end{aligned}
\]
Thus $f\in\mathcal F$.

Finally, write any real number as
\[
z=\alpha+n,
\qquad
\alpha\in[0,1),\ n\in\mathbb Z.
\]
Then the defining property of $g(\alpha)$ gives
\[
f(z)=g(\alpha)+n\ne p(\alpha+n)=pz.
\]
Therefore $p\notin\mathcal Z$.

We conclude that
\[
\boxed{
\mathcal Z=
\left\{
\frac{m+1}{m}:m\in\mathbb Z\setminus\{0\}
\right\}
}.
\]
