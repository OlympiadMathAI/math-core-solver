ID: 0329

SOURCE: IMO Shortlist

YEAR: 2020

TOPIC: Number Theory

SUBTOPICS: Euler Totient Function, Divisor Function, Prime Distribution, Prime Number Theorem

STATEMENT:
For a positive integer $n$, let $d(n)$ be the number of positive divisors of $n$, and let
\[
\varphi(n)
\]
be Euler's totient function.

Does there exist a constant $C$ such that
\[
\frac{\varphi(d(n))}{d(\varphi(n))}\le C
\]
for every positive integer $n$?

SOLUTION 1:
The answer is
\[
\boxed{\text{No}}.
\]

Choose an integer $N>1$ for which the interval
\[
(N,2N]
\]
contains at least one prime. Let
\[
p_1,p_2,\ldots,p_k
\]
be all primes not exceeding $N$, and let
\[
p_{k+1},\ldots,p_{k+s}
\]
be all primes in $(N,2N]$.

Every prime divisor of every number
\[
p_j-1
\qquad(1\le j\le k+s)
\]
does not exceed $N$. Indeed, if a prime $r>N$ divided $p_j-1<2N$, then necessarily
\[
p_j-1=r,
\]
which is impossible because $p_j-1$ is even and $r>2$ is odd. Hence there are nonnegative integers
\[
c_1,\ldots,c_k
\]
such that
\[
\prod_{j=1}^{k+s}(p_j-1)
=
\prod_{i=1}^{k}p_i^{c_i}.
\tag{1}
\]

Choose a very large prime $q>2N$, and define
\[
n=
(p_1p_2\cdots p_k)^{q-1}
(p_{k+1}\cdots p_{k+s}).
\tag{2}
\]
The first $k$ primes occur in $n$ with exponent $q-1$, while the remaining $s$ primes occur with exponent $1$. Therefore
\[
d(n)=q^k2^s,
\]
and
\[
\varphi(d(n))
=
q^{k-1}(q-1)2^{s-1}.
\tag{3}
\]

On the other hand,
\[
\begin{aligned}
\varphi(n)
&=
\left(\prod_{i=1}^k p_i^{q-2}\right)
\left(\prod_{j=1}^{k+s}(p_j-1)\right)\\
&=
\prod_{i=1}^k p_i^{q-2+c_i}
\end{aligned}
\]
by (1). Consequently,
\[
d(\varphi(n))
=
\prod_{i=1}^k(q-1+c_i).
\tag{4}
\]

Combining (3)--(4),
\[
\frac{\varphi(d(n))}{d(\varphi(n))}
=
2^{s-1}\cdot
\frac{q-1}{q}
\prod_{i=1}^k
\frac{q}{q-1+c_i}.
\tag{5}
\]
For fixed $N$, the factors after $2^{s-1}$ tend to $1$ as
\[
q\to\infty.
\]
Thus the ratio in (5) can be made arbitrarily close to
\[
2^{s-1}.
\tag{6}
\]

It remains to show that $s$, the number of primes in $(N,2N]$, is unbounded. This follows from the classical divergence
\[
\sum_{p\ \mathrm{prime}}\frac1p=\infty.
\]
Indeed, if every dyadic interval
\[
[2^j,2^{j+1})
\]
contained at most $M$ primes, then
\[
\sum_{p\ \mathrm{prime}}\frac1p
\le
\sum_{j=0}^{\infty}\frac{M}{2^j}
<\infty,
\]
a contradiction.

Hence $s$ can be arbitrarily large. Equations (5)--(6) show that the ratio
\[
\frac{\varphi(d(n))}{d(\varphi(n))}
\]
is unbounded, so no constant $C$ exists.

SOLUTION 2:
This solution uses the Prime Number Theorem.

Let $m>5$ be large, and put
\[
n=\prod_{p\le m}p,
\]
the product of all primes not exceeding $m$. Let
\[
\pi(m)
\]
denote the number of primes not exceeding $m$.

Since $n$ is squarefree,
\[
d(n)=2^{\pi(m)},
\]
and therefore
\[
\varphi(d(n))=2^{\pi(m)-1}.
\tag{1}
\]

We have
\[
\varphi(n)=\prod_{p\le m}(p-1).
\]
Every prime divisor of this product is at most $m/2$, so write
\[
\varphi(n)=\prod_{q\le m/2}q^{\alpha_q}.
\tag{2}
\]
Then
\[
d(\varphi(n))
=
\prod_{q\le m/2}(\alpha_q+1).
\tag{3}
\]

Split the primes in (3) at $\sqrt m$.

For
\[
q>\sqrt m,
\]
each factor $p-1$ contains at most one prime factor greater than $\sqrt m$, counted with multiplicity. Hence
\[
\sum_{q>\sqrt m}\alpha_q\le\pi(m).
\]
If $\ell$ is the number of primes $q$ in
\[
(\sqrt m,m/2],
\]
then
\[
\sum_{q>\sqrt m}(\alpha_q+1)
\le
\pi(m)+\pi(m/2).
\]
By the arithmetic--geometric mean inequality and the estimate
\[
\left(\frac Ax\right)^x\le e^{A/e},
\]
we obtain
\[
\prod_{q>\sqrt m}(\alpha_q+1)
\le
\exp\left(\frac{\pi(m)+\pi(m/2)}e\right).
\tag{4}
\]

For
\[
q\le\sqrt m,
\]
we use the crude estimate
\[
\alpha_q+1\le m^2
\]
for all sufficiently large $m$. Since there are at most $\sqrt m$ such primes,
\[
\prod_{q\le\sqrt m}(\alpha_q+1)
\le
m^{2\sqrt m}.
\tag{5}
\]

Equations (3)--(5) yield
\[
\log d(\varphi(n))
\le
2\sqrt m\log m
+
\frac{\pi(m)+\pi(m/2)}e.
\tag{6}
\]

The Prime Number Theorem states that
\[
\pi(x)=\frac{x}{\log x}(1+o(1)).
\]
Therefore,
\[
\limsup_{m\to\infty}
\frac{\log d(\varphi(n))}{m/\log m}
\le
\frac{3}{2e}.
\tag{7}
\]
On the other hand, by (1),
\[
\lim_{m\to\infty}
\frac{\log\varphi(d(n))}{m/\log m}
=
\log2.
\tag{8}
\]
Since
\[
\frac{3}{2e}<\log2,
\]
equations (7)--(8) show that
\[
\log\frac{\varphi(d(n))}{d(\varphi(n))}
\to+\infty
\]
along this sequence of values of $n$. Hence the ratio is unbounded.
