ID: 0201

SOURCE: IMO Shortlist

YEAR: 2016

TOPIC: Number Theory

SUBTOPICS: Polynomial GCDs, Chinese Remainder Theorem, Consecutive Values, Modular Arithmetic

STATEMENT:
Define
\[
P(n)=n^2+n+1.
\]
For positive integers $a$ and $b$, call the set
\[
\{P(a),P(a+1),P(a+2),\ldots,P(a+b)\}
\]
\emph{fragrant} if none of its elements is relatively prime to the product of all the other elements. Determine the smallest possible size of a fragrant set.

SOLUTION 1:
The answer is
\[
\boxed{6}.
\]

We begin with three gcd observations.

\textbf{Observation 1.}
For every integer $n$,
\[
\gcd(P(n),P(n+1))=1.
\tag{1}
\]
Indeed,
\[
P(n+1)-P(n)=2n+2.
\]
The number $P(n)$ is odd, and
\[
\gcd(P(n),n+1)=\gcd(1,n+1)=1,
\]
which proves (1).

\textbf{Observation 2.}
\[
\gcd(P(n),P(n+2))
=
\begin{cases}
7,&n\equiv2\pmod7,\\
1,&n\not\equiv2\pmod7.
\end{cases}
\tag{2}
\]
The identity
\[
(2n+7)P(n)-(2n-1)P(n+2)=14
\]
shows that the gcd divides $14$. Since both polynomial values are odd, the gcd divides $7$. Checking the seven residue classes modulo $7$ gives (2).

\textbf{Observation 3.}
\[
\gcd(P(n),P(n+3))>1
\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad
n\equiv1\pmod3.
\tag{3}
\]
Indeed,
\[
(n+5)P(n)-(n-1)P(n+3)=18.
\]
Thus the gcd is an odd divisor of $18$, and a direct check modulo $3$ gives (3).

We now show that no fragrant set has at most five elements.

A set of one or two consecutive values is plainly impossible by (1). In a set of three consecutive values, the middle term is coprime to both others by (1), so such a set is not fragrant.

For four consecutive values
\[
P(a),P(a+1),P(a+2),P(a+3),
\]
the term $P(a+1)$ can share a factor only with $P(a+3)$, which by (2) would require
\[
a+1\equiv2\pmod7.
\]
Similarly, $P(a+2)$ can share a factor only with $P(a)$, requiring
\[
a\equiv2\pmod7.
\]
These conditions are incompatible.

Finally, suppose that
\[
P(a),P(a+1),\ldots,P(a+4)
\]
were fragrant. The middle term $P(a+2)$ is coprime to its two neighbours by (1), so it must share a factor with $P(a)$ or $P(a+4)$.

If
\[
\gcd(P(a),P(a+2))>1,
\]
then $a\equiv2\pmod7$ by (2). Hence
\[
\gcd(P(a+1),P(a+3))=1.
\]
The term $P(a+1)$ must therefore share a factor with $P(a+4)$, while $P(a+3)$ must share a factor with $P(a)$. By (3), this requires simultaneously
\[
a+1\equiv1\pmod3
\qquad\text{and}\qquad
a\equiv1\pmod3,
\]
a contradiction.

If instead
\[
\gcd(P(a+2),P(a+4))>1,
\]
then $a+2\equiv2\pmod7$, and again
\[
\gcd(P(a+1),P(a+3))=1.
\]
The same fragrance requirements force the same two incompatible congruences modulo $3$.

Thus every fragrant set has at least six elements.

It remains to construct one of size six. By the Chinese Remainder Theorem, choose $a$ satisfying
\[
a\equiv7\pmod{19},
\qquad
a+1\equiv2\pmod7,
\qquad
a+2\equiv1\pmod3.
\]
For example,
\[
a=197
\]
works.

By (2), both
\[
P(a+1)\quad\text{and}\quad P(a+3)
\]
are divisible by $7$. By (3), both
\[
P(a+2)\quad\text{and}\quad P(a+5)
\]
are divisible by $3$. Finally,
\[
19\mid P(7)=57
\qquad\text{and}\qquad
19\mid P(11)=133,
\]
while
\[
a\equiv7\pmod{19},
\qquad
a+4\equiv11\pmod{19}.
\]
Hence
\[
19\mid P(a),P(a+4).
\]

Every one of the six values shares a nontrivial common factor with another one, so
\[
\{P(a),P(a+1),\ldots,P(a+5)\}
\]
is fragrant. The minimum size is therefore $6$.
