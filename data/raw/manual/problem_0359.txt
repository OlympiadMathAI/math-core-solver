ID: 0359

SOURCE: IMO Shortlist

YEAR: 2021

TOPIC: Number Theory

SUBTOPICS: Factorials, Prime Valuations, LTE, AM-GM Inequality, Parity

STATEMENT:
Prove that there are only finitely many quadruples
\[
(a,b,c,n)
\]
of positive integers satisfying
\[
n!=a^{n-1}+b^{n-1}+c^{n-1}.
\]

SOLUTION 1:
For each fixed $n$, there are only finitely many possibilities for $a,b,c$. It is therefore enough to prove that no solution exists for all sufficiently large $n$. We show that there is no solution when
\[
n>100.
\]

Assume, for contradiction, that $n>100$ and
\[
n!=a^{n-1}+b^{n-1}+c^{n-1}.
\tag{1}
\]

First we obtain an upper bound for $a,b,c$. By AM-GM,
\[
\begin{aligned}
n!
&=
2n(n-1)(n-2)(n-3)\cdot(3\cdot4\cdots(n-4))\\
&\le
2(n-1)^4
\left(\frac{3+4+\cdots+(n-4)}{n-6}\right)^{n-6}\\
&=
2(n-1)^4
\left(\frac{n-1}{2}\right)^{n-6}\\
&<
\left(\frac{n-1}{2}\right)^{n-1}.
\end{aligned}
\tag{2}
\]
The final inequality follows from $n-1>64$. Equation (1) and (2) give
\[
a,b,c<\frac{n-1}{2}.
\tag{3}
\]

For a prime $p$, write $v_p(x)$ for the exponent of $p$ in a nonzero integer $x$. Legendre's formula implies
\[
v_p(n!)
=
\sum_{j\ge1}
\left\lfloor\frac{n}{p^j}\right\rfloor
<
\sum_{j\ge1}\frac{n}{p^j}
=
\frac{n}{p-1}.
\tag{4}
\]

Suppose first that $n$ is odd. Then $n-1$ is even, so the three terms on the right side of (1) are squares. Since $4\mid n!$, reducing (1) modulo $4$ shows that $a,b,c$ are all even. Hence
\[
2^{n-1}\mid n!.
\]
But $n$ is odd, and therefore
\[
v_2(n!)=v_2((n-1)!)<n-1
\]
by (4), a contradiction.

Thus $n$ is even.

Assume first that all three numbers
\[
a+b,\qquad b+c,\qquad c+a
\]
are powers of $2$. Since each is at least $2$, they are even, and hence $a,b,c$ have the same parity.

The integers $a,b,c$ cannot all be odd, because the right side of (1) would then be odd. If
\[
4\mid a,\qquad4\mid b,\qquad4\mid c,
\]
then
\[
2^{2(n-1)}\mid n!,
\]
contradicting (4). Thus one of them, say $a$, is even but not divisible by $4$.

Now
\[
2a=(a+b)+(a+c)-(b+c)
\]
is not divisible by $8$. Since all three pairwise sums are powers of $2$ and are at least $4$, one of them must equal $4$. The two numbers in that sum are positive even integers, so they are both $2$. Relabelling if necessary, let
\[
a=b=2.
\]
Then
\[
a+c=b+c=2^r
\]
for some $r$, and hence
\[
c=2^r-2.
\]
By (3), $c<n$, so $c\mid n!$. Reducing (1) modulo $c$ gives
\[
c\mid2^{n}.
\]
Thus $2^r-2$ is a power of $2$, which forces
\[
r=2,\qquad c=2.
\]
Equation (1) would then become
\[
n!=3\cdot2^{n-1},
\]
which is impossible modulo $5$ because $n>100$.

We may therefore assume that at least one pairwise sum, say
\[
a+b,
\]
is not a power of $2$. It has an odd prime divisor $p$. By (3),
\[
p\le a+b<n-1<n.
\tag{5}
\]
Since $n!$ and $a^{n-1}+b^{n-1}$ are divisible by $p$, equation (1) gives
\[
p\mid c.
\tag{6}
\]

If $p$ divided both $a$ and $b$, then (6) and (1) would give
\[
p^{n-1}\mid n!,
\]
contradicting (4). Hence
\[
p\nmid ab.
\]
Because $n-1$ is odd, the lifting-the-exponent lemma gives
\[
v_p(a^{n-1}+b^{n-1})
=
v_p(a+b)+v_p(n-1).
\tag{7}
\]

By (6),
\[
v_p(c^{n-1})\ge n-1.
\]
On the other hand, since $p$ is odd, (4) gives
\[
v_p(n!)<\frac n2<n-1.
\]
Therefore
\[
v_p(n!-c^{n-1})=v_p(n!).
\]
Using (1) and (7),
\[
v_p(n!)
=
v_p(a+b)+v_p(n-1).
\tag{8}
\]

The left side of (8) is
\[
v_p(1)+v_p(2)+\cdots+v_p(n).
\]
The two terms on the right are the contributions of the distinct integers
\[
a+b
\qquad\text{and}\qquad
n-1,
\]
where $a+b<n-1$ by (3). Since every valuation is nonnegative, equality (8) implies that no other integer in
\[
\{1,2,\ldots,n\}
\]
is divisible by $p$.

But (3) and (6) give
\[
p\le c<\frac n2.
\]
Thus both $p$ and $2p$ lie in $\{1,\ldots,n\}$. They must therefore be exactly the two multiples listed above:
\[
a+b=p,\qquad n-1=2p.
\]
This is impossible because $n-1$ is odd while $2p$ is even.

We have reached a contradiction in every case. Hence no solution exists for $n>100$. Since only finitely many triples $(a,b,c)$ are possible for each of the finitely many remaining values of $n$, there are only finitely many quadruples in total.
