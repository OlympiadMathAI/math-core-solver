ID: 0297

SOURCE: IMO Shortlist

YEAR: 2019

TOPIC: Number Theory

SUBTOPICS: Pairwise Coprime Powers, Chinese Remainder Theorem, Euler Totient Function, Prime Number Theorem

STATEMENT:
Prove that there exists a constant $c>0$ and infinitely many positive integers $n$ with the following property:

There are infinitely many positive integers that cannot be expressed as the sum of fewer than
\[
cn\log n
\]
pairwise coprime $n$th powers.

SOLUTION 1:
We first give a general residue-class argument.

Suppose that positive integers $n$ and $N$ have the following property:
\[
\varphi(p^e)\mid n
\qquad
\text{for every prime power }p^e\Vert N.
\tag{1}
\]
Let $k$ be the number of distinct prime factors of $N$.

Modulo any prime power $p^e\Vert N$, every $n$th power is congruent to either $0$ or $1$. Indeed, condition (1) implies $n\ge e$, so the residue is $0$ if the base is divisible by $p$; otherwise Euler's theorem and (1) give the residue $1$.

Now consider a sum of $r$ pairwise coprime $n$th powers. At most one of their bases is divisible by $p$, so modulo $p^e$ the sum is congruent to either
\[
r
\qquad\text{or}\qquad
r-1.
\]
Therefore, for a fixed $r$, there are at most $2^k$ possible residue classes modulo $N$ for such sums. Consequently, sums of at most $m$ pairwise coprime $n$th powers occupy at most
\[
m2^k
\]
residue classes modulo $N$.

Thus, if
\[
N>m2^k,
\tag{2}
\]
some residue class modulo $N$ is not represented. Every positive integer in that class is then not expressible as a sum of at most $m$ pairwise coprime $n$th powers.

We now construct suitable $n$ and $N$. Fix a positive integer $t$. Choose primes
\[
p\mid 2^{2^{t-1}}+1
\qquad\text{and}\qquad
q\mid 2^{2^t}+1.
\]
The multiplicative orders of $2$ modulo $p$ and $q$ are respectively
\[
2^t
\qquad\text{and}\qquad
2^{t+1}.
\]
Hence
\[
2^t\mid p-1
\qquad\text{and}\qquad
2^{t+1}\mid q-1.
\]
Set
\[
N=pq,
\qquad
n=\frac{(p-1)(q-1)}{2^t}.
\tag{3}
\]
Then both $p-1$ and $q-1$ divide $n$, so condition (1) holds. Here
\[
k=2.
\]

We estimate the sizes. Since
\[
p-1\le2^{2^{t-1}}
\qquad\text{and}\qquad
q-1\le2^{2^t},
\]
we have
\[
\log_2 n
\le
2^{t-1}+2^t-t
<
2^{t+1}.
\tag{4}
\]
On the other hand,
\[
\frac Nn
=
\frac{2^tpq}{(p-1)(q-1)}
>
2^t.
\]
Combining this with (4),
\[
\log_2 n<\frac{2N}{n},
\]
and therefore
\[
N>\frac n2\log_2 n
=
\frac{n\log n}{2\log2}.
\tag{5}
\]

Choose any constant
\[
0<c<\frac1{8\log2}.
\]
If
\[
m<cn\log n,
\]
then, by (5),
\[
m2^k=4m<N.
\]
Condition (2) applies, so infinitely many positive integers are not sums of at most $m$ pairwise coprime $n$th powers.

The values of $n$ in (3) are unbounded as $t$ grows, because
\[
p\ge2^t+1,
\qquad
q\ge2^{t+1}+1.
\]
Thus there are infinitely many such $n$, completing the proof.

SOLUTION 2:
We use the same residue-class principle as in Solution 1: if
\[
\varphi(p^e)\mid n
\qquad(p^e\Vert N)
\tag{1}
\]
and $k$ is the number of distinct prime factors of $N$, then sums of at most $m$ pairwise coprime $n$th powers occupy at most
\[
m2^k
\]
residue classes modulo $N$. Hence it is enough to construct infinitely many pairs $(n,N)$ satisfying (1) with
\[
N>m2^k
\]
for some $m$ much larger than a constant multiple of $n\log n$.

Fix an integer $x\ge4$, and define
\[
N=\operatorname{lcm}(1,2,\ldots,2x),
\qquad
n=2\operatorname{lcm}(1,2,\ldots,x).
\tag{2}
\]
If $p^e\Vert N$, then
\[
\frac{\varphi(p^e)}2\le x.
\]
Except for the trivial case $p^e=2$, the number $\varphi(p^e)/2$ is a positive integer not exceeding $x$, and hence divides $\operatorname{lcm}(1,\ldots,x)$. The case $p^e=2$ is immediate because $\varphi(2)=1$. Therefore
\[
\varphi(p^e)\mid n,
\]
so (1) holds.

Let
\[
k=\pi(2x),
\]
the number of primes not exceeding $2x$. The quotient
\[
\frac{2N}{n}
\]
is the product of all primes that have some power in the interval $(x,2x]$. In particular, it contains every prime in $(x,2x]$, and hence
\[
\frac{2N}{n}
>
x^{\,\pi(2x)-\pi(x)}.
\tag{3}
\]

By the Prime Number Theorem,
\[
\pi(t)\sim\frac{t}{\log t}.
\]
Using (3),
\[
\begin{aligned}
\log\left(\frac{2N}{2^k n}\right)
&>
\bigl(\pi(2x)-\pi(x)\bigr)\log x-\pi(2x)\log2\\
&\sim x.
\end{aligned}
\tag{4}
\]
Also,
\[
\log n
\le
\log2+\pi(x)\log x
\sim x.
\tag{5}
\]
Equations (4)--(5) imply that, for every fixed $\varepsilon>0$ and all sufficiently large $x$,
\[
\log\left(\frac{N}{2^k n}\right)
>
(1-\varepsilon)\log n.
\]
Thus
\[
N>2^k n^{2-\varepsilon}.
\tag{6}
\]

Taking, for example, $\varepsilon=1/2$, equation (6) gives
\[
N>2^k n^{3/2}.
\]
For every fixed positive constant $c$ and all sufficiently large $n$,
\[
n^{3/2}>cn\log n.
\]
Hence
\[
N>2^k cn\log n.
\]
The residue-class argument now shows that infinitely many positive integers cannot be written as sums of fewer than
\[
cn\log n
\]
pairwise coprime $n$th powers.

Since the values of $n$ in (2) are unbounded, this proves the required assertion for infinitely many $n$.
