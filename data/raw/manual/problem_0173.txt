ID: 0173

SOURCE: IMO Shortlist

YEAR: 2015

TOPIC: Number Theory

SUBTOPICS: GCD Bounds, Chinese Remainder Theorem, Recursive Constructions, Parity

STATEMENT:
For a positive integer $k$, call a function
\[
f:\mathbb Z_{>0}\to\mathbb Z_{>0}
\]
\emph{$k$-good} if
\[
\gcd(f(m)+n,\ f(n)+m)\le k
\]
for all distinct positive integers $m,n$.

Determine all positive integers $k$ for which a $k$-good function exists.

SOLUTION 1:
The answer is
\[
\boxed{k\ge2}.
\]

A $k$-good function is automatically $(k+1)$-good, so it is enough to prove that no $1$-good function exists and that a $2$-good function does exist.

Suppose first that $f$ were $1$-good. There cannot be two distinct even integers $m,n$ for which both $f(m)$ and $f(n)$ are even, because then both
\[
f(m)+n
\qquad\text{and}\qquad
f(n)+m
\]
would be even. Similarly, there cannot be two distinct odd integers $m,n$ for which both $f(m)$ and $f(n)$ are odd.

Hence we may choose an even $m$ with $f(m)$ odd and an odd $n$ with $f(n)$ even. Then both $f(m)+n$ and $f(n)+m$ are even, again a contradiction. Therefore no $1$-good function exists.

We now construct a $2$-good function. Define
\[
g(1)=1,
\qquad
g(n+1)=\left(2^{g(n)+1}\right)!
\]
and
\[
f(n)=2^{g(n)+1}-n-1.
\]
Clearly $f(n)$ is a positive integer.

Take $m>n$ and set
\[
A=f(m)+n
=2^{g(m)+1}-m+n-1,
\]
\[
B=f(n)+m
=2^{g(n)+1}-n+m-1.
\]
We prove that
\[
\gcd(A,B)\le2.
\]

First,
\[
A+B=2^{g(m)+1}+2^{g(n)+1}-2
\]
is divisible by $2$ but not by $4$. Hence $\gcd(A,B)$ is not divisible by $4$.

Suppose that an odd prime $p$ divides both $A$ and $B$. We claim that
\[
B\le2^{g(m-1)+1}.
\tag{1}
\]
Indeed, $g(k+1)>g(k)$, so
\[
2^{g(k+1)+1}\ge2^{g(k)+1}+1.
\]
Repeatedly applying this from $k=n$ to $k=m-2$ gives (1).

Since $p\mid B$,
\[
p-1<B\le2^{g(m-1)+1}.
\]
Therefore
\[
p-1\mid\left(2^{g(m-1)+1}\right)!=g(m).
\]
By Fermat's little theorem,
\[
2^{g(m)}\equiv1\pmod p.
\]
Consequently,
\[
A+B
\equiv 2^{g(n)+1}\pmod p.
\]
But $p\mid A+B$, so the odd prime $p$ would divide a power of $2$, impossible.

Thus $\gcd(A,B)$ has no odd prime factor and is not divisible by $4$. Hence
\[
\gcd(A,B)\le2,
\]
and $f$ is $2$-good.

SOLUTION 2:
We give a recursive construction of another $2$-good function.

Let
\[
\mathcal P=\{4\}\cup\{\text{odd primes}\}.
\]
For $p\in\mathcal P$, call a residue $a_p\pmod p$ \emph{$p$-useful} if
\[
a_p\not\equiv-a_p\pmod p.
\]
For an odd prime this means $a_p\not\equiv0\pmod p$, while for $p=4$ it means $a_p\not\equiv0,2\pmod4$.

We construct $f(1),f(2),\ldots$ recursively, together with useful residues $a_p$. After step $m$, we require:

\begin{enumerate}
\item[(i)] $f(n)$ is defined for every $n\le m$, and $a_p$ is defined for every $p\in\mathcal P$ with $p\le m+2$;
\item[(ii)] for $n\le m$ and $p\le m+2$,
\[
f(n)+n\not\equiv a_p\pmod p;
\]
\item[(iii)] for $n_1<n_2\le m$,
\[
\gcd(f(n_1)+n_2,\ f(n_2)+n_1)\le2.
\]
\end{enumerate}

Start with
\[
f(1)=1,\qquad a_3=1.
\]

Suppose the construction has been completed through step $m-1$, where $m\ge2$. Let
\[
X_m=\left\{p\in\mathcal P:
p\mid f(n)+m\text{ for some }n<m\right\}.
\]
This is finite. We impose the following congruences on $f(m)$:

\[
f(m)\equiv-a_p-m\pmod p
\]
for every already defined $p\in\mathcal P$ with $p\le m+1$, and
\[
f(m)\equiv0\pmod p
\]
for every $p\in X_m$ with $p\ge m+2$.

The moduli involved are pairwise coprime, so the Chinese Remainder Theorem supplies a positive integer $f(m)$ satisfying all these congruences.

If $p=m+2$ belongs to $\mathcal P$, choose $a_p$ to avoid the residues
\[
0,\quad 2,\quad f(1)+1,\quad f(2)+2,\quad\ldots,\quad f(m)+m
\pmod p.
\]
Since $f(1)+1=2$, at most $m+1<p=m+2$ residues are excluded, so such an $a_p$ exists. It is automatically $p$-useful.

Condition (ii) is preserved: for an old modulus $p\le m+1$,
\[
f(m)+m\equiv-a_p\not\equiv a_p\pmod p,
\]
and for the new modulus $p=m+2$ it holds by the choice of $a_p$.

It remains to verify condition (iii). Suppose that for some $n<m$ the number
\[
D=\gcd(f(n)+m,\ f(m)+n)
\]
were greater than $2$. Then either $4\mid D$ or an odd prime divides $D$. In either case, some $p\in\mathcal P$ divides $D$. Since $p\mid f(n)+m$, we have $p\in X_m$.

If $p\ge m+2$, then $f(m)\equiv0\pmod p$, and
\[
0\equiv f(m)+n\equiv n\pmod p,
\]
impossible because $0<n<m<p$.

If $p\le m+1$, then
\[
f(m)+m\equiv-a_p\pmod p.
\]
Since $p$ divides both $f(n)+m$ and $f(m)+n$,
\[
0\equiv(f(n)+m)+(f(m)+n)
=(f(n)+n)+(f(m)+m)
\equiv f(n)+n-a_p\pmod p.
\]
This contradicts condition (ii).

Thus no element of $\mathcal P$ divides $D$, so $D$ has no odd prime divisor and is not divisible by $4$. Therefore $D\le2$.

The recursion continues indefinitely and produces a $2$-good function. Hence the admissible values are exactly
\[
\boxed{k\ge2}.
\]
