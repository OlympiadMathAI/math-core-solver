ID: 0143

SOURCE: IMO Shortlist

YEAR: 2014

TOPIC: Number Theory

SUBTOPICS: Chinese Remainder Theorem, Polynomial Sums Modulo Primes, Binomial Coefficients, Gap Lengths

STATEMENT:
Let
\[
a_1<a_2<\cdots<a_n
\]
be pairwise coprime positive integers, where $a_1$ is prime and
\[
a_1\ge n+2.
\]
On the segment
\[
I=[0,a_1a_2\cdots a_n]
\]
mark every integer divisible by at least one of $a_1,\ldots,a_n$. These marked points divide $I$ into smaller segments. Prove that the sum of the squares of the lengths of these segments is divisible by $a_1$.

SOLUTION 1:
Put
\[
A=a_1a_2\cdots a_n.
\]
Let $\mathcal S$ be the family of segments between consecutive marked points. Let $\mathcal T$ be the family of all intervals $[x,y]$ with integer endpoints such that
\[
0\le x\le A-1
\]
and the open interval $(x,y)$ contains no marked point. Every interval in $\mathcal T$ lies inside a unique member of $\mathcal S$.

Assign a weight depending only on length:
\[
w(1)=1,
\qquad
w(d)=2\quad(d\ge2).
\]
If $X\in\mathcal S$ has length $L$, then it contains $L-d+1$ integer-endpoint subintervals of length $d$. Therefore the total weight of its members of $\mathcal T$ is
\[
L+2\sum_{d=2}^{L}(L-d+1)=L^2.
\]
Consequently
\[
\sum_{X\in\mathcal S}|X|^2
=
\sum_{Y\in\mathcal T}w(|Y|).
\tag{1}
\]

Every multiple of $a_1$ is marked, so no interval in $\mathcal T$ has length greater than $a_1$. Fix $1\le d\le a_1$. We count the intervals $[x,x+d]\in\mathcal T$.

For each $i$, let $r_i$ be the residue of $x$ modulo $a_i$. The open interval $(x,x+d)$ contains no multiple of $a_i$ exactly when
\[
r_i\in\{0,1,\ldots,a_i-d\}.
\]
Thus there are $a_i-d+1$ choices for $r_i$. By the Chinese remainder theorem, the residue tuple $(r_1,\ldots,r_n)$ determines a unique $x$ in $\{0,1,\ldots,A-1\}$. Hence the number of intervals in $\mathcal T$ of length $d$ is
\[
f(d)=\prod_{i=1}^n(a_i+1-d).
\tag{2}
\]
Using (1) and the weights,
\[
\sum_{X\in\mathcal S}|X|^2
=2\sum_{d=1}^{a_1}f(d)-f(1).
\tag{3}
\]

We use the following standard lemma.

\textbf{Lemma.} If $p$ is prime and $F\in\mathbb Z[x]$ has degree at most $p-2$, then
\[
\sum_{x=1}^{p}F(x)\equiv0\pmod p.
\]

It is enough to prove this for $F(x)=x^j$, $0\le j\le p-2$. The case $j=0$ is immediate. For $j\ge1$, assume the result for smaller exponents and sum
\[
x^{j+1}-(x-1)^{j+1}
\]
from $x=1$ to $p$. The sum telescopes to $p^{j+1}\equiv0\pmod p$. After the binomial expansion, all lower-power sums vanish by induction, leaving
\[
(j+1)\sum_{x=1}^{p}x^j\equiv0\pmod p.
\]
Since $j+1<p$, the coefficient is invertible modulo $p$, proving the lemma.

Now $f$ in (2) is an integer polynomial of degree $n$, and
\[
n\le a_1-2.
\]
The lemma with $p=a_1$ gives
\[
\sum_{d=1}^{a_1}f(d)\equiv0\pmod{a_1}.
\]
Also
\[
f(1)=a_1a_2\cdots a_n
\]
is divisible by $a_1$. Formula (3) therefore proves that
\[
\sum_{X\in\mathcal S}|X|^2\equiv0\pmod{a_1}.
\]

SOLUTION 2:
We prove a more general statement. Let
\[
p=a_1<a_2<\cdots<a_n
\]
be pairwise coprime, with $p$ prime. If the gap lengths are $b_1,\ldots,b_s$, then for every integer
\[
1\le d\le p-n
\]
one has
\[
\sum_{j=1}^{s}\binom{b_j}{d}\equiv0\pmod p.
\tag{1}
\]
The problem follows by taking $d=1,2$ and using
\[
x^2=2\binom{x}{2}+\binom{x}{1}.
\tag{2}
\]

We prove (1) by induction on $n$. For $n=1$, the only gap has length $p$, and
\[
\binom pd\equiv0\pmod p
\qquad(1\le d\le p-1).
\]

Assume $n\ge2$ and that the statement is true for $n-1$. Set
\[
A'=a_1a_2\cdots a_{n-1},
\qquad
A=A'a_n.
\]
Mark multiples of $a_1,\ldots,a_{n-1}$ green and multiples of $a_n$ red. In $[0,A']$, the green points form intervals
\[
J_1,\ldots,J_\ell.
\]
For a fixed $J_i$, consider its translates
\[
J_i,
J_i+A',
\ldots,
J_i+(a_n-1)A'.
\]
Because $A'$ is coprime to $a_n$, their left endpoints form a complete residue system modulo $a_n$. Also $|J_i|\le p<a_n$, so each translate contains at most one red point in its interior.

Write $L=|J_i|$. For each $j=1,\ldots,L-1$, exactly one translate is split by a red point into gaps of lengths $j$ and $L-j$. The other $a_n-L+1$ translates contain no interior red point and remain a single gap of length $L$. Hence the total contribution of these translates to the sum in (1) is
\[
2\sum_{j=1}^{L-1}\binom jd
+(a_n-L+1)\binom Ld.
\]
Using the hockey-stick identity and
\[
(L-d)\binom Ld=(d+1)\binom L{d+1},
\]
this becomes
\[
(1-d)\binom L{d+1}
+(a_n-d+1)\binom Ld.
\tag{3}
\]

Summing (3) over $i=1,\ldots,\ell$, the desired total is
\[
(1-d)\sum_{i=1}^{\ell}\binom{|J_i|}{d+1}
+(a_n-d+1)\sum_{i=1}^{\ell}\binom{|J_i|}{d}.
\tag{4}
\]
Since
\[
1\le d<d+1\le p-n+1=p-(n-1),
\]
the induction hypothesis says that both sums in (4) are divisible by $p$. Thus (4) is divisible by $p$, proving (1).

Finally, the hypothesis $p=a_1\ge n+2$ allows both $d=1$ and $d=2$. Equation (2) then proves the required divisibility of the sum of the squared gap lengths.
