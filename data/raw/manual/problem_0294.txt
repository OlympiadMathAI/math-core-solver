ID: 0294

SOURCE: IMO Shortlist

YEAR: 2019

TOPIC: Number Theory

SUBTOPICS: Divisibility, Functional Equations on Positive Integers, Prime Arguments, Asymptotic Bounds

STATEMENT:
Let $\mathbb Z_{>0}$ denote the set of positive integers, and let $C$ be a fixed positive integer.

Determine all functions
\[
f:\mathbb Z_{>0}\to\mathbb Z_{>0}
\]
such that
\[
a+f(b)\mid a^2+b f(a)
\tag{1}
\]
for all positive integers $a,b$ satisfying
\[
a+b>C.
\]

SOLUTION 1:
The solutions are exactly
\[
\boxed{f(n)=kn}
\]
for arbitrary positive integers $k$.

It is immediate that every function of this form satisfies (1), because
\[
a+f(b)=a+kb
\]
and
\[
a^2+b f(a)=a^2+kab=a(a+kb).
\]

We prove the converse. First, setting $a=1$ in (1) gives, for all sufficiently large $b$,
\[
1+f(b)\mid1+b f(1),
\]
and hence
\[
f(b)\le b f(1).
\tag{2}
\]

We claim that
\[
b\mid f(b)^2
\qquad(b\ge1).
\tag{3}
\]
Fix $b$ and choose a positive integer $n$ so large that
\[
nb-f(b)>0
\qquad\text{and}\qquad
nb-f(b)+b>C.
\]
Set
\[
a=nb-f(b).
\]
Then
\[
a+f(b)=nb,
\]
so (1) gives
\[
nb\mid(nb-f(b))^2+b f(nb-f(b)).
\]
Reducing modulo $b$, we obtain
\[
b\mid f(b)^2,
\]
proving (3).

In particular, if $p$ is prime, then
\[
p\mid f(p).
\]
Write
\[
f(p)=k_p p.
\]
By (2), for every sufficiently large prime $p$,
\[
1\le k_p\le f(1).
\]
Therefore some positive integer $k$ occurs as $k_p$ for infinitely many primes $p$.

Fix an arbitrary positive integer $a$. For every sufficiently large prime $p$ in this infinite set, equation (1) with $b=p$ gives
\[
a+kp\mid a^2+p f(a).
\]
Subtracting $a(a+kp)$,
\[
a+kp\mid p(f(a)-ka).
\]
For sufficiently large $p$, we have
\[
\gcd(a+kp,p)=\gcd(a,p)=1.
\]
Hence
\[
a+kp\mid f(a)-ka.
\]
The left side is unbounded as $p$ ranges through the infinite set, while the right side is fixed. Therefore
\[
f(a)=ka.
\]
Since $a$ was arbitrary,
\[
f(n)=kn
\]
for all positive integers $n$.

SOLUTION 2:
Again, the functions $f(n)=kn$ clearly work. We prove that every solution has this form.

Put
\[
c=f(1).
\]
Setting $a=1$ in (1) gives
\[
f(b)\le bc
\tag{2}
\]
for every sufficiently large $b$.

Now set $b=1$. For every sufficiently large $a$,
\[
a+c\mid a^2+f(a).
\]
Therefore
\[
\frac{f(a)+c^2}{a+c}
=
c-a+\frac{a^2+f(a)}{a+c}
\]
is a positive integer. By (2),
\[
0<
\frac{f(a)+c^2}{a+c}
\le c
\]
for all sufficiently large $a$.

Hence one value
\[
k\in\{1,2,\ldots,c\}
\]
is attained for infinitely many $a$. For all such $a$,
\[
f(a)=ka+c(k-c).
\tag{3}
\]

Now fix an arbitrary positive integer $r$. In (1), take $a=r$ and let $b$ run through the infinite set from (3). We get
\[
r+kb+c(k-c)\mid r^2+b f(r).
\tag{4}
\]
The quotient in (4) is an integer and tends to
\[
\frac{f(r)}k
\]
as $b\to\infty$ through that set. Therefore the quotient is eventually constant, and
\[
\frac{f(r)}k
\]
is an integer. Equality in (4) then gives, for infinitely many $b$,
\[
r^2+b f(r)
=
\frac{f(r)}k\bigl(r+kb+c(k-c)\bigr).
\]
After cancelling the $b$-terms,
\[
\frac{f(r)}k\bigl(r+c(k-c)\bigr)=r^2.
\tag{5}
\]

Thus
\[
r+c(k-c)\mid r^2
\]
for every positive integer $r$. Consequently it also divides
\[
c^2(k-c)^2,
\]
because
\[
r\equiv-c(k-c)\pmod{r+c(k-c)}.
\]
If $c(k-c)\ne0$, the positive integers
\[
r+c(k-c)
\]
are unbounded for large $r$, yet all divide the fixed nonzero integer $c^2(k-c)^2$, which is impossible. Hence
\[
k=c.
\]
Equation (5) now becomes
\[
\frac{f(r)}k\,r=r^2,
\]
so
\[
f(r)=kr
\]
for every $r$.

SOLUTION 3:
We first record upper and lower bounds valid for all sufficiently large $t$.

Setting $a=1$ in (1),
\[
f(t)\le t f(1).
\tag{2}
\]
Setting $b=1$ gives
\[
t+f(1)\mid t^2+f(t).
\]
Subtracting
\[
(t-f(1))(t+f(1)),
\]
we obtain
\[
t+f(1)\mid f(t)+f(1)^2.
\]
Since the latter is positive,
\[
f(t)+f(1)^2\ge t+f(1),
\]
or
\[
f(t)\ge t+f(1)(1-f(1)).
\tag{3}
\]
Thus $f(t)$ grows linearly in $t$.

Fix two distinct positive integers $a$ and $b$. For every sufficiently large $c$, the two numbers
\[
(a^2+c f(a))(b+f(c))
\]
and
\[
(b^2+c f(b))(a+f(c))
\]
are divisible by
\[
(a+f(c))(b+f(c)).
\]
Taking the linear combination obtained by multiplying the first expression by $f(b)$ and the second by $f(a)$ and subtracting, we find that
\[
(a+f(c))(b+f(c))
\]
divides
\[
\begin{aligned}
E(c)={}&
\bigl(a^2f(b)-b^2f(a)\bigr)f(c)\\
&+(b-a)f(a)f(b)c\\
&+ab\bigl(af(b)-bf(a)\bigr).
\end{aligned}
\tag{4}
\]

By (2)--(3), the divisor in (4) has order $c^2$, whereas $E(c)$ has order at most $c$. Hence, for all sufficiently large $c$,
\[
E(c)=0.
\tag{5}
\]

Since $a\ne b$, the coefficient
\[
(b-a)f(a)f(b)
\]
of $c$ in (4) is nonzero. Thus the coefficient of $f(c)$ cannot vanish, and (5) shows that
\[
f(c)=kc+\ell
\]
for all sufficiently large $c$, for some rational constants $k,\ell$. Since $f$ is integer-valued at all sufficiently large consecutive integers,
\[
k,\ell\in\mathbb Z.
\]

Taking the constant term after substituting $f(c)=kc+\ell$ into (5), we obtain, for all distinct positive integers $a,b$,
\[
\bigl(a^2f(b)-b^2f(a)\bigr)\ell
+
ab\bigl(af(b)-bf(a)\bigr)
=0.
\tag{6}
\]
Reducing (6) modulo $b$ gives
\[
b\mid \ell a^2f(b).
\tag{7}
\]
For all sufficiently large $b$,
\[
f(b)=kb+\ell,
\]
so (7) yields
\[
b\mid \ell^2a^2.
\]
Taking arbitrarily large $b$, we conclude that
\[
\ell=0.
\]

Equation (6) now becomes
\[
ab\bigl(af(b)-bf(a)\bigr)=0.
\]
Hence
\[
\frac{f(a)}a=\frac{f(b)}b
\]
for all distinct positive integers $a,b$. Therefore
\[
f(n)=kn
\]
for a fixed positive integer $k$.

SOLUTION 4:
Let
\[
\Gamma=\{(a,f(a)):a\in\mathbb Z_{>0}\}.
\]
For a point
\[
A=(a,f(a))\in\Gamma,
\]
define
\[
A'=\left(-f(a),-\frac{f(a)^2}{a}\right).
\]
Let
\[
\Gamma'=\{A':A\in\Gamma\}.
\]

We use the upper bound
\[
f(t)\le f(1)t
\tag{2}
\]
for all sufficiently large $t$, obtained by setting $a=1$ in (1).

\textbf{Claim.}
For every point $A\in\Gamma$, the set $\Gamma$ is contained in finitely many lines through $A'$.

\textit{Proof.}
Fix
\[
A=(a,f(a)).
\]
Interchanging $a$ and $b$ in (1), we have, for all sufficiently large $b$,
\[
b+f(a)\mid b^2+a f(b).
\]
Subtracting $b(b+f(a))$ gives
\[
b+f(a)\mid a f(b)-b f(a).
\]
Thus there is an integer $m$ such that
\[
a f(b)-f(a)b=m(b+f(a)).
\tag{3}
\]
Geometrically, the point
\[
B=(b,f(b))
\]
lies on the line
\[
ay-f(a)x=m(x+f(a)),
\]
which passes through
\[
A'=\left(-f(a),-\frac{f(a)^2}{a}\right).
\]

Apart from finitely many exceptional points, $\Gamma$ lies in the region
\[
0<y\le f(1)x.
\]
Since $A'$ lies in the lower-left quadrant, only finitely many of the integer-slope parameters $m$ in (3) can produce a line through $A'$ meeting this region. Adding the finitely many exceptional points proves the claim. $\square$

Take two distinct points $A,B\in\Gamma$. Then $A'\ne B'$. By the claim, $\Gamma$ is covered by finitely many lines through $A'$ and also by finitely many lines through $B'$. Two such lines have at most one common point unless they coincide. Hence all but finitely many points of $\Gamma$ lie on the unique line
\[
A'B'.
\]

Now take any third point $C\in\Gamma$. The same argument shows that all but finitely many points of $\Gamma$ lie on the line $A'C'$. Therefore
\[
A'B'=A'C'.
\]
Thus all points of $\Gamma'$ lie on a single line $\ell$, and all but finitely many points of $\Gamma$ lie on $\ell$ as well.

For each $A\in\Gamma$, the points
\[
A,\quad (0,0),\quad A'
\]
are collinear. Choose one of the infinitely many points $A\in\Gamma\cap\ell$. Then both $A$ and $A'$ lie on $\ell$, so
\[
(0,0)\in\ell.
\]
For an arbitrary point $A\in\Gamma$, we have $A'\in\ell$ and $(0,0)\in\ell$. Since $A,A',(0,0)$ are collinear,
\[
A\in\ell.
\]
Therefore all of $\Gamma$ lies on a line through the origin. Hence
\[
f(a)=ka
\]
for some positive constant $k$. Since $k=f(1)$, it is a positive integer.

Thus every solution has the form
\[
\boxed{f(n)=kn}.
\]
