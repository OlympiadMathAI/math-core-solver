ID: 0024

SOURCE: IMO Shortlist

YEAR: 2010

TOPIC: Number Theory

SUBTOPICS: Exponential Diophantine Equations, Multiplicative Order, Descent Bounds

STATEMENT:
Find all pairs $(m,n)$ of nonnegative integers satisfying
\[
m^2+2\cdot3^n
=
m\left(2^{n+1}-1\right).
\tag{1}
\]

SOLUTION 1:
The solutions are
\[
\boxed{(6,3),\ (9,3),\ (9,5),\ (54,5)}.
\]

For fixed $n$, equation (1) is quadratic in $m$:
\[
m^2-\left(2^{n+1}-1\right)m+2\cdot3^n=0.
\]
For $0\le n\le5$, direct calculation gives:
\[
\begin{array}{c|c|c}
n&\text{equation}&\text{nonnegative integer roots}\\
\hline
0&m^2-m+2=0&\text{none}\\
1&m^2-3m+6=0&\text{none}\\
2&m^2-7m+18=0&\text{none}\\
3&m^2-15m+54=0&m=6,9\\
4&m^2-31m+162=0&\text{none}\\
5&m^2-63m+486=0&m=9,54
\end{array}
\]

We prove that no solution exists for $n\ge6$. Suppose that $(m,n)$ satisfies (1). Since
\[
m\mid2\cdot3^n,
\]
the integer $m$ has one of the forms
\[
m=3^p
\quad\text{or}\quad
m=2\cdot3^q
\]
for suitable $0\le p,q\le n$.

Dividing (1) by $m$ and choosing the other exponent so that $p+q=n$, both cases reduce to
\[
3^p+2\cdot3^q=2^{n+1}-1,
\qquad
p+q=n.
\tag{2}
\]

Since each term on the left of (2) is less than $2^{n+1}$ and
\[
2<3^{2/3},
\]
we obtain
\[
p<\frac{2(n+1)}3,
\qquad
q<\frac{2(n+1)}3.
\]
Together with $p+q=n$, this gives
\[
\frac{n-2}{3}<p,q<\frac{2(n+1)}3.
\tag{3}
\]

Let
\[
h=\min\{p,q\}.
\]
For $n\ge6$, relation (3) gives $h>1$. The left side of (2) is divisible by $3^h$, so
\[
9\mid2^{n+1}-1.
\]
The multiplicative order of $2$ modulo $9$ is $6$. Hence
\[
6\mid n+1.
\]
Write
\[
n+1=6r
\qquad(r\ge1).
\]

Then
\[
\begin{aligned}
2^{n+1}-1
&=2^{6r}-1\\
&=4^{3r}-1\\
&=(4^{2r}+4^r+1)(2^r-1)(2^r+1).
\end{aligned}
\tag{4}
\]
The first factor in (4) is divisible by $3$ but not by $9$, because
\[
4^{2r}+4^r+1\equiv3\pmod9.
\]
The two odd numbers $2^r-1$ and $2^r+1$ are coprime. Since the whole product is divisible by $3^h$, one of them must be divisible by $3^{h-1}$. Thus
\[
3^{h-1}\le2^r+1\le3^r,
\]
and hence
\[
h-1\le r=\frac{n+1}{6}.
\tag{5}
\]

But from (3),
\[
h-1>\frac{n-2}{3}-1.
\]
Combining this with (5),
\[
\frac{n-2}{3}-1<\frac{n+1}{6},
\]
which simplifies to
\[
n<11.
\]
This is impossible, because $n\ge6$ and $6\mid n+1$; the next possible value after $n=5$ is $n=11$.

Therefore no solutions occur for $n\ge6$, and the four pairs listed above are all the solutions.
