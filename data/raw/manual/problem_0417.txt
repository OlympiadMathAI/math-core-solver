ID: 0417

SOURCE: IMO Shortlist

YEAR: 2023

TOPIC: Geometry

SUBTOPICS: Equilateral Triangles, Circumcentres, Radical Axes, Coaxial Circles, Directed Angles

STATEMENT:
Let $ABC$ be an equilateral triangle. Points $A_1,B_1,C_1$ lie inside $ABC$ such that triangle $A_1B_1C_1$ is scalene and
\[
BA_1=A_1C,
\qquad
CB_1=B_1A,
\qquad
AC_1=C_1B,
\]
while
\[
\angle BA_1C+\angle CB_1A+\angle AC_1B=480^\circ.
\]

Define
\[
A_2=BC_1\cap CB_1,
\qquad
B_2=CA_1\cap AC_1,
\qquad
C_2=AB_1\cap BA_1.
\]

Prove that the circumcircles of triangles
\[
AA_1A_2,\qquad BB_1B_2,\qquad CC_1C_2
\]
have two common points.

SOLUTION 1:
Let
\[
\delta_A=(AA_1A_2),\qquad
\delta_B=(BB_1B_2),\qquad
\delta_C=(CC_1C_2).
\]
We construct two distinct points having equal powers with respect to all three circles.

\textbf{Claim 1.}
The point $A_1$ is the circumcentre of triangle $A_2BC$. Cyclically, $B_1$ and $C_1$ are the circumcentres of triangles $B_2CA$ and $C_2AB$.

Because
\[
BA_1=A_1C,
\]
the point $A_1$ lies on the perpendicular bisector of $BC$. It lies inside triangle $BA_2C$, so it remains to show
\[
\angle BA_1C=2\angle BA_2C.
\]
Using $A_2B=BC_1$ as a line and $A_2C=CB_1$ as a line,
\[
\begin{aligned}
\angle BA_2C
&=
\angle A_2BA+\angle BAC+\angle ACA_2\\
&=
\frac12\bigl(180^\circ-\angle AC_1B\bigr)
+60^\circ
+\frac12\bigl(180^\circ-\angle CB_1A\bigr)\\
&=
240^\circ
-\frac12\bigl(\angle AC_1B+\angle CB_1A\bigr)\\
&=
\frac12\angle BA_1C.
\end{aligned}
\]
This proves the claim.

Claim 1 gives
\[
\begin{aligned}
\angle B_1B_2C_1
&=\angle B_1B_2A\\
&=\angle B_2AB_1\\
&=\angle C_1AC_2\\
&=\angle AC_2C_1\\
&=\angle B_1C_2C_1.
\end{aligned}
\]
Hence
\[
B_1,C_1,B_2,C_2
\]
are concyclic. Cyclically,
\[
\begin{gathered}
C_1,A_1,C_2,A_2\ \text{are cyclic},\\
A_1,B_1,A_2,B_2\ \text{are cyclic}.
\end{gathered}
\tag{1}
\]

The six points
\[
A_1,B_2,C_1,A_2,B_1,C_2
\]
do not lie on one circle. Indeed, if they did, the sum of the three alternate angles
\[
\angle C_2A_1B_2
+
\angle B_2C_1A_2
+
\angle A_2B_1C_2
\]
would be $360^\circ$. These angles are respectively
\[
\angle BA_1C,\qquad
\angle AC_1B,\qquad
\angle CB_1A,
\]
whose sum is $480^\circ$.

Apply the radical-axis theorem to the three circles in (1). Their pairwise radical axes are
\[
A_1A_2,\qquad B_1B_2,\qquad C_1C_2.
\]
Since the three circles are not identical, these lines concur at a point $X$.

Because
\[
X\in A_1A_2\cap B_1B_2\cap C_1C_2,
\]
the equal-power relation for the three auxiliary circles is
\[
XA_1\cdot XA_2
=
XB_1\cdot XB_2
=
XC_1\cdot XC_2.
\]
These three products are precisely the powers of $X$ with respect to
\[
\delta_A,\qquad\delta_B,\qquad\delta_C.
\]
Thus
\[
\operatorname{Pow}_{\delta_A}(X)
=
\operatorname{Pow}_{\delta_B}(X)
=
\operatorname{Pow}_{\delta_C}(X).
\tag{2}
\]

We now construct a second equal-power point.

Let the circle $(A_2BC)$ meet $\delta_A$ again at
\[
A_3\ne A_2.
\]
Define $B_3,C_3$ cyclically:
\[
B_3\in(B_2CA)\cap\delta_B,
\qquad
C_3\in(C_2AB)\cap\delta_C.
\]

\textbf{Claim 2.}
The points
\[
B,C,B_3,C_3
\]
are concyclic.

We use directed angles. Since
\[
B,C_2,A,C_3
\]
are cyclic and
\[
C,C_1,C_2,C_3
\]
are cyclic,
\[
\begin{aligned}
\measuredangle BC_3C
&=
\measuredangle BC_3C_2
+
\measuredangle C_2C_3C\\
&=
\measuredangle BAC_2
+
\measuredangle C_2C_1C.
\end{aligned}
\]
The line $CC_1$ is perpendicular to $AB$, because $C_1$ lies on the perpendicular bisector of $AB$. Also $A,C_2,B_1$ are collinear. Hence
\[
\measuredangle BC_3C
=
90^\circ+\measuredangle C_1C_2B_1.
\tag{3}
\]

Similarly,
\[
\measuredangle BB_3C
=
90^\circ+\measuredangle C_1B_2B_1.
\tag{4}
\]
The cyclic quadrilateral $B_1C_1B_2C_2$ gives
\[
\measuredangle C_1B_2B_1
=
\measuredangle C_1C_2B_1.
\]
Equations (3)-(4) prove Claim 2.

Cyclically,
\[
\begin{gathered}
C,A,C_3,A_3\ \text{are cyclic},\\
A,B,A_3,B_3\ \text{are cyclic}.
\end{gathered}
\tag{5}
\]

The six points
\[
A,C_3,B,A_3,C,B_3
\]
are not concyclic. Otherwise $A,B,C,B_3$ would lie on one circle, namely the circumcircle of $ABC$. But $A,C,B_2,B_3$ are cyclic by the definition of $B_3$, so this would force
\[
B_2
\]
onto the circumcircle of $ABC$, impossible because $B_2$ lies inside triangle $ABC$.

Apply the radical-axis theorem to the three circles in (5). Their pairwise radical axes are
\[
AA_3,\qquad BB_3,\qquad CC_3,
\]
so these lines concur at a point $Y$. As before,
\[
YA\cdot YA_3
=
YB\cdot YB_3
=
YC\cdot YC_3,
\]
and these are the powers of $Y$ with respect to $\delta_A,\delta_B,\delta_C$. Therefore
\[
\operatorname{Pow}_{\delta_A}(Y)
=
\operatorname{Pow}_{\delta_B}(Y)
=
\operatorname{Pow}_{\delta_C}(Y).
\tag{6}
\]

It remains to show that
\[
X\ne Y
\tag{7}
\]
and that the common radical axis through them meets the circles in two real points.

Let $O$ be the centre of the equilateral triangle $ABC$. Since the other two angles in the given sum are each smaller than $180^\circ$,
\[
\angle BA_1C>120^\circ.
\]
The point $A_1$ lies on the perpendicular bisector $AO$ of $BC$, so this inequality places $A_1$ inside triangle $BOC$. Cyclically,
\[
B_1\in\triangle COA,
\qquad
C_1\in\triangle AOB.
\]
The three triangles
\[
BA_1C,\qquad CB_1A,\qquad AC_1B
\]
therefore have disjoint interiors. It follows that
\[
A_1B_2C_1A_2B_1C_2
\]
is a convex hexagon. Its three main diagonals
\[
A_1A_2,\qquad B_1B_2,\qquad C_1C_2
\]
meet at $X$, so
\[
X\in A_1A_2.
\]
In particular,
\[
X\ \text{lies inside each of }\delta_A,\delta_B,\delta_C.
\tag{8}
\]

By Claim 1, $A_1$ is the centre of $(A_2BC)$. Since both $A_2$ and $A_3$ lie on this circle,
\[
A_1A_2=A_1A_3.
\tag{9}
\]
The four points $A,A_2,A_1,A_3$ lie on $\delta_A$. Equality (9) implies that the lines
\[
AA_2
\qquad\text{and}\qquad
AA_3=AY
\]
are reflections of each other in line $AA_1$, which is the perpendicular bisector of $BC$.

We make the required reflection observation explicit. Choose coordinates with $AA_1$ as the vertical axis and write
\[
A=(0,a),\qquad A_1=(0,h),\qquad A_2=(u,v),
\]
where $a>h$ and $v<a$ because $A_1,A_2$ lie inside triangle $ABC$. If $u\ne0$, the reflection of line $AA_2$ in $AA_1$ is the line through
\[
A
\qquad\text{and}\qquad
(-u,v).
\]
Its intersection with line $A_1A_2$ has the form
\[
A_1+t(A_2-A_1),
\qquad
t=\frac{a-h}{2v-a-h}.
\]
If the denominator is nonpositive, then $t\le0$; if it is positive, then
\[
2v-a-h<a-h
\]
because $v<a$, and hence $t>1$. Thus this reflected line never meets the segment $A_1A_2$ when $u\ne0$.

Since $X\in A_1A_2$ and $Y\in AA_3$, equality $X=Y$ can therefore occur only if $u=0$, that is,
\[
A_2\in AA_1.
\]
In that case $A_2$ lies on the perpendicular bisector of $BC$. The lines
\[
BA_2=BC_1
\qquad\text{and}\qquad
CA_2=CB_1
\]
are then reflections of one another in this perpendicular bisector, so $B_1$ and $C_1$ are reflections of one another. Because $A_1$ lies on the axis,
\[
A_1B_1=A_1C_1,
\]
contradicting the assumption that $A_1B_1C_1$ is scalene. This proves (7).

By (2) and (6), the distinct points $X,Y$ lie on the common radical axis of
\[
\delta_A,\delta_B,\delta_C.
\]
By (8), $X$ is inside each circle. Hence this common radical axis cuts $\delta_A$ in two real points. At either intersection the common power is $0$, so the point also lies on $\delta_B$ and $\delta_C$.

Therefore the three circles have two common points.
