ID: 0088

SOURCE: IMO Shortlist

YEAR: 2012

TOPIC: Number Theory

SUBTOPICS: Finite Fields, Additive Energy, Cauchy-Schwarz Inequality, Primitive Roots, Euler's Criterion

STATEMENT:
Prove that for every prime $p>100$ and every integer $r$, there exist integers $a,b$ such that
\[
p\mid a^2+b^5-r.
\]

SOLUTION 1:
All congruences in this solution are taken modulo $p$. Let
\[
\mathcal P=\{0,1,\ldots,p-1\}
\]
be a complete set of residue classes. For each $r\in\mathcal P$, define
\[
S_r=\{(a,b)\in\mathcal P^2:a^2+b^5\equiv r\},
\qquad
s_r=|S_r|.
\]
We must prove that $s_r>0$ for every $r$.

We shall use the elementary fact that, for any positive integer $d$ and any residue $c$, the congruence
\[
x^d\equiv c
\]
has at most $d$ solutions modulo $p$.

Let $N$ be the number of quadruples $(a,b,c,d)\in\mathcal P^4$ satisfying
\[
a^2+b^5\equiv c^2+d^5.
\]
Counting by the common value gives
\[
N=\sum_{r\in\mathcal P}s_r^2.
\tag{1}
\]
We now prove the upper bound
\[
N\le p(p^2+4p-4).
\tag{2}
\]

Fix a pair $(b,d)\in\mathcal P^2$.

If $b^5\equiv d^5$, then $a^2\equiv c^2$, so $a\equiv\pm c$. There are exactly
\[
2p-1
\]
such ordered pairs $(a,c)$: the $p$ pairs with $a=c$ and the $p$ pairs with $a=-c$, with $(0,0)$ counted twice.

Let $K$ be the number of pairs $(b,d)$ satisfying $b^5\equiv d^5$. For $b=0$, only $d=0$ works; for each nonzero $b$, there are at most five possible values of $d$. Hence
\[
K\le1+5(p-1)=5p-4.
\tag{3}
\]

If $b^5\not\equiv d^5$, then
\[
(a-c)(a+c)=d^5-b^5\ne0.
\]
The nonzero value of $a-c$ can be chosen in $p-1$ ways, and it then uniquely determines $a+c$, hence uniquely determines $a$ and $c$ because $p$ is odd. Thus there are exactly $p-1$ suitable pairs $(a,c)$.

Consequently,
\[
\begin{aligned}
N
&=K(2p-1)+(p^2-K)(p-1)\\
&=p^2(p-1)+Kp\\
&\le p^2(p-1)+(5p-4)p\\
&=p(p^2+4p-4),
\end{aligned}
\]
which proves (2).

Suppose now, for a contradiction, that
\[
S_r=\varnothing
\]
for some $r\in\mathcal P$. Clearly $r\ne0$. Let
\[
T=\{z^{10}:z\in\mathcal P\setminus\{0\}\}
\]
be the set of nonzero tenth powers. Since every residue has at most ten tenth roots,
\[
|T|\ge\frac{p-1}{10}\ge4.
\tag{4}
\]

For every $t\in T$, we also have
\[
S_{tr}=\varnothing.
\tag{5}
\]
Indeed, write $t=z^{10}$ with $z\ne0$. If $(x,y)\in S_{tr}$, then
\[
(z^{-5}x)^2+(z^{-2}y)^5
\equiv
z^{-10}(x^2+y^5)
\equiv
t^{-1}(tr)
\equiv r,
\]
contradicting $S_r=\varnothing$.

Because multiplication by the nonzero residue $r$ is injective, (4) and (5) show that at least four of the numbers $s_u$ are zero. Therefore at most $p-4$ of them are nonzero. Since
\[
\sum_{u\in\mathcal P}s_u=p^2,
\]
the Cauchy-Schwarz inequality and (1) give
\[
N=\sum_{u\in\mathcal P}s_u^2
\ge
\frac{p^4}{p-4}.
\tag{6}
\]
But
\[
\frac{p^4}{p-4}>p(p^2+4p-4),
\]
because their difference is
\[
\frac{4p(5p-4)}{p-4}>0.
\]
This contradicts (2). Hence every $S_r$ is nonempty, proving the result.

SOLUTION 2:
Again, all congruences are modulo $p$.

If $5\nmid p-1$, then the map
\[
b\longmapsto b^5
\]
is a bijection of the residue classes modulo $p$. Thus every $r$ is itself a fifth power, and the result follows by taking $a=0$.

We may therefore assume that $5\mid p-1$. Since $p$ is odd,
\[
p=10k+1
\]
for some integer $k\ge10$. Let $g$ be a primitive root modulo $p$.

We use the following two facts.

\textbf{(F1)} If a nonzero residue $x$ is not a quadratic residue, then Euler's criterion gives
\[
x^{(p-1)/2}=x^{5k}\equiv-1.
\]

\textbf{(F2)} For every integer $d$,
\[
\sum_{i=0}^{2k-1}g^{5di}
\equiv
\begin{cases}
2k,&2k\mid d,\\
0,&2k\nmid d.
\end{cases}
\tag{1}
\]
Indeed, $g^5$ has order $2k$, so this is the usual geometric-series sum.

Assume, for a contradiction, that some residue $r$ cannot be represented as
\[
a^2+b^5.
\]
Then $r\ne0$. For every residue $b$, the number $r-b^5$ is nonzero and is not a square; otherwise $r$ would have the required representation. Hence, by (F1),
\[
(r-b^5)^{5k}\equiv-1.
\tag{2}
\]

For $t=1,2,\ldots,k-1$, define
\[
S(t)=\sum_{i=0}^{2k-1}(r-g^{5i})^{5k}g^{5ti}.
\]
Using (2) with $b=g^i$ and then (F2),
\[
S(t)
\equiv
-\sum_{i=0}^{2k-1}g^{5ti}
\equiv0,
\tag{3}
\]
because $2k\nmid t$.

On the other hand, expanding by the binomial theorem and applying (F2),
\[
\begin{aligned}
S(t)
&=\sum_{i=0}^{2k-1}\sum_{j=0}^{5k}
(-1)^j\binom{5k}{j}r^{5k-j}g^{5(j+t)i}\\
&\equiv
\sum_{j=0}^{5k}(-1)^j\binom{5k}{j}r^{5k-j}
\begin{cases}
2k,&2k\mid j+t,\\
0,&2k\nmid j+t.
\end{cases}
\end{aligned}
\tag{4}
\]
Since
\[
1\le j+t<6k,
\]
the condition $2k\mid j+t$ occurs only for
\[
j=2k-t
\qquad\text{or}\qquad
j=4k-t.
\]
Combining (3) and (4), and then dividing by the nonzero factor $(-1)^t2k r^{k+t}$, gives
\[
\binom{5k}{2k-t}r^{2k}+\binom{5k}{4k-t}\equiv0.
\tag{5}
\]

Use (5) first with $t=1$ and then with $t=2$. Eliminating $r^{2k}$ yields
\[
\begin{aligned}
0
&\equiv
\binom{5k}{2k-2}\binom{5k}{4k-1}
-
\binom{5k}{2k-1}\binom{5k}{4k-2}\\
&=
\frac{(5k)!^2}{(2k-1)!(3k+2)!(4k-1)!(k+2)!}
\Bigl((2k-1)(k+2)-(3k+2)(4k-1)\Bigr)\\
&=
-\frac{(5k)!^2\,2k(5k+1)}
{(2k-1)!(3k+2)!(4k-1)!(k+2)!}
\pmod p.
\end{aligned}
\tag{6}
\]
Every factorial argument in (6), as well as the factors $2k$ and $5k+1$, is strictly less than
\[
p=10k+1.
\]
Therefore none of the factors occurring in the numerator or denominator is divisible by $p$. The denominator is invertible modulo $p$, while the numerator is nonzero modulo $p$. This contradicts (6).

Therefore every residue $r$ can be written as $a^2+b^5$ modulo $p$, which is equivalent to the required divisibility statement.
