ID: 0327

SOURCE: IMO Shortlist

YEAR: 2020

TOPIC: Number Theory

SUBTOPICS: Modular Recurrences, Multiplicative Orders, Periodic Residues, Prime Divisors

STATEMENT:
For an odd prime $p$ and an integer $n$, let
\[
d_p(n)\in\{0,1,\ldots,p-1\}
\]
be the remainder of $n$ modulo $p$.

A sequence
\[
(a_0,a_1,a_2,\ldots)
\]
is called a \emph{$p$-sequence} if $a_0$ is a positive integer coprime to $p$ and
\[
a_{n+1}=a_n+d_p(a_n)
\qquad(n\ge0).
\]

\begin{enumerate}
\item[(a)] Do there exist infinitely many primes $p$ for which there are two $p$-sequences $(a_n)$ and $(b_n)$ such that
\[
a_n>b_n
\]
for infinitely many $n$, and
\[
b_n>a_n
\]
for infinitely many $n$?

\item[(b)] Do there exist infinitely many primes $p$ for which there are two $p$-sequences $(a_n)$ and $(b_n)$ such that
\[
a_0<b_0,
\]
but
\[
a_n>b_n
\qquad(n\ge1)?
\]
\end{enumerate}

SOLUTION 1:
The answer to both parts is
\[
\boxed{\text{Yes}}.
\]

Fix an odd prime $p$, and let
\[
T=\operatorname{ord}_p(2)
\]
be the multiplicative order of $2$ modulo $p$.

Consider any $p$-sequence $(x_n)$. Modulo $p$,
\[
x_{n+1}\equiv2x_n\pmod p,
\]
and hence
\[
x_n\equiv2^n x_0\pmod p.
\tag{1}
\]
It follows that
\[
d_p(x_{n+T})=d_p(x_n)
\]
for every $n$. Define
\[
S_p(x_0)=\sum_{i=0}^{T-1}d_p(2^i x_0),
\tag{2}
\]
where the arguments in (2) may be arbitrary integers. Then
\[
x_{n+kT}=x_n+kS_p(x_0)
\qquad(n,k\ge0).
\tag{3}
\]
Also,
\[
S_p(x_0)=S_p(2^t x_0)
\qquad(t\ge0).
\tag{4}
\]

For convenience, put
\[
S_p^+=S_p(1)
=\sum_{i=0}^{T-1}d_p(2^i)
\]
and
\[
S_p^-=S_p(-1)
=\sum_{i=0}^{T-1}d_p(-2^i).
\]

\textbf{Part (a).}

Let $q>3$ be a prime, and choose a prime divisor
\[
p>3
\]
of
\[
2^q+1.
\]
Such a divisor exists. Indeed, $3\mid2^q+1$, but
\[
9\nmid2^q+1
\]
because $q\equiv1$ or $5\pmod6$.

The order of $2$ modulo $p$ is
\[
T=2q.
\]
Indeed,
\[
2^q\equiv-1\pmod p,
\]
and the order can be neither $1$, $2$, nor $q$.

Because multiplication by $2^q$ changes every nonzero residue to its negative, the two multisets
\[
\{d_p(2^i):0\le i<2q\}
\]
and
\[
\{d_p(-2^i):0\le i<2q\}
\]
coincide. Hence
\[
S_p^+=S_p^-.
\tag{5}
\]

Let $(a_n)$ and $(b_n)$ be the $p$-sequences beginning with
\[
a_0=p+1,
\qquad
b_0=p-1.
\]
Then
\[
a_0>b_0,
\]
whereas
\[
a_1=p+2<2p-2=b_1.
\]
By (3) and (5),
\[
a_{2kq}=a_0+kS_p^+>b_0+kS_p^+=b_{2kq}
\]
and
\[
a_{2kq+1}=a_1+kS_p^+<b_1+kS_p^+=b_{2kq+1}
\]
for every $k\ge0$. Thus the inequalities alternate infinitely often.

There are infinitely many possible primes $p$. If $q<r$ are distinct odd primes, then
\[
\gcd(2^q+1,2^r+1)=3.
\]
Therefore the prime divisors greater than $3$ obtained from different $q$ are distinct.

\textbf{Part (b).}

Let $q$ be an odd prime, and let $p$ be any prime divisor of
\[
2^q-1.
\]
Then
\[
T=q.
\]
The numbers $2^q-1$ corresponding to distinct prime values of $q$ are pairwise coprime, because
\[
\gcd(2^q-1,2^r-1)=2^{\gcd(q,r)}-1=1.
\]
Hence this construction also produces infinitely many primes $p$.

For every nonzero residue $x$ modulo $p$,
\[
d_p(x)+d_p(-x)=p.
\]
There are $q$ terms in each of the sums defining $S_p^+$ and $S_p^-$, so
\[
S_p^++S_p^-=pq.
\tag{6}
\]
The right-hand side is odd; consequently,
\[
S_p^+\ne S_p^-.
\tag{7}
\]

We now choose initial values $x_0<y_0$ such that
\[
S_p(x_0)>S_p(y_0).
\]
If
\[
S_p^+>S_p^-,
\]
take
\[
x_0=1,\qquad y_0=p-1.
\]
If
\[
S_p^+<S_p^-,
\]
take
\[
x_0=p-1,\qquad y_0=p+1.
\]
In either case the required two inequalities hold.

Let $(x_n)$ and $(y_n)$ be the corresponding $p$-sequences. For
\[
0\le r<q,
\]
equation (3) gives
\[
x_{Mq+r}-y_{Mq+r}
=
(x_r-y_r)
+
M\bigl(S_p(x_0)-S_p(y_0)\bigr).
\tag{8}
\]
Thus
\[
x_n>y_n
\]
for all sufficiently large $n$.

The two sequences never meet. Indeed, if
\[
x_n=y_n,
\]
then by (4),
\[
S_p(x_0)=S_p(x_n)=S_p(y_n)=S_p(y_0),
\]
contrary to the choice of the initial values.

Since $x_0<y_0$ but eventually $x_n>y_n$, there is a largest index $n_0$ for which
\[
x_{n_0}<y_{n_0}.
\]
Define
\[
a_n=x_{n+n_0},
\qquad
b_n=y_{n+n_0}.
\]
Then
\[
a_0<b_0,
\]
and, because equality never occurs,
\[
a_n>b_n
\qquad(n\ge1).
\]
This proves part (b).
