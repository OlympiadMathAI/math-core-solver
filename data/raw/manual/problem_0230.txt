ID: 0230

SOURCE: IMO Shortlist

YEAR: 2017

TOPIC: Geometry

SUBTOPICS: Common Tangents, Planar Graphs, Euler Formula, Continuous Deformation, Convex Hull

STATEMENT:
There are $2017$ mutually external circles drawn on a blackboard. No two circles are tangent, and no three circles have a common tangent.

A \emph{tangent segment} is a common tangent segment of two circles, starting at one tangency point and ending at the other. Luciano draws tangent segments one at a time so that no drawn segment intersects another circle or a previously drawn tangent segment. He continues until no further tangent segment can be drawn.

Find all possible numbers of tangent segments when he stops.

SOLUTION 1:
We prove the more general statement that for $n$ circles every maximal final configuration contains exactly
\[
3(n-1)
\]
tangent segments. For $n=2017$, this equals
\[
\boxed{6048}.
\]

First consider a special arrangement. Let the circle $C_i$ have center
\[
(i^2,0)
\]
and radius
\[
\frac i2
\qquad(1\le i\le n).
\]
The circles are mutually external, and every circle is visually eclipsed from the non-neighboring circles by its neighbors. The only drawable tangent segments join consecutive circles $C_i,C_{i+1}$, and exactly three can be drawn for each consecutive pair. Thus a maximal configuration has
\[
3(n-1)
\]
segments in this arrangement.

We show that the number cannot change when the circles are continuously moved and resized. A possible common tangent segment between two circles is identified by its orientation at the two circles: at each endpoint it leaves the circle in either the clockwise or the counterclockwise direction. Thus every pair of circles has four identified possible tangent segments.

Starting from any maximal configuration, move and resize the circles continuously, one at a time, while keeping them mutually external. By an arbitrarily small generic perturbation, the motion may be chosen so that no four circles ever have a common tangent. Keep each already selected segment with the same identification as long as it remains unobstructed. A change can occur only at a transition at which a selected segment becomes obstructed, or an unselected segment becomes unobstructed.

\textbf{Transition claim.}
At every transition, three circles $C_1,C_2,C_3$ become tangent to one common line $\ell$, and the only segments whose availability may change are the three pairwise tangent segments on $\ell$.

Indeed, if a tangent segment is first obstructed by a circle, that circle becomes the third circle tangent to its supporting line. If it is first obstructed by another tangent segment, the two segments first meet at an endpoint of one of them. They are then tangent at the same point of one circle but have opposite orientations there, so they lie on one line; their other endpoint circles give the required three-circle common tangent. The reverse argument handles a segment that becomes available.

Order the three tangency points on $\ell$ as those of
\[
C_1,C_2,C_3,
\]
and write $t_{ij}$ for the corresponding segment joining $C_i$ and $C_j$. Immediately before or after the transition, there are two possibilities.

\begin{itemize}
\item If $t_{13}$ passes through $C_2$, then $t_{13}$ is unavailable, while both $t_{12}$ and $t_{23}$ are available.
\item If $t_{13}$ does not pass through $C_2$, then $t_{13}$ is available, while $t_{12}$ and $t_{23}$ obstruct each other, so exactly one of them can be selected.
\end{itemize}

In either case, every maximal selection contains exactly two of
\[
t_{12},t_{23},t_{13}.
\]
Thus the number of selected segments is unchanged across the transition.

The space of arrangements of mutually external circles can be joined by such generic motions, so any given arrangement can be deformed to the special aligned arrangement. The number in a maximal configuration is invariant throughout the deformation. Hence it is always
\[
3(n-1).
\]
For $n=2017$, the only possible final number is $6048$.

SOLUTION 2:
Again let there be $n$ circles, and let
\[
s
\]
be the number of tangent segments in a maximal final configuration.

All tangent segments occurring on the boundary of the convex hull of the circles must be drawn, since they meet neither a circle nor another segment. Regard the final picture as a planar multigraph $G$: the circles are its $n$ vertices and the tangent segments are its $s$ edges.

The arcs of the circles and the tangent segments divide the plane into regions. At an endpoint of a tangent segment, the segment and a boundary arc may meet smoothly on one side. On the other side the direction of a traveler following the boundary makes a sudden turn of $180^\circ$; call such an incidence a \emph{sharp corner}.

\textbf{Claim 1.} The outer boundary of every internal region has at least three sharp corners.

Traverse such a boundary counterclockwise. Along circular arcs the tangent direction turns clockwise, and along straight segments it does not turn. Nevertheless, the total turning after a complete counterclockwise circuit is $2\pi$ counterclockwise. The necessary counterclockwise turning can occur only at sharp corners, with at most $\pi$ contributed by each. Two corners cannot suffice because at least one circular arc is present. Hence there are at least three.

\textbf{Claim 2.} Every internal region is simply connected.

Suppose an internal region had an outer boundary $B_1$ and at least one inner boundary. Start at a sharp corner $P_1$ of $B_1$ and move counterclockwise along $B_1$ with a vehicle whose front always follows the tangent direction, allowing the vehicle to move forward or backward when it passes a sharp corner. Attach a forward-pointing laser ray to the vehicle.

The first point where this ray meets the boundary must move continuously along $B_1$. If it jumped to another point of $B_1$, or to an inner boundary, then at the instant of the jump the unobstructed part of the ray would be an additional drawable common tangent segment, contradicting maximality.

Let $P_2,P_3$ be the next two sharp corners after $P_1$. Between $P_1$ and $P_3$, the vehicle and the laser endpoint move around $B_1$ in opposite senses and together sweep the entire region enclosed by $B_1$. The laser ray would therefore have to cross an inner boundary, contradicting the continuity just established. Hence no inner boundary exists.

The graph $G$ is connected. Indeed, all graph components meeting the outer boundary are joined by the convex-hull tangent segments. Any other component would lie inside an internal region and would form an inner boundary of that region, contradicting Claim 2.

\textbf{Claim 3.} Every internal region has exactly three sharp corners.

Use the same vehicle and laser construction on a simply connected internal region. By Claim 1, let $P_1,P_2,P_3$ be three consecutive sharp corners. As the vehicle moves from $P_1$ to $P_3$, its laser endpoint sweeps the whole boundary. If there were a fourth sharp corner $P_4$, the laser endpoint would pass through $P_4$ while the vehicle was at another point. The vehicle would then lie on the extension of the tangent segment through $P_4$. This would put three circles on a common tangent line, contrary to the hypothesis. Hence there are exactly three sharp corners.

Let $r$ be the number of internal regions. Every tangent segment contributes exactly one sharp corner at each endpoint, hence two sharp corners in total. Claim 3 gives
\[
2s=3r.
\tag{1}
\]
Since the connected planar multigraph has $n$ vertices, $s$ edges, and $r+1$ faces, Euler's formula gives
\[
n-s+(r+1)=2,
\]
or
\[
n-s+r=1.
\tag{2}
\]
Solving (1)--(2),
\[
s=3n-3,
\qquad
r=2n-2.
\]
Therefore, for $n=2017$,
\[
s=3\cdot2017-3=6048.
\]
