ID: 0120

SOURCE: IMO Shortlist

YEAR: 2014

TOPIC: Algebra

SUBTOPICS: Polynomials, Symmetry, Functional Equivalences, Asymptotic Growth, Inequalities

STATEMENT:
Consider all polynomials $P(x)$ with real coefficients having the following property: for every pair of real numbers $x,y$,
\[
|y^2-P(x)|\le2|x|
\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad
|x^2-P(y)|\le2|y|.
\tag{1}
\]
Determine all possible values of $P(0)$.

SOLUTION 1:
The set of possible values is
\[
\boxed{(-\infty,0)\cup\{1\}}.
\]

We first construct polynomials giving all these values.

Let $C>0$ and define
\[
P_C(x)=-\left(\frac{2x^2}{C}+C\right).
\]
Then $P_C(0)=-C$. For all real $x,y$,
\[
\begin{aligned}
|y^2-P_C(x)|
&=y^2+\frac{2x^2}{C}+C\\
&=y^2+\frac{x^2}{C}+\frac{(|x|-C)^2}{C}+2|x|\\
&\ge\frac{x^2}{C}+2|x|
\ge2|x|.
\end{aligned}
\]
Equality in the first estimate would require $y=0$ and $|x|=C$, while equality in the second would require $x=0$; these conditions are incompatible because $C>0$. Thus
\[
|y^2-P_C(x)|>2|x|
\]
for every $x,y$. By symmetry, the other inequality in (1) is also always false, so the equivalence holds.

The value $1$ is obtained from
\[
P(x)=x^2+1.
\]
Indeed,
\[
\begin{aligned}
|y^2-P(x)|\le2|x|
&\Longleftrightarrow (y^2-x^2-1)^2\le4x^2\\
&\Longleftrightarrow
\bigl(y^2-(x-1)^2\bigr)\bigl((x+1)^2-y^2\bigr)\ge0\\
&\Longleftrightarrow
\bigl((x+y)^2-1\bigr)\bigl(1-(x-y)^2\bigr)\ge0.
\end{aligned}
\]
The final condition is symmetric in $x$ and $y$, proving (1).

It remains to show that no nonnegative value other than $1$ is possible. Assume that $P$ satisfies (1) and
\[
P(0)\ge0.
\]

\textbf{Step 1: $P$ is even.}
For fixed $x$, condition (1), applied twice, gives
\[
|y^2-P(x)|\le2|x|
\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad
|y^2-P(-x)|\le2|x|
\]
for every $y$. Hence
\[
[P(x)-2|x|,P(x)+2|x|]\cap[0,\infty)
=
[P(-x)-2|x|,P(-x)+2|x|]\cap[0,\infty).
\tag{2}
\]

There are infinitely many $x$ for which
\[
P(x)+2|x|\ge0.
\]
Indeed, let $c$ be the coefficient of $x$ in $P$, and choose $\varepsilon\in\{-1,1\}$ so that $\varepsilon c\ge0$. For all sufficiently small $t>0$,
\[
P(\varepsilon t)+2t\ge0,
\]
because $P(0)\ge0$ and, when $P(0)=0$, the linear term of the left-hand side has coefficient $\varepsilon c+2>0$. Hence the required inequality holds for infinitely many values $x=\varepsilon t$.

For every such $x$, the intervals in (2) are nonempty and have equal right endpoints. Therefore
\[
P(x)=P(-x).
\]
This holds for infinitely many $x$, so the polynomial $P(x)-P(-x)$ is identically zero. Thus $P$ is even.

\textbf{Step 2: $P(t)>0$ for every real $t$.}
Suppose first that $P(t)=0$ for some $t\ne0$. By continuity, there is an open interval $I$ around $t$ such that
\[
|P(y)|\le2|y|
\]
for all $y\in I$. Putting $x=0$ in (1), the right-hand inequality is then true, so the left-hand inequality gives
\[
y^2=P(0)
\]
for every $y\in I$, which is impossible. Hence
\[
P(t)\ne0
\]
for every $t\ne0$.

If $P(0)=0$, then, since $P$ is even, we may write
\[
P(x)=x^2Q(x)
\]
for some polynomial $Q$. For every $y\ne0$, the left-hand condition in (1) with $x=0$ is false. Therefore the right-hand condition must also be false:
\[
|P(y)|>2|y|.
\]
This gives
\[
|yQ(y)|>2
\]
for every $y\ne0$, which is impossible for sufficiently small $y$. Thus $P(0)>0$. Since $P$ has no real zero, continuity now gives
\[
P(t)>0
\]
for all $t\in\mathbb R$.

\textbf{Step 3: $P$ has degree $2$.}
The polynomial $P$ is not constant. Otherwise, taking $x=\sqrt{P(0)}$ and then taking $|y|$ large would make the first condition in (1) false and the second one true.

Because $P$ is even and everywhere positive, its degree $n$ is even, at least $2$, and its leading coefficient is positive. Suppose $n\ge4$. Put
\[
y=\sqrt{P(x)}.
\]
The first condition in (1) is true, so
\[
\left|x^2-P\!\left(\sqrt{P(x)}\right)\right|
\le2\sqrt{P(x)},
\]
and hence
\[
P\!\left(\sqrt{P(x)}\right)
\le x^2+2\sqrt{P(x)}.
\tag{3}
\]

Choose constants $a,b>0$ and $x_0>0$ such that
\[
ax^n<P(x)<bx^n
\]
for all $x>x_0$. For sufficiently large $x$, also $\sqrt{P(x)}>x_0$, and therefore
\[
P\!\left(\sqrt{P(x)}\right)
>aP(x)^{n/2}
>a^{n/2+1}x^{n^2/2}.
\]
On the other hand, (3) gives
\[
P\!\left(\sqrt{P(x)}\right)
<x^2+2\sqrt b\,x^{n/2}
\le(1+2\sqrt b)x^{n/2}
\]
for large $x$, since $n/2\ge2$. Thus
\[
a^{n/2+1}x^{(n^2-n)/2}<1+2\sqrt b
\]
for arbitrarily large $x$, a contradiction. Hence $\deg P=2$.

\textbf{Step 4: $P(x)=x^2+1$.}
Write
\[
P(x)=Ax^2+B,
\qquad A>0.
\]
For sufficiently large positive $x$, choose
\[
y=\sqrt A\,x.
\]
Then
\[
|y^2-P(x)|=|B|\le2x,
\]
so the other inequality in (1) gives
\[
|(1-A^2)x^2-B|\le2\sqrt A\,x.
\]
Dividing by $x^2$ and letting $x\to\infty$ yields $A^2=1$. Since $A>0$,
\[
A=1.
\]

Now put $y=x+1$ with $x>0$. Condition (1) becomes
\[
|2x+1-B|\le2x
\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad
|2x+1+B|\le2x+2.
\]
Equivalently,
\[
B\in[1,4x+1]
\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad
B\in[-4x-3,1].
\]
For sufficiently large $x$, at least one of these two inclusions holds; by equivalence both must hold. Their intersection is $\{1\}$, so
\[
B=1.
\]
Therefore the only polynomial satisfying (1) with $P(0)\ge0$ is
\[
P(x)=x^2+1,
\]
and the complete set of possible values of $P(0)$ is
\[
(-\infty,0)\cup\{1\}.
\]
