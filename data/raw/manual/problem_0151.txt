ID: 0151

SOURCE: IMO Shortlist

YEAR: 2015

TOPIC: Algebra

SUBTOPICS: Polynomials, Finite Differences, Root Counting, Rearrangement of Values

STATEMENT:
Let $n\ge2$ be fixed. Two polynomials $P,Q$ with real coefficients are called \emph{block-similar} if, for every $i\in\{1,2,\ldots,n\}$, the two sequences
\[
P(2015i),P(2015i-1),\ldots,P(2015i-2014)
\]
and
\[
Q(2015i),Q(2015i-1),\ldots,Q(2015i-2014)
\]
are permutations of each other.

\textbf{(a)} Prove that there exist distinct block-similar polynomials of degree $n+1$.

\textbf{(b)} Prove that there do not exist distinct block-similar polynomials of degree $n$.

SOLUTION 1:
Put
\[
k=2015=2\ell+1,
\qquad \ell=1007.
\]

\textbf{Part (a).}
Define
\[
P(x)=\prod_{j=0}^{n}(x-jk),
\qquad
Q(x)=\prod_{j=0}^{n}(x-jk-1)=P(x-1).
\]
Both polynomials have degree $n+1$ and are distinct.

For a fixed $i\in\{1,\ldots,n\}$, the values of $Q$ on the $i$th block are
\[
P(ik-1),P(ik-2),\ldots,P((i-1)k).
\]
The values of $P$ on that block are
\[
P(ik),P(ik-1),\ldots,P((i-1)k+1).
\]
Since
\[
P(ik)=P((i-1)k)=0,
\]
these two lists are permutations of each other. Thus $P$ and $Q$ are block-similar.

\textbf{Part (b).}
For a polynomial $F$ and a nonnegative integer $m$, define
\[
\Sigma F(m)=\sum_{j=1}^{m}F(j),
\qquad
\Sigma F(0)=0.
\]
By the standard formulas for sums of powers, $\Sigma F(m)$ extends uniquely to a polynomial in $m$ of degree $\deg F+1$ when $F\ne0$.

Assume, for a contradiction, that distinct block-similar polynomials $P,Q$ of degree at most $n$ exist. Since corresponding blocks are permutations, their sums and sums of squares agree. Consequently both
\[
\Sigma(P-Q)
\qquad\text{and}\qquad
\Sigma(P^2-Q^2)
\]
vanish at
\[
0,k,2k,\ldots,nk.
\tag{1}
\]
Set
\[
T(x)=\prod_{j=0}^{n}(x-jk).
\]

\textbf{Lemma.}
Let $F$ be a nonzero polynomial such that $\Sigma F$ vanishes at all the points in (1). Then
\[
\deg F\ge n,
\]
and there exists a polynomial $G$ with
\[
\deg G=\deg F-n
\]
such that
\[
F(x)=T(x)G(x)-T(x-1)G(x-1).
\tag{2}
\]

\textit{Proof.}
If $\deg F<n$, then $\deg\Sigma F<n+1$, while $\Sigma F$ has at least $n+1$ distinct roots. Hence $\Sigma F=0$, which would imply $F=0$, a contradiction. Thus $\deg F\ge n$.

Because of the roots in (1),
\[
\Sigma F(x)=T(x)G(x)
\]
for some polynomial $G$ of degree $\deg F-n$. Define
\[
F_1(x)=T(x)G(x)-T(x-1)G(x-1).
\]
For every positive integer $m$,
\[
\begin{aligned}
\Sigma F_1(m)
&=\sum_{j=1}^{m}\bigl(T(j)G(j)-T(j-1)G(j-1)\bigr)\\
&=T(m)G(m)-T(0)G(0)\\
&=T(m)G(m)=\Sigma F(m).
\end{aligned}
\]
Thus the polynomial $\Sigma(F-F_1)$ has infinitely many zeros, so it is identically zero. Hence $F=F_1$, proving (2). $\square$

Apply the lemma to
\[
R_1=P-Q.
\]
This polynomial is nonzero and has degree at most $n$, so
\[
\deg R_1=n
\]
and
\[
R_1(x)=\alpha\bigl(T(x)-T(x-1)\bigr)
\tag{3}
\]
for some nonzero constant $\alpha$.

We next prove that
\[
S=P+Q
\]
is constant. Suppose not. Then
\[
R_2=P^2-Q^2=R_1S
\]
is nonzero, satisfies the lemma, and has degree strictly between $n$ and $2n$. Therefore
\[
R_2(x)=T(x)G(x)-T(x-1)G(x-1)
\tag{4}
\]
for some polynomial $G$ with
\[
1\le\deg G\le n.
\]
Rewrite (4) as
\[
R_2(x)
=T(x)\bigl(G(x)-G(x-1)\bigr)
+G(x-1)\bigl(T(x)-T(x-1)\bigr).
\]
By (3), the polynomial $T(x)-T(x-1)$ divides $R_2(x)$, hence it divides
\[
T(x)\bigl(G(x)-G(x-1)\bigr).
\]
But
\[
\gcd\bigl(T(x),T(x)-T(x-1)\bigr)
=\gcd\bigl(T(x),T(x-1)\bigr)=1,
\]
because the roots $0,k,\ldots,nk$ of $T(x)$ are disjoint from the roots $1,k+1,\ldots,nk+1$ of $T(x-1)$. Thus
\[
T(x)-T(x-1)\mid G(x)-G(x-1).
\]
This is impossible: the divisor has degree $n$, whereas the nonzero polynomial $G(x)-G(x-1)$ has degree at most $n-1$. Hence $S$ is constant, say
\[
P+Q=\beta.
\]

Replacing $P,Q$ by
\[
\frac{2P-\beta}{\alpha},
\qquad
\frac{2Q-\beta}{\alpha},
\]
we may assume, without loss of generality, that
\[
P=-Q=T(x)-T(x-1).
\tag{5}
\]

For every $i=1,\ldots,n$, the values
\[
T((i-1)k+1)
\quad\text{and}\quad
T(ik-1)
\]
have the same sign, since both arguments lie strictly between the consecutive roots $(i-1)k$ and $ik$ of $T$. By (5),
\[
P((i-1)k+1)=T((i-1)k+1)
\]
and
\[
P(ik)=-T(ik-1)
\]
have opposite signs. Hence $P$ has a root in each of the $n$ disjoint intervals
\[
[(i-1)k+1,ik].
\]
Since $\deg P=n$, it has exactly one root in each such interval.

In the first block, the list
\[
P(1),P(2),\ldots,P(k)
\]
changes sign exactly once. Since $P$ and $-P$ are block-similar, this list contains equally many positive and negative terms. As $k=2\ell+1$ is odd, its middle term must be zero:
\[
P(\ell+1)=0.
\]
By (5), this means
\[
T(\ell+1)=T(\ell).
\tag{6}
\]
However,
\[
\begin{aligned}
|T(\ell+1)|
&=|\ell+1|\,|\ell|\prod_{j=2}^{n}|\ell+1-jk|\\
&<|\ell|\,|\ell+1|\prod_{j=2}^{n}|\ell-jk|\\
&=|T(\ell)|,
\end{aligned}
\]
because $n\ge2$. This contradicts (6). Therefore no two distinct block-similar polynomials of degree at most $n$ exist, proving part (b).

SOLUTION 2:
Part (a) is proved by the same construction as in Solution 1. We give a different proof of part (b).

Suppose that distinct block-similar polynomials $P,Q$ of degree at most $n$ exist. Put
\[
R=P-Q,
\qquad
S=P+Q,
\qquad
k=2015=2\ell+1.
\]
For $i=1,2,\ldots,n$, define
\[
I_i=[(i-1)k+1,ik]
\]
and let
\[
Z_i=\{(i-1)k+1,(i-1)k+2,\ldots,ik\}
\]
be its set of integer points.

\textbf{Step 1: $R$ has exactly one simple root in each $I_i$.}
Choose $p^-,p^+\in Z_i$ such that
\[
P(p^-)=\min_{x\in Z_i}P(x),
\qquad
P(p^+)=\max_{x\in Z_i}P(x).
\]
Since the values of $P$ and $Q$ on $Z_i$ are permutations of each other,
\[
R(p^-)=P(p^-)-Q(p^-)\le0,
\]
and
\[
R(p^+)=P(p^+)-Q(p^+)\ge0.
\]
Thus $R$ has a root in $I_i$. This holds for each of the $n$ pairwise disjoint intervals. Since $R$ is nonzero and $\deg R\le n$, it has exactly one root in each $I_i$, and every one of these roots is simple.

\textbf{Step 2: $S$ is constant.}
We first show that, for each $i$, the sequence
\[
S((i-1)k+1),S((i-1)k+2),\ldots,S(ik)
\]
cannot be strictly increasing.

By interchanging $P$ and $Q$ if necessary, assume
\[
P(ik)\le Q(ik).
\tag{7}
\]
Choose $p^-,p^+$ as above. If $P(p^-)=P(p^+)$, then $P$ is constant on $Z_i$; because the $Q$-values are a permutation of the $P$-values, $Q$ is constant there as well, giving more than one zero of $R$ in $I_i$, impossible. Hence $p^-\ne p^+$.

If $p^->p^+$, then
\[
S(p^-)=P(p^-)+Q(p^-)
\le Q(p^+)+P(p^+)=S(p^+),
\]
so the sequence is not strictly increasing.

It remains to consider $p^-<p^+$. If
\[
P(p^+)>Q(p^+),
\]
then
\[
R(p^-)\le0<R(p^+)
\quad\text{and}\quad
R(ik)\le0
\]
by (7). Thus $R$ has roots on both sides of $p^+$ inside $I_i$, contradicting Step 1.

Hence
\[
P(p^+)=Q(p^+),
\]
so $p^+$ is the unique root of $R$ in $I_i$. If $p^+=ik$, then all the other values of $R$ on $Z_i$ have the same sign. Their sum cannot be zero, contradicting the equality of the sums of the two block permutations. If $p^+<ik$, then $R(p^-)$ and $R(ik)$ are both negative. A polynomial having the same sign at these endpoints must have an even number of simple sign-changing roots between them, contradicting the fact that $p^+$ is the unique simple root. Therefore the sequence of $S$-values is not strictly increasing.

By the symmetric argument, it is not strictly decreasing either. Hence the polynomial
\[
\Delta S(x)=S(x)-S(x-1)
\]
takes both a nonnegative and a nonpositive value in every interval $I_i$, and therefore has a root in each $I_i$. But
\[
\deg\Delta S<n,
\]
so $\Delta S$ must be identically zero. Thus $S$ is constant.

\textbf{Step 3: final contradiction.}
Subtracting half of this constant from both $P$ and $Q$, we may assume
\[
P=-Q.
\]
Then $R=2P$, so $P$ has exactly one simple root in every $I_i$.

Because the values of $P$ and $-P$ on each $Z_i$ are permutations, there are equally many positive and negative values. Since $|Z_i|=k$ is odd, exactly one value is zero, and the unique root must be the middle point of $Z_i$. Therefore the roots of $P$ are
\[
ik-\ell,
\qquad i=1,2,\ldots,n.
\]
Thus
\[
P(x)=c\prod_{i=1}^{n}(x-ik+\ell)
\]
for some nonzero real constant $c$.

For every $t\in Z_1\setminus\{1\}$,
\[
\begin{aligned}
|P(t)|
&=|c|\,|t-\ell-1|\prod_{i=2}^{n}|t-ik+\ell|\\
&<|c|\,\ell\prod_{i=2}^{n}|1-ik+\ell|\\
&=|P(1)|.
\end{aligned}
\]
Therefore no value $P(t)$ in the first block equals $-P(1)$. This contradicts the fact that the first-block values of $P$ and $-P$ are permutations of each other. Hence distinct block-similar polynomials of degree at most $n$ do not exist.
