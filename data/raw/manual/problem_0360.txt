ID: 0360

SOURCE: IMO Shortlist

YEAR: 2021

TOPIC: Number Theory

SUBTOPICS: Modular Permutations, Induction, Partition Lemma, Cyclic Shifts

STATEMENT:
Determine all integers $n\ge2$ with the following property:

Every set of $n$ pairwise distinct integers whose sum is not divisible by $n$ can be arranged in an order
\[
a_1,a_2,\ldots,a_n
\]
such that
\[
n\mid a_1+2a_2+\cdots+na_n.
\]

SOLUTION 1:
The answer is
\[
\boxed{\text{all odd integers and all powers of }2}.
\]

We first exclude every other integer. Let
\[
n=2^ku,
\]
where $k\ge1$ and $u\ge3$ is odd. Take the integer
\[
2^k+1
\]
together with
\[
1+n,\ 1+2n,\ \ldots,\ 1+(n-1)n.
\]
These are $n$ pairwise distinct integers, and the last $n-1$ of them are congruent to $1$ modulo $n$. Their total sum is congruent to
\[
2^k\not\equiv0\pmod n.
\]
However, all $n$ integers are congruent to $1$ modulo $2^k$. Therefore, for every ordering,
\[
\begin{aligned}
a_1+2a_2+\cdots+na_n
&\equiv
1+2+\cdots+n\\
&=
\frac{n(n+1)}2\\
&=
2^{k-1}u(2^ku+1)
\not\equiv0
\pmod{2^k}.
\end{aligned}
\]
The weighted sum is not divisible by $n$. Thus no even integer having an odd factor greater than $1$ has the property.

We now prove that every odd $n$ and every power of $2$ works.

Let $S$ be a set of $n$ integers, and let
\[
s=\sum_{x\in S}x.
\]

\textbf{Lemma 1.}
Suppose an ordering
\[
a_1,\ldots,a_n
\]
of $S$ satisfies
\[
\gcd(n,s)\mid\sum_{i=1}^n ia_i.
\]
Then a cyclic shift of this ordering has weighted sum divisible by $n$.

\textit{Proof.}
Put
\[
r=\sum_{i=1}^n ia_i
\]
and extend the indices cyclically by $a_{j+n}=a_j$. For
\[
b_i=a_{i+x},
\]
we have
\[
\sum_{i=1}^n ib_i
\equiv
r-xs
\pmod n.
\]
The congruence
\[
r-xs\equiv0\pmod n
\]
is solvable precisely because $\gcd(n,s)\mid r$. $\square$

\textbf{Lemma 2.}
Let $k\ge1$ and $m>1$. Every set of $km$ integers can be partitioned into $m$ sets of $k$ integers so that each part satisfies at least one of the following:

\begin{enumerate}
\item its sum is not divisible by $k$;
\item all its elements are congruent modulo $k$.
\end{enumerate}

\textit{Proof.}
Let $T$ be the given set. The case $k=1$ is immediate, so assume $k\ge2$.

First let $m=2$. If $k$ elements have the same residue modulo $k$, use them as one part $A$, and let the other part be $B$. If the sum of $B$ is not divisible by $k$, we are done. If its sum is divisible by $k$ and some element of $B$ has a different residue from the elements of $A$, exchange those two elements. Both new parts then have nonzero sums modulo $k$. If no such exchange is possible, all $2k$ elements are congruent modulo $k$, and every partition works.

Suppose now that no $k$ elements have the same residue. This case cannot occur when $k=2$; for $k\ge3$, at least three residue classes occur. Choose elements $a,b,c$ with pairwise distinct residues and choose
\[
U\subseteq T\setminus\{a,b,c\},
\qquad |U|=k-1.
\]
The three sets
\[
U\cup\{a\},\quad
U\cup\{b\},\quad
U\cup\{c\}
\]
have three distinct sums modulo $k$. At least one of those sums is congruent to neither $0$ nor the total sum of all $2k$ elements. Choose that set as $A$. Then neither $A$ nor its complement has sum divisible by $k$.

This proves the case $m=2$. For $m>2$, proceed by induction. If $k$ elements have the same residue, take them as one part and apply the induction hypothesis to the remaining elements. Otherwise, choose any $k-1$ elements as $U$. Among the remaining elements there is an $a$ such that
\[
a\not\equiv-\sum_{x\in U}x\pmod k;
\]
otherwise at least $k$ remaining elements would have the same residue. Then
\[
U\cup\{a\}
\]
has nonzero sum modulo $k$, and the induction hypothesis applies to its complement. $\square$

We prove the positive result by induction on $n$ within the class of odd integers and powers of $2$.

If $n$ is prime, then
\[
\gcd(n,s)=1
\]
because $n\nmid s$. Any ordering satisfies the divisibility required in Lemma 1, so a cyclic shift gives the desired arrangement.

Now suppose that $n$ is composite and belongs to the allowed class. Choose a prime $p\mid n$ such that
\[
v_p(s)<v_p(n),
\]
and put
\[
k=\frac np.
\]
Then
\[
\gcd(n,s)\mid k.
\tag{1}
\]

By Lemma 2, partition $S$ into $p$ parts of size $k$, each of which either has sum not divisible by $k$ or consists of elements congruent modulo $k$.

First suppose that $n$ is odd. Then $k$ is odd. In a part of the first type, the induction hypothesis provides an ordering
\[
x_1,\ldots,x_k
\]
such that
\[
k\mid\sum_{i=1}^k ix_i.
\]
In a part of the second type, every ordering has this property, because if all terms are congruent to $x$ modulo $k$, then
\[
\sum_{i=1}^k ix_i
\equiv
x\frac{k(k+1)}2
\equiv0\pmod k.
\]
Concatenate the ordered parts. The resulting weighted sum is divisible by $k$, since the positional offset of each block is a multiple of $k$. By (1) and Lemma 1, a cyclic shift makes it divisible by $n$.

It remains to treat
\[
n=2^h
\]
with $h\ge2$. Here
\[
p=2,\qquad k=2^{h-1}.
\]
Lemma 2 gives two parts of size $k$. Every part admits an ordering
\[
x_1,\ldots,x_k
\]
for which
\[
\frac k2\mid\sum_{i=1}^k ix_i.
\tag{2}
\]
For a part of the first type this follows from the induction hypothesis, which gives divisibility even by $k$; for a part of the second type it follows from
\[
\sum_{i=1}^k ix_i
\equiv
x\frac{k(k+1)}2
\equiv0\pmod{k/2}.
\]

If both parts are of the first type, concatenate their inductively chosen orderings. Each block contribution is divisible by $k$, so the full weighted sum is divisible by $k$.

Otherwise, choose a part of the second type for the odd positions
\[
1,3,\ldots,2k-1,
\]
and put the other part in the even positions
\[
2,4,\ldots,2k,
\]
using orderings satisfying (2). For the second-type part, its ordinary sum is divisible by $k$, so its contribution at the odd positions is
\[
\sum_{i=1}^k(2i-1)x_i
=
2\sum_{i=1}^kix_i-\sum_{i=1}^kx_i,
\]
which is divisible by $k$. The contribution of the other part at the even positions is
\[
2\sum_{i=1}^kiy_i,
\]
also divisible by $k$. Thus the total weighted sum is divisible by $k$.

In either case, (1) and Lemma 1 provide a cyclic shift whose weighted sum is divisible by $n$. This completes the induction and the classification.
