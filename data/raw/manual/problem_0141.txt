ID: 0141

SOURCE: IMO Shortlist

YEAR: 2014

TOPIC: Number Theory

SUBTOPICS: Floor Functions, Parity, Modular Arithmetic, Fermat's Little Theorem, Divisibility

STATEMENT:
Let $n>1$ be a fixed integer. Prove that infinitely many terms of the sequence $(a_k)_{k\ge1}$ defined by
\[
a_k=\left\lfloor\frac{n^k}{k}\right\rfloor
\]
are odd.

SOLUTION 1:
If $n$ is odd, take
\[
k=n^m\qquad(m=1,2,\ldots).
\]
Then
\[
a_k=\frac{n^{n^m}}{n^m}=n^{n^m-m},
\]
which is odd. Thus assume that $n$ is even, and write $n=2t$.

For every $m\ge2$, consider
\[
n^{2^m}-2^m
=2^m\left(2^{2^m-m}t^{2^m}-1\right).
\]
The factor in parentheses is an odd integer greater than $1$, so it has an odd prime divisor $p$. Set
\[
k=p2^m.
\]
Because
\[
n^{2^m}\equiv2^m\pmod p,
\]
Fermat's little theorem gives
\[
n^k=(n^{2^m})^p\equiv(2^m)^p=(2^p)^m\equiv2^m\pmod p.
\]
Also $n^k\equiv2^m\equiv0\pmod{2^m}$. Since $p$ is odd,
\[
p2^m\mid n^k-2^m.
\tag{1}
\]
Moreover,
\[
0<2^m<p2^m=k,
\]
so (1) identifies the remainder of $n^k$ upon division by $k$. Consequently
\[
a_k=\frac{n^k-2^m}{p2^m}
=\frac{n^k/2^m-1}{p}.
\]
The integer $n^k/2^m$ is even, hence the numerator is odd; division by the odd number $p$ leaves an odd integer. Thus $a_k$ is odd.

For different values of $m$, the constructed $k$ have different $2$-adic valuations $v_2(k)=m$. Hence infinitely many distinct indices are obtained.

SOLUTION 2:
The case of odd $n$ is handled by taking $k=n^m$, exactly as in Solution 1. Suppose that $n$ is even.

First assume $n>2$. Choose an odd prime $p$ dividing $n-1$. We prove by induction that
\[
p^{i+1}\mid n^{p^i}-1
\qquad(i\ge0).
\tag{1}
\]
The case $i=0$ is the choice of $p$. If (1) holds for $i$, then
\[
n^{p^{i+1}}-1
=(n^{p^i}-1)\left(1+n^{p^i}+\cdots+n^{(p-1)p^i}\right).
\]
Every term in the second factor is congruent to $1$ modulo $p$, so that factor is divisible by $p$. This proves the induction step.

For $i\ge1$, put $k=p^i$. Then $k\mid n^k-1$ and $0<1<k$, whence
\[
a_k=\frac{n^k-1}{p^i}.
\]
The numerator and denominator are both odd, so $a_k$ is odd. The indices $p^i$ are distinct.

It remains to treat $n=2$. For every $i\ge1$, set
\[
k=3\cdot4^i.
\]
Both $2^k$ and $4^i$ are divisible by $4^i$, and they are both congruent to $1$ modulo $3$. Therefore
\[
3\cdot4^i\mid2^k-4^i.
\]
Since $0<4^i<k$,
\[
a_k=\frac{2^k-4^i}{3\cdot4^i}
=\frac{2^{k-2i}-1}{3},
\]
which is odd. Again, this supplies infinitely many indices.

SOLUTION 3:
For odd $n$, use $k=n^m$ as before. Let $n$ now be even. Choose an odd prime $p$ dividing $n+1$.

For each $i\ge1$, define
\[
u_i=
\min\{u\in\mathbb Z_{>0}:2^i\mid pu+1\}.
\]
Because $p$ is odd, the congruence $pu\equiv-1\pmod{2^i}$ has a solution, and we may choose
\[
1\le u_i<2^i.
\tag{1}
\]
The sequence $(u_i)$ is unbounded: otherwise some fixed positive $u$ would satisfy $2^i\mid pu+1$ for arbitrarily large $i$. Hence there are infinitely many indices $i$ for which
\[
u_i<u_{i+1}.
\tag{2}
\]
For every such $i$, minimality gives
\[
2^{i+1}\nmid pu_i+1.
\tag{3}
\]

Set
\[
k=p2^i.
\]
Since $p\mid n+1$, we have $p\mid n^p+1$, and therefore
\[
p\mid n^{p2^i}-1=n^k-1.
\]
Also $2^i$ divides both $n^k$ and $pu_i+1$. Thus
\[
p2^i\mid n^k-(pu_i+1).
\tag{4}
\]
By (1),
\[
0<pu_i+1<p2^i=k,
\]
so (4) yields
\[
a_k=\frac{n^k-(pu_i+1)}{p2^i}.
\]
Now $2^{i+1}\mid n^k$, whereas (3) says that $pu_i+1$ is divisible by $2^i$ but not by $2^{i+1}$. Hence the numerator in the last fraction has exact $2$-adic valuation $i$. After division by $p2^i$, the result is odd.

Condition (2) holds for infinitely many $i$, so this produces infinitely many odd terms.
