ID: 0042

SOURCE: IMO Shortlist

YEAR: 2011

TOPIC: Combinatorics

SUBTOPICS: Double Counting, Residue Classes, Extremal Configurations

STATEMENT:
On a square table of $2011\times2011$ cells, place finitely many napkins, each covering a square of $52\times52$ cells. In each cell write the number of napkins covering it. For a fixed configuration, let $k$ be the greatest number of cells that all contain the same nonzero integer.

Over all possible napkin configurations, determine the largest possible value of $k$.

SOLUTION 1:
The answer is
\[
\boxed{3986729}.
\]
Indeed,
\[
2011^2=4044121
\]
and
\[
(52^2-35^2)\cdot39-17^2=57392,
\]
so the claimed answer is
\[
2011^2-57392=3986729.
\]

We first give a construction. Put
\[
m=39,
\qquad
2011=52m-17.
\]
Number rows from bottom to top and columns from left to right by $1,2,\ldots,2011$. Denote a napkin by the coordinates of its lower-left cell. Use the following napkins:

\[
(52i+36,52j+1)
\qquad(0\le j\le i\le m-2),
\]
\[
(52i+1,52j+36)
\qquad(0\le i\le j\le m-2),
\]
\[
(52i+36,52i+36)
\qquad(0\le i\le m-2),
\]
and the single napkin
\[
(1,1).
\]
Inspect the board in $52\times52$ residue blocks. The construction covers every cell. A direct block count gives the following distribution:
\[
2SHm=2\cdot35\cdot17\cdot39=46410
\]
cells are covered twice,
\[
H^2(m-2)=17^2\cdot37=10693
\]
cells are covered three times, and
\[
H^2=17^2=289
\]
cells are covered four times, where $S=35$ and $H=17$. All remaining cells are covered exactly once. Thus the number not covered exactly once is
\[
2SHm+H^2(m-1)
=(52^2-35^2)m-17^2
=57392.
\]
Hence $3986729$ cells contain the same positive number, namely $1$.

It remains to prove the upper bound. Fix any configuration and any positive integer $M$. Let $g$ be the number of cells whose entry is different from $M$. We prove
\[
g\ge57392.
\tag{1}
\]
A \emph{line} means a row or a column.

Consider a line $\ell$, and let
\[
a_1,a_2,\ldots,a_{52m-17}
\]
be its consecutive entries. For each residue $r\in\{1,\ldots,52\}$, set
\[
s_r=\sum_{t\equiv r\pmod{52}}a_t.
\]
Every napkin meeting $\ell$ contributes exactly $1$ to each of the $52$ sums, so
\[
s_1=s_2=\cdots=s_{52}=s,
\tag{2}
\]
where $s$ is the number of napkins meeting $\ell$.

The sums $s_1,\ldots,s_{35}$ have $m$ terms each, whereas $s_{36},\ldots,s_{52}$ have $m-1$ terms each. Call $\ell$ \emph{rich} if
\[
s>(m-1)M
\]
and \emph{poor} otherwise.

If $\ell$ is rich, then each of the $17$ short sums contains a term greater than $M$; mark one such cell for each short residue. Thus a rich line has at least $17$ marked bad cells. If $\ell$ is poor, then $s<mM$, so each of the $35$ long sums contains a term smaller than $M$; mark one such cell for each long residue. Thus a poor line has at least $35$ marked bad cells. Every marked cell has entry different from $M$.

Call an index \emph{small} if it is congruent modulo $52$ to one of
\[
1,2,\ldots,35,
\]
and \emph{big} otherwise. A line is small or big according to its index. A rich line has its marked cells in big perpendicular lines, while a poor line has its marked cells in small perpendicular lines.

Put one strawberry on every cell marked for each of its two lines. In addition, on every small rich line put one extra strawberry on each of its marked cells. A cell receiving an extra strawberry has entry greater than $M$ and lies at the intersection of a small rich line and a big perpendicular line. It is not marked by the perpendicular line: if that perpendicular line is poor, it marks only entries smaller than $M$, and if it is rich, it marks only cells on big parallel indices. Thus every cell receives at most two strawberries. Since only cells counted by $g$ receive strawberries, the total number $N$ of strawberries satisfies
\[
N\le2g.
\tag{3}
\]

There are $35m$ small rows and $35m$ small columns. Every small line receives at least $34$ strawberries: a rich one has at least $17$ marked cells, each counted twice, and a poor one has at least $35$. There are $17(m-1)$ big rows and the same number of big columns, and every big line receives at least $17$ strawberries. Hence
\[
\begin{aligned}
N
&\ge2\bigl(35m\cdot34+17(m-1)\cdot17\bigr)\\
&=2(1479m-289)\\
&=2\cdot57392.
\end{aligned}
\]
Together with (3), this proves (1). Thus at most
\[
2011^2-57392=3986729
\]
cells can contain any fixed nonzero number, and the construction attains this bound.

SOLUTION 2:
We retain the construction from Solution 1 and give another proof of the upper bound. Put
\[
S=35,
\qquad
H=17,
\qquad
m=39.
\]
Then
\[
52=S+H,
\qquad
2011=Sm+H(m-1).
\]
Fix a positive integer $M$ and call a cell \emph{vicious} if its entry differs from $M$. We prove that the total number $g$ of vicious cells satisfies
\[
g\ge H^2(m-1)+2SHm=57392.
\tag{1}
\]

Call the residues $1,\ldots,S$ modulo $S+H$ small and the remaining $H$ residues big. For residues $i,j$, let $v_{ij}$ be the number of vicious cells whose row has residue $i$ and whose column has residue $j$. Let
\[
a=\sum_{s,s'\text{ small}}v_{ss'},
\qquad
b=\sum_{s\text{ small},\,h\text{ big}}v_{sh},
\]
\[
c=\sum_{h\text{ big},\,s\text{ small}}v_{hs},
\qquad
d=\sum_{h,h'\text{ big}}v_{hh'}.
\]
Thus
\[
g=a+b+c+d.
\]

\textbf{Claim 1.}
\[
m\le\frac{a}{S^2}+\frac{b+c}{2SH}.
\tag{2}
\]
Consider a small row. Let $\alpha$ and $\beta$ be the numbers of vicious cells in that row lying in small and big columns, respectively. The residue-sum argument from Solution 1 shows that either there is a vicious cell in every small column residue, giving $\alpha\ge S$, or there is one in every big column residue, giving $\beta\ge H$. Hence
\[
\frac{\alpha}{S}+\frac{\beta}{H}\ge1.
\]
Summing over the $mS$ small rows gives
\[
\frac{a}{S}+\frac{b}{H}\ge mS.
\]
Interchanging rows and columns gives
\[
\frac{a}{S}+\frac{c}{H}\ge mS.
\]
Adding and dividing by $2S$ proves (2).

\textbf{Claim 2.} For any small residues $s,s'$ and big residues $h,h'$,
\[
2m-1\le v_{ss'}+v_{sh'}+v_{hh'}.
\tag{3}
\]
Every $52\times52$ napkin contains exactly one cell of each ordered residue type modulo $52$. Remove all napkins that cover a vicious cell of type $(s,s')$. Among the $m^2$ cells of this type, the remaining $m^2-v_{ss'}$ cells each had entry $M$. Thus exactly
\[
(m^2-v_{ss'})M
\]
napkins remain. Write in red, in every cell, the number of remaining napkins covering it.

In one dimension, a fixed coordinate with a big residue can belong to a length-$52$ interval together with at most two coordinates having any prescribed small residue: one may lie in the adjacent residue block on either side. Therefore, in two dimensions, an $(h,h')$-cell can share a napkin with at most $2\cdot2=4$ cells of type $(s,s')$. For an $(s,h')$-cell, one coordinate residue is already fixed in the same class, so it can share a napkin with at most two cells of type $(s,s')$ and at most two cells of type $(h,h')$. Consequently, every red number in an $(h,h')$-cell is at most $4M$, and every red number in an $(s,h')$-cell is at most $2M$.

Let $x,y,z$ be the numbers of $(h,h')$-cells whose red entries lie in
\[
(M,2M],
\qquad
(2M,3M],
\qquad
(3M,4M],
\]
respectively. All these cells are vicious, so
\[
x+y+z\le v_{hh'}.
\tag{4}
\]
There are $(m-1)^2$ cells of type $(h,h')$, and every remaining napkin covers exactly one of them. Therefore
\[
\begin{aligned}
(m^2-v_{ss'})M
&\le\bigl((m-1)^2-x-y-z\bigr)M\\
&\quad+2xM+3yM+4zM.
\end{aligned}
\]
After simplification,
\[
2m-1\le v_{ss'}+x+2y+3z.
\tag{5}
\]

Say that an $(h,h')$-cell $\delta$ \emph{forces} an $(s,h')$-cell $\beta$ if more than $M$ remaining napkins cover both. Each $(s,h')$-cell is forced by at most one $(h,h')$-cell, because its red entry is at most $2M$ and the sets of napkins associated with distinct $(h,h')$-cells are disjoint.

An $(h,h')$-cell with red entry greater than $2M$ forces at least one of its at most two $(s,h')$ neighbors. If its red entry is greater than $3M$, it forces both: otherwise the two overlap counts would sum to at most $M+2M=3M$. Hence the $y$ cells of the second kind and the $z$ cells of the third kind force at least
\[
y+2z
\]
distinct vicious $(s,h')$-cells. Therefore
\[
y+2z\le v_{sh'}.
\tag{6}
\]
Combining (4)--(6) yields (3).

\textbf{Claim 3.}
\[
2m-1
\le
\frac{a}{S^2}
+\frac{b+c}{2SH}
+\frac{d}{H^2}.
\tag{7}
\]
Average (3) over all $S^2H^2$ choices of $(s,s',h,h')$. This gives
\[
2m-1\le\frac{a}{S^2}+\frac{b}{SH}+\frac{d}{H^2}.
\]
Interchanging rows and columns gives the same inequality with $b$ replaced by $c$. Averaging the two proves (7).

Multiply (7) by $H^2$, multiply (2) by $2SH-H^2$, and add. The left side becomes
\[
H^2(2m-1)+(2SH-H^2)m
=H^2(m-1)+2SHm.
\]
The right side is
\[
\frac{2H}{S}a+b+c+d.
\]
Since
\[
2H=34\le35=S,
\]
this is at most
\[
a+b+c+d=g.
\]
Thus (1) follows, proving the same upper bound as in Solution 1. Therefore the largest possible value is
\[
\boxed{3986729}.
\]
