ID: 0199

SOURCE: IMO Shortlist

YEAR: 2016

TOPIC: Number Theory

SUBTOPICS: Digit Sums, Integer Polynomials, Decimal Expansions, Asymptotic Growth

STATEMENT:
For a positive integer $k$, let $S(k)$ denote the sum of the digits in its decimal representation. Find all polynomials $P(x)$ with integer coefficients such that, for every positive integer $n\ge2016$, the integer $P(n)$ is positive and
\[
S(P(n))=P(S(n)).
\]

SOLUTION 1:
The complete list is
\[
\boxed{P(x)=x}
\]
and
\[
\boxed{P(x)=c\quad\text{for an integer }1\le c\le9.}
\]

We consider three cases.

\textbf{Case 1: $P$ is constant.}

Write $P(x)=c$. Since $P(n)>0$, we have $c>0$, and the equation becomes
\[
S(c)=c.
\]
This holds exactly for
\[
1\le c\le9.
\]

\textbf{Case 2: $\deg P=1$.}

Write
\[
P(x)=ax+b,
\qquad a,b\in\mathbb Z,\quad a\ne0.
\]
Since $P(n)>0$ for all sufficiently large $n$, we have $a\ge1$.

For positive integers $u,v$,
\[
S(u+v)\le S(u)+S(v).
\tag{1}
\]
Using $n=2025$ and $n=2020$ in the required identity gives
\[
S(2025a+b)-S(2020a+b)=5a.
\tag{2}
\]
On the other hand, by (1),
\[
S(2025a+b)
=S((2020a+b)+5a)
\le S(2020a+b)+S(5a).
\]
Together with (2), this yields
\[
5a\le S(5a)\le5a.
\]
Equality $S(5a)=5a$ is possible only when $5a$ is a one-digit positive integer. Hence
\[
a=1.
\]

We are left with
\[
S(n+b)=S(n)+b
\qquad(n\ge2016).
\tag{3}
\]
Applying (3) to $n$ and $n+1$ and subtracting,
\[
S(n+1+b)-S(n+b)=S(n+1)-S(n).
\tag{4}
\]

Suppose first that $b>0$. Choose a sufficiently large $k$ and put
\[
n=10^k-b-1.
\]
Then
\[
n+b=10^k-1,
\qquad
n+1+b=10^k,
\]
so the left side of (4) is
\[
1-9k.
\]
But $n<10^k-1$, and therefore
\[
S(n)\le9k-1,
\qquad
S(n+1)\ge1.
\]
Thus the right side of (4) is at least
\[
2-9k,
\]
a contradiction.

Now suppose that $b<0$, say $b=-c$ with $c>0$. Choose a sufficiently large $k$ and put
\[
n=10^k-1.
\]
The right side of (4) is
\[
1-9k.
\]
Meanwhile,
\[
n+b=10^k-c-1<10^k-1,
\]
so
\[
S(n+b)\le9k-1,
\qquad
S(n+1+b)\ge1.
\]
The left side is therefore at least $2-9k$, again a contradiction.

Hence $b=0$, and the only linear solution is
\[
P(x)=x.
\]

\textbf{Case 3: $\deg P=d\ge2$.}

Let the leading coefficient be $a_d>0$. Take
\[
n=10^k-1.
\]
Then
\[
S(n)=9k,
\]
and hence
\[
S(P(10^k-1))=P(9k).
\tag{5}
\]
Since
\[
P(10^k-1)=O(10^{dk}),
\]
the number of its decimal digits is $O(k)$, and consequently
\[
S(P(10^k-1))=O(k).
\]
However,
\[
P(9k)=\Theta(k^d),
\]
which cannot equal $O(k)$ for $d\ge2$ and large $k$. Thus this case is impossible.

All listed polynomials are readily checked to satisfy the condition.

SOLUTION 2:
Write
\[
P(x)=a_dx^d+a_{d-1}x^{d-1}+\cdots+a_0,
\qquad a_d>0.
\]
Choose an integer $m\ge1$ such that
\[
|a_i|9^i<10^m
\qquad(0\le i\le d).
\]
For sufficiently large $k$, put
\[
n=9\cdot10^k.
\]
Then
\[
S(n)=9
\]
and
\[
P(n)=\sum_{i=0}^d a_i9^i10^{ik}.
\tag{1}
\]

We first show that
\[
a_i\ge0
\qquad(0\le i<d).
\tag{2}
\]
Suppose otherwise, and choose the largest index $i<d$ with $a_i<0$. In the decimal expansion of the sum in (1), the subtraction contributed by the block
\[
a_i9^i10^{ik}
\]
forces a borrow from a higher block. Because the blocks are separated by $k$ positions and each fixed coefficient block has fewer than $m+1$ digits, this produces at least
\[
k-m-1
\]
consecutive digits equal to $9$. Therefore
\[
S(P(n))\ge9(k-m-1).
\]
But the required identity gives
\[
S(P(n))=P(S(n))=P(9),
\]
which is independent of $k$. This contradiction proves (2).

For large $k$, the nonnegative blocks in (1) are separated far enough that there are no carries between them. Hence
\[
S(P(n))
=
\sum_{i=0}^d S(a_i9^i).
\]
Since $S(n)=9$, we obtain
\[
\sum_{i=0}^d S(a_i9^i)
=P(9)
=\sum_{i=0}^d a_i9^i.
\tag{3}
\]
For every nonnegative integer $u$,
\[
S(u)\le u,
\]
with equality only for $u=0$ or $1\le u\le9$. Thus equality in (3) forces every nonzero number $a_i9^i$ to lie between $1$ and $9$.

It follows immediately that
\[
a_i=0\qquad(i\ge2).
\]
If $a_1\ne0$, then $a_1=1$. Hence the only remaining possibilities are
\[
P(x)=a_0
\quad\text{or}\quad
P(x)=x+a_0,
\qquad 0\le a_0\le9.
\]

The zero polynomial is excluded by positivity. A constant works exactly when
\[
1\le a_0\le9.
\]
If
\[
P(x)=x+a_0
\]
with $1\le a_0\le9$, choose a sufficiently large $k$ and set
\[
n=10^k+(10-a_0).
\]
Then
\[
S(P(n))
=S(10^k+10)=2,
\]
whereas
\[
P(S(n))
=P(11-a_0)=11.
\]
This is impossible. Therefore $a_0=0$, giving
\[
P(x)=x.
\]

Thus the solutions are precisely the identity polynomial and the constants $1,2,\ldots,9$.
