ID: 0055

SOURCE: IMO Shortlist

YEAR: 2011

TOPIC: Number Theory

SUBTOPICS: Divisibility Functions, Induction, Finite Image

STATEMENT:
Let
\[
f:\mathbb Z\to\mathbb Z_{>0}
\]
be a function such that, for every pair of integers $m,n$,
\[
f(m-n)\mid f(m)-f(n).
\tag{1}
\]
Prove that whenever
\[
f(m)\le f(n),
\]
one has
\[
f(m)\mid f(n).
\]

SOLUTION 1:
Suppose first that
\[
f(x)<f(y).
\]
We prove that
\[
f(x)\mid f(y).
\]

From (1), applied to $m=x$ and $n=y$,
\[
f(x-y)\mid f(x)-f(y).
\]
Since $f(x-y)>0$,
\[
f(x-y)\le f(y)-f(x)<f(y).
\tag{2}
\]

Put
\[
d=f(x)-f(x-y).
\]
The inequalities in (2) imply
\[
-f(y)<-f(x-y)<d<f(x)<f(y),
\]
and hence
\[
|d|<f(y).
\tag{3}
\]

Now apply (1) with $m=x$ and $n=x-y$. Since
\[
m-n=y,
\]
we get
\[
f(y)\mid f(x)-f(x-y)=d.
\]
By (3), this is possible only if
\[
d=0.
\]
Thus
\[
f(x)=f(x-y).
\tag{4}
\]

Returning to (1) with $m=x$ and $n=y$, relation (4) gives
\[
f(x)=f(x-y)\mid f(x)-f(y).
\]
Therefore
\[
f(x)\mid f(y).
\]

If $f(x)=f(y)$, the divisibility is immediate. Hence, whenever $f(m)\le f(n)$,
\[
f(m)\mid f(n).
\]

SOLUTION 2:
We prove the result through several claims.

\textbf{Claim 1.} For all integers $m,n$,
\[
f(n)\mid f(mn).
\tag{2}
\]

For every integer $r$, condition (1) gives
\[
f(n)=f\bigl((r+1)n-rn\bigr)
\mid f((r+1)n)-f(rn).
\]
Starting with the trivial divisibility $f(n)\mid f(n)$ and proceeding by induction in both directions on $r$, we obtain (2).

\textbf{Claim 2.} For every integer $n$,
\[
f(n)\mid f(0)
\qquad\text{and}\qquad
f(n)=f(-n).
\tag{3}
\]

The first assertion follows from Claim 1 by taking $m=0$. Taking $m=-1$ in Claim 1 gives
\[
f(n)\mid f(-n).
\]
Replacing $n$ by $-n$ gives the reverse divisibility. Since both values are positive, they are equal.

Claim 1 with $n=1$ shows that
\[
f(1)\mid f(m)
\qquad(m\in\mathbb Z).
\tag{4}
\]
Thus $f(1)$ is the least value attained by $f$. By (3), every value of $f$ divides the fixed positive integer $f(0)$, so $f$ takes only finitely many values.

We now use induction on the number $N_f$ of distinct values attained by $f$.

If $N_f\le2$, the conclusion is immediate. Indeed, if $f(0)=f(1)$, then (3) and (4) force $f$ to be constant. Otherwise the two values are $f(1)$ and $f(0)$, with
\[
f(1)\mid f(0),
\]
so the required assertion holds.

Assume now that
\[
N_f\ge3
\]
and that the result has been proved for every function with fewer than $N_f$ values.

Let $a$ be the least positive integer satisfying
\[
f(a)>f(1).
\tag{5}
\]
Such an $a$ exists: otherwise, by the symmetry $f(n)=f(-n)$, the function would take at most the two values $f(1)$ and $f(0)$, contradicting $N_f\ge3$.

\textbf{Claim 3.} For every integer $n$,
\[
f(n)\ne f(1)
\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad
a\mid n.
\tag{6}
\]

We first prove that
\[
f(n)=f(1)
\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad
f(n+a)=f(1).
\tag{7}
\]

Suppose $f(n)=f(1)$. Applying (1) to the pair $(a,-n)$ gives
\[
f(n+a)\mid f(a)-f(-n)=f(a)-f(n)>0.
\]
Hence
\[
f(n+a)\le f(a)-f(n)<f(a).
\tag{8}
\]
On the other hand, applying (1) to $(n+a,n)$ gives
\[
f(a)\mid f(n+a)-f(n).
\tag{9}
\]
The difference in (9) is nonnegative because $f(n)=f(1)$ is the minimum value of $f$, and by (8) it is strictly smaller than $f(a)$. Therefore
\[
f(n+a)-f(n)=0,
\]
so
\[
f(n+a)=f(1).
\]

Conversely, if $f(n+a)=f(1)$, then by Claim 2,
\[
f(-n-a)=f(1).
\]
Applying the implication already proved to $-n-a$ gives
\[
f(-n)=f(1),
\]
and Claim 2 yields $f(n)=f(1)$. Thus (7) holds.

By the definition of $a$,
\[
f(1)=f(2)=\cdots=f(a-1)<f(a).
\]
Also, $f(0)\ne f(1)$; otherwise (3) and (4) would make $f$ constant. Using the periodicity (7), we conclude that $f(n)=f(1)$ exactly when $a\nmid n$. This proves (6).

Now take arbitrary integers $m,n$ with
\[
f(m)\le f(n).
\]

If $a\nmid m$, then Claim 3 gives
\[
f(m)=f(1),
\]
and the desired divisibility follows from (4).

Suppose instead that
\[
a\mid m.
\]
Then $f(m)>f(1)$ by Claim 3, so the inequality $f(m)\le f(n)$ implies
\[
f(n)>f(1),
\]
and hence
\[
a\mid n.
\]

Define
\[
g(x)=f(ax).
\]
The function $g$ satisfies the same condition:
\[
g(u-v)=f(a(u-v))\mid f(au)-f(av)=g(u)-g(v).
\]
By Claim 3, $g$ never takes the value $f(1)$, so
\[
N_g<N_f.
\]
The induction hypothesis applied to $g$ gives
\[
g(m/a)\mid g(n/a).
\]
That is,
\[
f(m)\mid f(n).
\]

This completes the induction and the proof.
