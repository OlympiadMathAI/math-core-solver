ID: 0184

SOURCE: IMO Shortlist

YEAR: 2016

TOPIC: Combinatorics

SUBTOPICS: Divisors, Double Counting, Extremal Arguments, Divisor Functions

STATEMENT:
Find all positive integers $n$ for which all positive divisors of $n$ can be placed into the cells of a rectangular table so that:

\begin{itemize}
\item each cell contains a distinct divisor of $n$;
\item all row sums are equal; and
\item all column sums are equal.
\end{itemize}

SOLUTION 1:
The only possible value is
\[
\boxed{n=1}.
\]

Clearly $n=1$ works. Suppose now that $n>1$, and assume that its divisors are arranged in a $k\times \ell$ table. Interchanging rows and columns if necessary, we may suppose
\[
k\le\ell.
\]
The case $k=1$ is impossible, since then the columns contain single, distinct divisors but are required to have equal sums. Thus $k\ge2$.

Let the common column sum be $s$. The column containing $n$ contains at least one other positive divisor, so
\[
s>n.
\tag{1}
\]

For each column, let its largest entry be $d_j$, and order the columns so that
\[
d_1>d_2>\cdots>d_\ell.
\]
The numbers
\[
\frac n{d_1},\frac n{d_2},\ldots,\frac n{d_\ell}
\]
are distinct positive integers in increasing order. Hence
\[
\frac n{d_\ell}\ge\ell,
\qquad\text{so}\qquad
d_\ell\le\frac n\ell.
\tag{2}
\]

On the other hand, $d_\ell$ is at least the average entry in its column, and therefore
\[
d_\ell\ge\frac sk>\frac nk
\tag{3}
\]
by (1). Combining (2) and (3) gives
\[
\frac n\ell>\frac nk,
\]
hence $k>\ell$, contradicting $k\le\ell$.

Therefore no $n>1$ works, and the answer is $n=1$.

SOLUTION 2:
Again, $n=1$ clearly works. Suppose that $n>1$, with prime factorization
\[
n=p_1^{r_1}p_2^{r_2}\cdots p_t^{r_t},
\]
and suppose its divisors fill a $k\times\ell$ table with
\[
1<k\le\ell.
\]

Let $\tau(n)$ and $\sigma(n)$ denote the number and the sum of the positive divisors of $n$, respectively. Then
\[
k\ell=\tau(n)
=(r_1+1)(r_2+1)\cdots(r_t+1).
\]
Every column has sum $\sigma(n)/\ell$. Since the column containing $n$ has at least two positive entries,
\[
\frac{\sigma(n)}{\ell}>n,
\qquad\text{so}\qquad
\ell<\frac{\sigma(n)}n.
\]
Consequently,
\[
\tau(n)=k\ell\le\ell^2<
\left(\frac{\sigma(n)}n\right)^2.
\tag{1}
\]

For a prime $p$ and a positive integer $r$, define
\[
F(p,r)
=
\frac{r+1}
{\left(1+\frac1p+\cdots+\frac1{p^r}\right)^2}
=
\frac{(r+1)\left(1-\frac1p\right)^2}
{\left(1-\frac1{p^{r+1}}\right)^2}.
\]
Inequality (1) is equivalent to
\[
\prod_{i=1}^t F(p_i,r_i)<1.
\tag{2}
\]

We have
\[
F(2,1)=\frac89,
\qquad
F(2,2)=\frac{48}{49},
\qquad
F(3,1)=\frac98.
\]
Also,
\[
F(2,r)>1\quad(r\ge3),
\]
because $(r+1)/4\ge1$ and the denominator
\[
\left(1-\frac1{2^{r+1}}\right)^2
\]
is less than $1$. Similarly,
\[
F(3,r)>\frac43>\frac98
\quad(r\ge2),
\]
and
\[
F(p,r)>\frac{32}{25}>\frac98
\quad(p\ge5,\ r\ge1).
\]

Thus every factor in (2) exceeds $1$, except possibly $F(2,1)$ and $F(2,2)$. Moreover, multiplying either exceptional factor by any other possible factor gives a number at least $1$. Hence (2) can hold only when
\[
n=2
\quad\text{or}\quad
n=4.
\]
For these values, the numbers of divisors are $2$ and $3$, respectively, so any rectangular table has only one row or one column; its distinct entries cannot have equal sums in the other direction. Thus neither value works.

Therefore the unique answer is
\[
\boxed{1}.
\]
