ID: 0062

SOURCE: IMO Shortlist

YEAR: 2012

TOPIC: Algebra

SUBTOPICS: Polynomials, Rational Roots, Gauss's Lemma, Vieta's Formulas

STATEMENT:
Let $f$ and $g$ be two nonzero polynomials with integer coefficients and
\[
\deg f>\deg g.
\]
Suppose that for infinitely many primes $p$, the polynomial $pf+g$ has a rational root. Prove that $f$ has a rational root.

SOLUTION 1:
Because $\deg f>\deg g$, there exists $R>0$ such that
\[
\left|\frac{g(x)}{f(x)}\right|<1
\qquad\text{whenever }|x|>R.
\]
Therefore, for every prime $p$ and every $|x|>R$,
\[
|pf(x)+g(x)|
\ge |f(x)|\left(p-\frac{|g(x)|}{|f(x)|}\right)>0.
\]
Thus every real root of every polynomial $pf+g$ lies in the fixed interval $[-R,R]$.

Write
\[
f(x)=a_nx^n+a_{n-1}x^{n-1}+\cdots+a_0,
\qquad
g(x)=b_mx^m+b_{m-1}x^{m-1}+\cdots+b_0,
\]
where $n>m$. Replacing $f(x)$ and $g(x)$ by
\[
a_n^{n-1}f\left(\frac{x}{a_n}\right)
\quad\text{and}\quad
a_n^{n-1}g\left(\frac{x}{a_n}\right),
\]
respectively, preserves integer coefficients and reduces the problem to the case in which $f$ is monic. Renaming the transformed polynomials as $f,g$ and choosing a corresponding uniform root bound again denoted by $R$, we therefore assume from now on that $a_n=1$.

For a prime $p$ under consideration, let
\[
r=\frac{u}{v}
\]
be a rational root of $pf+g$, written in lowest terms with $v>0$. Since the leading coefficient of $pf+g$ is $p$, the rational root theorem gives
\[
v=1
\quad\text{or}\quad
v=p.
\]
At least one of these two cases occurs for infinitely many primes.

\textbf{Case 1: $v=1$ infinitely often.}
Then $r=u$ is an integer and $|u|\le R$, so only finitely many values of $u$ are possible. Hence there are distinct primes $p$ and $q$ for which the same integer $u$ is a root of both $pf+g$ and $qf+g$. Subtracting the two equations gives
\[
(p-q)f(u)=0,
\]
so $f(u)=0$. Thus $f$ has an integer root.

\textbf{Case 2: $v=p$ infinitely often.}
For such a prime, write $r=u/p$ with $p\nmid u$. Comparing the powers of $p$ in the denominators of
\[
pf\left(\frac{u}{p}\right)+g\left(\frac{u}{p}\right)=0
\]
shows that $m=n-1$: if $m<n-1$, multiplying by $p^{n-1}$ and reducing modulo $p$ would give $u^n\equiv 0\pmod p$, impossible.

Thus the equation becomes
\[
\begin{aligned}
&\left(u^n+a_{n-1}pu^{n-1}+\cdots+a_0p^n\right)\\
&\qquad+
\left(b_{n-1}u^{n-1}+b_{n-2}pu^{n-2}+\cdots+b_0p^{n-1}\right)=0.
\end{aligned}
\]
Reducing modulo $p$ gives
\[
u^{n-1}(u+b_{n-1})\equiv 0\pmod p.
\]
Since $p\nmid u$, there is an integer $k$ such that
\[
u+b_{n-1}=pk.
\]
Because $|u/p|\le R$,
\[
|k|
\le R+\frac{|b_{n-1}|}{p}
\le R+|b_{n-1}|.
\]
Hence only finitely many integers $k$ are possible. Passing to an infinite subsequence, we may assume that the same $k$ occurs for infinitely many primes $p$. For those primes,
\[
r=k-\frac{b_{n-1}}{p}
\]
and
\[
f\left(k-\frac{b_{n-1}}{p}\right)
+\frac1p g\left(k-\frac{b_{n-1}}{p}\right)=0.
\]
Consequently, the polynomial
\[
F(x)=f(k-b_{n-1}x)+xg(k-b_{n-1}x)
\]
has infinitely many roots of the form $x=1/p$. Hence $F$ is identically zero. Substituting $x=0$ gives
\[
f(k)=0.
\]
Thus $f$ has an integer root in the normalized problem, and therefore the original polynomial $f$ has a rational root.

SOLUTION 2:
If $\deg f=1$, the conclusion is immediate, so assume $\deg f=n\ge 2$. After discarding finitely many primes, we may also assume that no prime under consideration divides the leading coefficient of $f$.

There is a constant $R$ such that every complex root of every polynomial $pf+g$ lies in the disk
\[
|z|\le R.
\]
For instance, divide $pf+g$ by $p$ and apply a uniform Cauchy root bound to the family $f+p^{-1}g$.

For each prime $p$ for which $pf+g$ has a rational root, Gauss's lemma gives a factorization in $\mathbb{Z}[x]$ into a linear factor and a factor of degree $n-1$. The product of their leading coefficients is $p$ times the leading coefficient of $f$. Since $p$ does not divide that fixed leading coefficient, one of the two factors has leading coefficient dividing the leading coefficient of $f$. Denote that factor by $h_p$.

There are only two possibilities for $\deg h_p$, namely $1$ and $n-1$, and only finitely many possibilities for its leading coefficient. Restricting to an infinite set of primes, we may assume that all $h_p$ have the same degree and the same leading coefficient.

Every root of $h_p$ is a root of $pf+g$, so all roots of $h_p$ lie in $|z|\le R$. By Vieta's formulas, the coefficients of the polynomials $h_p$ are then uniformly bounded. Since these coefficients are integers, only finitely many such polynomials are possible. Hence one polynomial $h$ occurs as $h_p$ for infinitely many primes $p$.

Choose distinct such primes $p$ and $q$. Then
\[
h\mid pf+g
\quad\text{and}\quad
h\mid qf+g,
\]
so, over $\mathbb{Q}[x]$,
\[
h\mid (p-q)f,
\]
and therefore $h\mid f$. If $\deg h=1$, then $f$ has a rational root. If $\deg h=n-1$, then the quotient $f/h$ is linear over $\mathbb{Q}$, so again $f$ has a rational root.

SOLUTION 3:
As in Solution 1, there exists $R>0$ such that all real roots of all polynomials $pf+g$ lie in $[-R,R]$.

Let
\[
p_1<p_2<\cdots
\]
be infinitely many primes for which $p_kf+g$ has a rational root $r_k$. The sequence $(r_k)$ is bounded, so after passing to a subsequence we may assume that it converges:
\[
r_k\to\alpha.
\]
On $[-R,R]$, the polynomial $g$ is bounded, say $|g(x)|\le M$. Since
\[
p_kf(r_k)+g(r_k)=0,
\]
we have
\[
|f(r_k)|=\frac{|g(r_k)|}{p_k}\le\frac{M}{p_k}\to 0.
\]
By continuity,
\[
f(\alpha)=0.
\]
It remains to prove that $\alpha$ is rational.

Write
\[
r_k=\frac{u_k}{v_k}
\]
in lowest terms, with $v_k>0$. Let $a$ be the leading coefficient of $f$, and let
\[
b=f(0),\qquad c=g(0).
\]
The rational root theorem gives
\[
v_k\mid p_ka,
\qquad
u_k\mid p_kb+c.
\tag{1}
\]
Discarding finitely many terms, assume $p_k\nmid a$. Then (1) implies that either
\[
v_k\mid a
\]
or
\[
v_k=p_kd_k
\quad\text{for some positive divisor }d_k\text{ of }|a|.
\]

If the first alternative occurs infinitely often, then the denominators $v_k$ are bounded. Passing to a further subsequence, one denominator is fixed; since the corresponding rational numbers converge, their integer numerators are eventually constant. Hence $\alpha$ is rational.

We may therefore assume that
\[
v_k=p_kd_k
\]
for all $k$ in the remaining subsequence. If $u_k=0$ infinitely often, then $\alpha=0$ and we are done. Also, if $b=c=0$, then $f(0)=0$ and the conclusion is immediate. Thus, after discarding at most one further term, we may assume that $u_k\ne0$ and $p_kb+c\ne0$.

Define the nonzero integer $e_k$ by
\[
p_kb+c=u_ke_k.
\]
Then
\[
\frac{u_k}{v_k}
=
\frac{p_kb+c}{p_kd_ke_k}
=
\frac{b}{d_ke_k}
+
\frac{c}{p_kd_ke_k}.
\]
The second term tends to $0$, so
\[
\frac{b}{d_ke_k}\to\alpha.
\]
If $b=0$, this already gives $\alpha=0$. If $b\ne0$, a convergent sequence of numbers of the form $b/q_k$ with nonzero integers $q_k$ has a rational limit: either the integers $q_k$ are bounded, in which case one value occurs infinitely often, or $|q_k|\to\infty$ along a subsequence, in which case the limit is $0$. Thus $\alpha\in\mathbb{Q}$.

We have shown that $\alpha$ is a rational root of $f$.
