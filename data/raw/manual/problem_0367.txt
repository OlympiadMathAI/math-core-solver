ID: 0367

SOURCE: IMO Shortlist

YEAR: 2022

TOPIC: Algebra

SUBTOPICS: Geometric Progressions, Difference Sets, Convexity, Extremal Exponents

STATEMENT:
Find all positive integers $n\ge2$ for which there exist real numbers
\[
a_1<a_2<\cdots<a_n
\]
and a real number $r>0$ such that the
\[
\binom n2
\]
differences
\[
a_j-a_i
\qquad(1\le i<j\le n)
\]
are, in some order, the numbers
\[
r,r^2,\ldots,r^{\binom n2}.
\]

SOLUTION 1:
The answer is
\[
\boxed{n\in\{2,3,4\}}.
\]

We first give constructions.

For $n=2$, take
\[
(a_1,a_2)=(1,3),
\qquad r=2.
\]

For $n=3$, let $r>1$ satisfy
\[
r^2-r-1=0
\]
and take
\[
(a_1,a_2,a_3)=(0,r,r+r^2).
\]
Then the three differences are
\[
r,
\qquad r^2,
\qquad r+r^2=r^3.
\]

For $n=4$, let $r\in(1,2)$ satisfy
\[
r^3-r-1=0
\]
and take
\[
(a_1,a_2,a_3,a_4)
=(0,r,r+r^2,r+r^2+r^3).
\]
The six differences are
\[
r,r^2,r^3,r^4,r^5,r^6,
\]
because
\[
r^4=r+r^2,
\qquad
r^5=r^2+r^3,
\qquad
r^6=r+r^2+r^3.
\]

We now prove that no $n\ge5$ works. Suppose otherwise, and put
\[
B=\binom n2.
\]
The case $r=1$ is impossible because the pairwise differences would all be equal. If $0<r<1$, multiply every $a_i$ by $r^{-B-1}$ and replace $r$ by $r^{-1}$; the new differences are again precisely the first $B$ powers of the new ratio. Hence we may assume
\[
r>1.
\]

\textbf{Lemma.}
\[
r^{n-1}>2.
\tag{1}
\]

Among the $n$ powers
\[
r,r^2,\ldots,r^n,
\]
at least one is not an adjacent difference $a_{i+1}-a_i$, because there are only $n-1$ adjacent differences. It is therefore equal to a difference $a_j-a_i$ with $j\ge i+2$, which is a sum of at least two distinct adjacent differences. Every adjacent difference is at least $r$, so this sum is strictly greater than $2r$. Thus
\[
r^n>2r,
\]
proving (1).

The largest difference is
\[
a_n-a_1=r^B.
\tag{2}
\]
For every $i=2,\ldots,n-1$, write
\[
r^B=(a_n-a_i)+(a_i-a_1).
\tag{3}
\]
One of the two terms on the right is at least $r^B/2$. By (1),
\[
r^{B-(n-1)}<\frac{r^B}{2}.
\]
Consequently, the larger term in each of the $n-2$ equations (3) must be one of
\[
r^{B-1},r^{B-2},\ldots,r^{B-(n-2)}.
\]
All pairwise differences are distinct, so these $n-2$ large powers occur exactly once.

After ordering the equations accordingly, write
\[
r^B=r^{B-i}+r^{\alpha_i}
\qquad(1\le i\le n-2),
\tag{4}
\]
where
\[
1\le\alpha_1<\alpha_2<\cdots<\alpha_{n-2}\le B-(n-1).
\tag{5}
\]
Subtracting consecutive equations in (4),
\[
r^{\alpha_{i+1}}-r^{\alpha_i}
=r^{B-i}-r^{B-i-1}.
\tag{6}
\]
The right-hand side strictly decreases as $i$ increases. Since the function $x\mapsto r^x$ is strictly increasing and strictly convex, (6) implies that the positive integers
\[
\alpha_{i+1}-\alpha_i
\]
strictly decrease with $i$.

The last of these differences is at least $2$. Indeed, if
\[
\alpha_{n-2}-\alpha_{n-3}=1,
\]
then the last instance of (6) would give
\[
r^{\alpha_{n-3}}(r-1)
=r^{B-(n-2)}(r-1),
\]
so
\[
\alpha_{n-3}=B-(n-2).
\]
But that exponent is already used by one of the large terms in (4), contradicting the fact that all differences are distinct.

Therefore
\[
\begin{aligned}
\alpha_{n-2}-\alpha_1
&\ge2+3+\cdots+(n-2)\\
&=\frac{n(n-3)}2.
\end{aligned}
\tag{7}
\]
On the other hand, (5) gives
\[
\alpha_{n-2}-\alpha_1
\le B-n
=\frac{n(n-3)}2.
\tag{8}
\]
Thus equality holds everywhere in (7)--(8). In particular,
\[
\alpha_{n-2}=B-(n-1)
\]
and
\[
\alpha_{n-3}=B-(n+1).
\]
The last two equations in (4) are therefore
\[
r^B=r^{B-(n-2)}+r^{B-(n-1)}
\]
and
\[
r^B=r^{B-(n-3)}+r^{B-(n+1)}.
\]
After division, these become
\[
r^{n-1}=r+1
\tag{9}
\]
and
\[
r^{n+1}=r^4+1.
\tag{10}
\]
Using (9) in (10),
\[
r^4+1=r^{n+1}=r^2(r+1)=r^3+r^2.
\]
Hence
\[
(r-1)(r^3-r-1)=0.
\]
Since $r>1$,
\[
r^3=r+1.
\]
Comparing this with (9) yields
\[
n-1=3,
\]
so $n=4$, contradicting $n\ge5$.

Thus precisely
\[
\boxed{n=2,3,4}
\]
are possible.
