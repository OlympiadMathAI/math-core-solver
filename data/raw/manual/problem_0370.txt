ID: 0370

SOURCE: IMO Shortlist

YEAR: 2022

TOPIC: Algebra

SUBTOPICS: Functional Sequences, Periodicity, Classification, Integer Products, Exponential Growth

STATEMENT:
For a positive integer $n$, an \emph{$n$-sequence} is a sequence
\[
(a_0,a_1,\ldots,a_n)
\]
of nonnegative integers satisfying the following condition: whenever $i,j$ are nonnegative integers with $i+j\le n$, one has
\[
a_i+a_j\le n
\qquad\text{and}\qquad
a_{a_i+a_j}=a_{i+j}.
\tag{1}
\]
Let $f(n)$ be the number of $n$-sequences. Prove that there are positive real constants $c_1,c_2,\lambda$ such that
\[
c_1\lambda^n<f(n)<c_2\lambda^n
\]
for every positive integer $n$.

SOLUTION 1:
We prove that the assertion holds with
\[
\boxed{\lambda=3^{1/6}}.
\]
In fact, we classify all $n$-sequences and then estimate their number.

Put
\[
k=\left\lfloor\frac n2\right\rfloor.
\]
Call an $n$-sequence \emph{large} if $a_i>k$ for some $i$, and \emph{small} otherwise. We first study a nonidentity sequence, meaning that $a_i\ne i$ for at least one index.

\textbf{Lemma 1.}
If
\[
a_r=a_s
\qquad\text{and}\qquad
r,s<n,
\]
then
\[
a_{r+1}=a_{s+1}.
\]

\textit{Proof.}
Using (1) with the pairs $(r,1)$ and $(s,1)$,
\[
a_{r+1}=a_{a_r+a_1}=a_{a_s+a_1}=a_{s+1}.
\]

\textbf{Lemma 2.}
If $i\le k$, then
\[
a_i\le k.
\]

\textit{Proof.}
Since $2i\le n$, condition (1) applied to $(i,i)$ gives
\[
2a_i\le n.
\]

\textbf{Lemma 3.}
There are distinct indices $r,s$ such that
\[
a_r=a_s.
\]

\textit{Proof.}
If $a_0\ne0$, condition (1) for $(0,0)$ gives
\[
a_{2a_0}=a_0,
\]
and $2a_0\ne0$. If $a_0=0$, then (1) for $(i,0)$ gives
\[
a_{a_i}=a_i.
\]
Choose $i$ with $a_i\ne i$; then the two distinct indices $i$ and $a_i$ have equal values.

Let $r$ be the smallest index for which
\[
a_s=a_r
\]
for some $s>r$, and let $d$ be the smallest positive integer satisfying
\[
a_{r+d}=a_r.
\]

\textbf{Lemma 4.}
The sequence has the following structure.

\begin{enumerate}
\item The tail
\[
a_r,a_{r+1},\ldots,a_n
\]
is periodic with minimal period $d$. More precisely, for $u<v$,
\[
a_u=a_v
\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad
u,v\ge r\ \text{ and }\ d\mid v-u.
\tag{2}
\]
\item One has
\[
a_i=i\quad(i<r),
\qquad
a_i\ge r\quad(i\ge r).
\tag{3}
\]
\end{enumerate}

\textit{Proof.}
Lemma 1 propagates the equality $a_r=a_{r+d}$ forward, so
\[
a_{r+t}=a_{r+d+t}
\]
whenever both indices are at most $n$. Thus the tail is periodic with period $d$.

Suppose that $a_u=a_v$ for $r\le u<v$. Reduce both indices modulo $d$ to indices
\[
r\le u_0,v_0<r+d.
\]
If $u_0\ne v_0$, say $u_0<v_0$, then Lemma 1 propagates
\[
a_{u_0}=a_{v_0}
\]
forward by $r+d-v_0$ places and gives
\[
a_{r+d+u_0-v_0}=a_{r+d}=a_r.
\]
The new index lies strictly between $r$ and $r+d$, contradicting the minimality of $d$. Hence $u_0=v_0$, proving (2). The minimality of $r$ excludes any other repeated value before $r$.

If $r>0$, the equality $a_{2a_0}=a_0$ from Lemma 3 forces $a_0=0$. Then
\[
a_{a_i}=a_i.
\]
For $i<r$, the minimality of $r$ forces $a_i=i$. If $i\ge r$ but $a_i<r$, the equality $a_{a_i}=a_i$ would create a repeated value beginning before $r$. This proves (3). The case $r=0$ is immediate.

We say that the sequence has \emph{period $d$ and offset $r$}.

\textbf{Lemma 5.}
Exactly one of the following alternatives holds:
\[
d\mid a_i-i
\qquad\text{for every }i,
\tag{4}
\]
or
\[
r=0,
\qquad d\text{ is even},
\qquad
d\mid a_i-i-\frac d2
\quad\text{for every }i.
\tag{5}
\]

\textit{Proof.}
Condition (1) with the pair $(i,0)$ gives
\[
a_{a_i+a_0}=a_i.
\]
By Lemma 4,
\[
d\mid a_i-i+a_0
\tag{6}
\]
for every $i$. Taking $i=0$ gives
\[
d\mid2a_0.
\]
If $d\mid a_0$, equation (6) yields (4). Otherwise $d$ is even and
\[
a_0\equiv\frac d2\pmod d,
\]
so (6) yields (5). In the latter case $r=0$, because Lemma 4 gives $a_0=0$ whenever $r>0$.

\textbf{Lemma 6.}
Every sequence satisfying the second alternative (5) is small.

\textit{Proof.}
If $d\le k+1$, then the period
\[
a_0,a_1,\ldots,a_{d-1}
\]
consists only of terms with indices at most $k$, so Lemma 2 makes the whole sequence small.

Now suppose $d>k+1$. Since $d$ is even and $d/2\le k$, Lemma 2 and (5) give
\[
d\mid a_{d/2},
\qquad
0\le a_{d/2}\le k<d,
\]
so
\[
a_{d/2}=0.
\tag{7}
\]
We prove by induction that $a_i\le k$ for all $i$. Lemma 2 handles $i\le k$. If $i>k$, then $i-d/2<i$, so the induction hypothesis and (7) give
\[
\begin{aligned}
a_i
&=a_{(i-d/2)+d/2}\\
&=a_{a_{i-d/2}+a_{d/2}}\\
&=a_{a_{i-d/2}}\\
&\le k,
\end{aligned}
\]
because the index $a_{i-d/2}$ is at most $k$ and Lemma 2 applies to it.

\textbf{Lemma 7.}
If the sequence is small, then
\[
r+d\le k+1.
\tag{8}
\]

\textit{Proof.}
The $k+2$ values
\[
a_0,a_1,\ldots,a_{k+1}
\]
all lie in the set $\{0,1,\ldots,k\}$. Hence two of them are equal, say $a_u=a_v$ with $u<v\le k+1$. Lemma 4 gives $u\ge r$ and $d\mid v-u$, so
\[
r+d\le u+d\le v\le k+1.
\]

\textbf{Lemma 8.}
If the sequence is large, then
\[
r+d>k+1
\tag{9}
\]
and
\[
a_i=i
\qquad(0\le i<r+d).
\tag{10}
\]

\textit{Proof.}
A value greater than $k$ occurs in the period
\[
a_r,\ldots,a_{r+d-1}.
\]
By Lemma 2, its index is greater than $k$, so $r+d-1>k$, proving (9).

Lemma 6 excludes alternative (5), so (4) holds. Lemma 4 already gives $a_i=i$ for $i<r$. For
\[
r\le i\le k,
\]
both $a_i$ and $i$ lie in $[r,k]$, while $d>k+1-r$. Since $d\mid a_i-i$, we must have $a_i=i$.

Now let $k<i<r+d$, and assume inductively that $a_j=j$ for every $j<i$. If $a_i<i$, then $a_i\ge r$ and $d\mid i-a_i$; periodicity gives
\[
a_{a_i}=a_i,
\]
which is a repetition at two indices less than $r+d$ whose positive difference is smaller than $d$, impossible. Hence $a_i\ge i$.

Since $n-i\le k$, we have
\[
a_{n-i}=n-i.
\]
The first part of condition (1), applied to $(i,n-i)$, gives
\[
a_i+n-i\le n,
\]
so $a_i\le i$. Therefore $a_i=i$, completing the induction.

We now count all $n$-sequences.

Large sequences are completely determined by a pair $(r,d)$ satisfying
\[
0\le r<n,
\qquad d\ge1,
\qquad k+1<r+d\le n.
\]
For such a pair, the sequence is
\[
a_i=i\quad(i<r+d)
\]
and is periodic with period $d$ from index $r$ onward. Conversely, every sequence of this form is an $n$-sequence. Indeed, if $i+j\le n$, at most one of $i,j$ can be at least $r+d$, because $2(r+d)>n$. If both are smaller, then $a_i+a_j=i+j$. If, say, $i\ge r+d$, then $a_j=j$, $a_i\le i$, and
\[
a_i+j\equiv i+j\pmod d.
\]
Both indices lie in the periodic part, so $a_{a_i+j}=a_{i+j}$, and also $a_i+j\le n$. The number of possible pairs is
\[
\sum_{s=k+2}^{n}s
=
\frac{(n-k-1)(n+k+2)}2.
\tag{11}
\]

For the small sequences, define, for positive integers $x,d$ with $d\le x$,
\[
g(x,d)=(p+1)^q p^{d-q},
\qquad
p=\left\lfloor\frac xd\right\rfloor,
\qquad
q=x-pd.
\tag{12}
\]
This is the product of the numbers of representatives of the $d$ residue classes in any interval of $x$ consecutive integers.

Under alternative (4), a small sequence has some offset $r$ and period $d$ with
\[
r+d\le k+1.
\]
Its period values must satisfy
\[
r\le a_j\le k,
\qquad
a_j\equiv j\pmod d
\qquad(r\le j<r+d).
\]
Conversely, any such choices define an $n$-sequence. All values are at most $k$, so their pairwise sums are at most $2k\le n$. Whenever at least one of $i,j$ is in the periodic part, the two indices $a_i+a_j$ and $i+j$ are at least $r$ and are congruent modulo $d$; if both indices are below $r$, the required identity is immediate. The congruence classes of the $d$ period values are all distinct, so the period is genuinely minimal and no sequence is counted with a smaller $d$. The values in the tail are at least $r$, so the offset is genuinely $r$. The number of choices is
\[
g(k+1-r,d).
\]

Under alternative (5), one has $r=0$, $d$ is even, and $d\le k+1$. The period values satisfy
\[
0\le a_j\le k,
\qquad
a_j\equiv j+\frac d2\pmod d.
\]
Again every such choice works: all values are at most $k$, and
\[
a_i+a_j\equiv i+j+d\equiv i+j\pmod d.
\]
Again the $d$ period values occupy distinct residue classes modulo $d$, so the minimal period is $d$. There are
\[
g(k+1,d)
\]
choices.

Including the identity sequence, we obtain the exact formula
\[
\begin{aligned}
f(n)
={}&1+
\frac{(n-k-1)(n+k+2)}2\\
&+\sum_{e=1}^{\lfloor(k+1)/2\rfloor}g(k+1,2e)\\
&+\sum_{r=0}^{k}\sum_{d=1}^{k+1-r}g(k+1-r,d).
\end{aligned}
\tag{13}
\]

We next derive the lower bound. For $n\ge4$, put
\[
x=k+1,
\qquad
d=\left\lfloor\frac{x}{3}\right\rfloor.
\]
Then $d\ge1$, and every factor in (12) is at least $3$, so
\[
f(n)>g(x,d)\ge3^d.
\]
Since
\[
d>\frac n6-1,
\]
we get
\[
f(n)>3^{n/6-1}=\frac13\left(3^{1/6}\right)^n.
\tag{14}
\]
For $n=1,2,3$, the trivial bound $f(n)\ge1$ is enough. Thus, for example,
\[
c_1=\frac14
\]
works for every positive integer $n$.

It remains to prove an upper bound. We use the following estimate.

\textbf{Lemma 9.}
For $1\le d\le x$,
\[
g(x,d)\le
\begin{cases}
\displaystyle
3^{x/3}\left(\frac{64}{81}\right)^{x/3-d},&d\le x/3,\\[3mm]
\displaystyle
3^{x/3}\left(\frac89\right)^{d-x/3},&d\ge x/3.
\end{cases}
\tag{15}
\]

\textit{Proof.}
The quantity $g(x,d)$ is also the largest possible product of $d$ positive integers whose sum is $x$: the product is maximized when the integers differ by at most $1$. Formula (12) also gives
\[
g(tx,td)=g(x,d)^t
\tag{16}
\]
for every positive integer $t$.

Suppose first that $d\le x/3$, and put
\[
h=x-3d\ge0.
\]
Take an optimal collection of $3d$ positive integers with sum $3x$ and adjoin $4h$ copies of $3$. This gives
\[
3^{4h}g(3x,3d)
\le
g(15x-36d,4x-9d).
\tag{17}
\]
The average on the right lies between $3$ and $4$, so
\[
g(15x-36d,4x-9d)=3^x4^{3h}.
\]
Using (16) in (17),
\[
g(x,d)^3
\le
3^x\left(\frac{64}{81}\right)^h.
\]
Taking cube roots proves the first estimate in (15).

Now suppose $d\ge x/3$, and put
\[
h=3d-x\ge0.
\]
Adjoin $2h$ copies of $3$ to an optimal collection for $g(3x,3d)$. Then
\[
3^{2h}g(3x,3d)
\le
g(18d-3x,9d-2x).
\tag{18}
\]
The average on the right lies between $2$ and $3$, so
\[
g(18d-3x,9d-2x)=2^{3h}3^x.
\]
Equations (16) and (18) give
\[
g(x,d)^3
\le
3^x\left(\frac89\right)^h,
\]
which yields the second estimate in (15).

The two geometric estimates in (15) imply
\[
\sum_{d=1}^{x}g(x,d)
<14\cdot3^{x/3}
\tag{19}
\]
for every positive integer $x$, because
\[
\frac1{1-64/81}+\frac1{1-8/9}<14.
\]

Using (13) and (19),
\[
f(n)
\le
3n^2+C\sum_{x=1}^{k+1}3^{x/3}
\le
3n^2+C'3^{(k+1)/3}
\tag{20}
\]
for absolute positive constants $C,C'$. Since
\[
k+1\le\frac n2+1,
\]
we have
\[
3^{(k+1)/3}
\le3^{1/3}\left(3^{1/6}\right)^n.
\]
Also, because $3^{1/6}>1$, the sequence
\[
\frac{n^2}{(3^{1/6})^n}
\]
is bounded. Hence (20) implies that there is a positive constant $c_2$ such that
\[
f(n)<c_2\left(3^{1/6}\right)^n
\]
for all positive integers $n$.

Together with the lower bound, this proves the assertion with
\[
\boxed{\lambda=3^{1/6}}.
\]
