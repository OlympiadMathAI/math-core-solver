ID: 0296

SOURCE: IMO Shortlist

YEAR: 2019

TOPIC: Number Theory

SUBTOPICS: Beatty Sequences, Diophantine Approximation, Pell Equations, Additive Combinatorics

STATEMENT:
Let
\[
H=\left\{\left\lfloor i\sqrt2\right\rfloor:i\in\mathbb Z_{>0}\right\}
=\{1,2,4,5,7,\ldots\}.
\]
Prove that there exists a positive constant $C$ such that, for every positive integer $n$, every set
\[
A\subseteq\{1,2,\ldots,n\}
\]
with
\[
|A|\ge C\sqrt n
\]
contains two elements $a,b$ satisfying
\[
a-b\in H.
\]

SOLUTION 1:
We prove the contrapositive. Suppose that no positive difference of two elements of $A$ belongs to $H$. We show that
\[
|A|<2\sqrt n.
\]

For a positive integer $r$,
\[
r\in H
\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad
\left\{\frac r{\sqrt2}\right\}>1-\frac1{\sqrt2},
\tag{1}
\]
where $\{x\}$ denotes the fractional part of $x$.

Indeed, $r\in H$ exactly when there is an integer $i$ satisfying
\[
r<i\sqrt2<r+1,
\]
or equivalently
\[
\frac r{\sqrt2}<i<\frac r{\sqrt2}+\frac1{\sqrt2}.
\]
This interval contains an integer exactly under condition (1).

Write
\[
A=\{a_1<a_2<\cdots<a_k\}.
\]
Translation does not change the differences, so we may assume
\[
0=a_1<a_2<\cdots<a_k<n.
\]
By (1),
\[
\left\{\frac{a_i}{\sqrt2}\right\}
<
1-\frac1{\sqrt2}
\qquad(i\ge2).
\tag{2}
\]

Moreover, these fractional parts are strictly increasing. Suppose that $i<j$ but
\[
\left\{\frac{a_j}{\sqrt2}\right\}
<
\left\{\frac{a_i}{\sqrt2}\right\}.
\]
Then, using (2),
\[
\left\{\frac{a_j-a_i}{\sqrt2}\right\}
=
1+
\left\{\frac{a_j}{\sqrt2}\right\}
-
\left\{\frac{a_i}{\sqrt2}\right\}
>
\frac1{\sqrt2}
>
1-\frac1{\sqrt2}.
\]
By (1), this would give
\[
a_j-a_i\in H,
\]
a contradiction.

Put
\[
d_i=a_{i+1}-a_i
\qquad(1\le i<k).
\]
Because the fractional parts are increasing,
\[
\left\{\frac{a_{i+1}}{\sqrt2}\right\}
-
\left\{\frac{a_i}{\sqrt2}\right\}
=
\left\{\frac{d_i}{\sqrt2}\right\}.
\]
Thus
\[
1-\frac1{\sqrt2}
>
\sum_{i=1}^{k-1}
\left\{\frac{d_i}{\sqrt2}\right\}.
\tag{3}
\]

For every positive integer $d$,
\[
\left\{\frac d{\sqrt2}\right\}
>
\frac1{2d\sqrt2}.
\tag{4}
\]
To prove this, let
\[
q=\left\lfloor\frac d{\sqrt2}\right\rfloor.
\]
Then
\[
\left\{\frac d{\sqrt2}\right\}
\left(
\frac d{\sqrt2}+q
\right)
=
\frac{d^2-2q^2}{2}
\ge\frac12.
\]
Since
\[
\frac d{\sqrt2}+q<\frac{2d}{\sqrt2},
\]
inequality (4) follows.

Applying (4) and Cauchy--Schwarz to (3),
\[
\begin{aligned}
1-\frac1{\sqrt2}
&>
\frac1{2\sqrt2}
\sum_{i=1}^{k-1}\frac1{d_i}\\
&\ge
\frac{(k-1)^2}
{2\sqrt2\sum_{i=1}^{k-1}d_i}\\
&>
\frac{(k-1)^2}{2\sqrt2\,n}.
\end{aligned}
\]
Therefore
\[
(k-1)^2<(2\sqrt2-2)n,
\]
and hence
\[
k<2\sqrt n.
\]
Thus one may take, for example,
\[
\boxed{C=2}.
\]

SOLUTION 2:
Let
\[
\alpha=2+\sqrt2.
\]
Since
\[
\frac1{\sqrt2}+\frac1\alpha=1,
\]
Beatty's theorem shows that
\[
J=\{\lfloor i\alpha\rfloor:i\in\mathbb Z_{>0}\}
\]
is the complementary Beatty sequence to $H$:
\[
H\cap J=\varnothing,
\qquad
H\cup J=\mathbb Z_{>0}.
\]

Suppose that no positive difference of two elements of
\[
A=\{a_1<a_2<\cdots<a_k\}
\]
belongs to $H$. Then every such difference belongs to $J$. Hence there are integers
\[
0=b_1<b_2<\cdots<b_k
\]
such that
\[
a_i-a_1=\lfloor\alpha b_i\rfloor.
\tag{1}
\]

For $j>i$, the difference
\[
\lfloor\alpha b_j\rfloor-\lfloor\alpha b_i\rfloor
\]
equals either
\[
\lfloor\alpha(b_j-b_i)\rfloor
\]
or that number plus $1$. Since $\alpha>2$, two consecutive positive integers cannot both lie in $J$. The difference belongs to $J$, so the second possibility is impossible. Therefore
\[
\lfloor\alpha b_j\rfloor-\lfloor\alpha b_i\rfloor
=
\lfloor\alpha(b_j-b_i)\rfloor.
\tag{2}
\]

Put
\[
d_i=b_{i+1}-b_i
\qquad(1\le i<k).
\]
Taking $i=1$ and $j=k$ in (2), and then summing the adjacent relations, gives
\[
\left\lfloor
\alpha\sum_{i=1}^{k-1}d_i
\right\rfloor
=
\sum_{i=1}^{k-1}\lfloor\alpha d_i\rfloor.
\]
Hence
\[
\sum_{i=1}^{k-1}\{\alpha d_i\}<1.
\tag{3}
\]
Since
\[
\{\alpha d\}=\{d\sqrt2\},
\]
and the same quadratic-irrationality calculation as in Solution 1 gives
\[
\{d\sqrt2\}>\frac1{2d\sqrt2},
\tag{4}
\]
equations (3)--(4) imply
\[
1>
\frac1{2\sqrt2}
\sum_{i=1}^{k-1}\frac1{d_i}
\ge
\frac{(k-1)^2}
{2\sqrt2\sum_{i=1}^{k-1}d_i}.
\tag{5}
\]

Furthermore,
\[
1+\left\lfloor
\alpha\sum_{i=1}^{k-1}d_i
\right\rfloor
=
1+a_k-a_1
\le a_k
\le n.
\]
Thus
\[
\sum_{i=1}^{k-1}d_i<\frac n\alpha.
\]
Substituting this into (5),
\[
(k-1)^2
<
\frac{2\sqrt2}{\alpha}\,n
=
(2\sqrt2-2)n.
\]
Therefore
\[
|A|<2\sqrt n,
\]
and again $C=2$ is sufficient.

SOLUTION 3:
Let
\[
J=\mathbb Z_{>0}\setminus H.
\]
Suppose that every positive difference between elements of $A$ lies in $J$.

We first make a general observation. Let
\[
B\subseteq\{1,2,\ldots,n\}
\]
be a set all of whose positive differences lie in $H$. Then
\[
|A|\,|B|\le2n.
\tag{1}
\]
Indeed, all sums
\[
a+b
\qquad(a\in A,\ b\in B)
\]
are distinct. Otherwise
\[
a_1+b_1=a_2+b_2
\]
would give
\[
|a_1-a_2|=|b_1-b_2|,
\]
with the left side in $J$ and the right side in $H$. The sums lie between $2$ and $2n$, proving (1).

It remains to construct a set $B$ of size at least a positive constant times $\sqrt n$ whose differences all lie in $H$.

Consider the negative Pell equation
\[
X^2-2Y^2=-1.
\tag{2}
\]
It has infinitely many positive solutions, and the $X$-coordinates satisfy
\[
X_1=1,\qquad X_2=7,\qquad
X_{r}=6X_{r-1}-X_{r-2}.
\]
The ratio of two consecutive $X$-coordinates is at most $7$. Hence, for every $n$, we can choose a solution with
\[
\frac{\sqrt n}{7}<X\le\sqrt n.
\tag{3}
\]

From (2),
\[
\frac X{\sqrt2}-Y
=
-\frac1{\sqrt2(X+\sqrt2Y)}.
\]
Because $\sqrt2Y>X$ and (3) holds,
\[
0>
\frac X{\sqrt2}-Y
>
-\frac7{2\sqrt{2n}}.
\tag{4}
\]

Put
\[
r=\left\lfloor\frac{\sqrt n}{4}\right\rfloor
\]
and define
\[
B=\{X,2X,\ldots,rX\}.
\]
We have $B\subseteq\{1,\ldots,n\}$. For $1\le j\le r$, equation (4) gives
\[
0>
j\left(\frac X{\sqrt2}-Y\right)
>
-\frac1{\sqrt2}.
\]
Therefore
\[
\left\{\frac{jX}{\sqrt2}\right\}
>
1-\frac1{\sqrt2},
\]
so, by the characterization from Solution 1,
\[
jX\in H.
\]
Every positive difference between elements of $B$ has this form, and hence lies in $H$.

For $n\ge64$,
\[
|B|=r\ge\frac{\sqrt n}{8}.
\]
Using (1),
\[
|A|\le\frac{2n}{|B|}
\le16\sqrt n.
\]
For $n<64$, the trivial estimate
\[
|A|\le n<8\sqrt n
\]
is sufficient. Thus, for example,
\[
\boxed{C=16}
\]
works.

SOLUTION 4:
We retain the negative-Pell construction from Solution 3, but use it with a different counting argument.

Choose a solution of
\[
X^2-2Y^2=-1
\]
such that
\[
\frac{\sqrt n}{7}<X\le\sqrt n.
\]
As proved in Solution 3, if
\[
R=\left\lfloor\frac{\sqrt n}{4}\right\rfloor,
\]
then
\[
X,2X,\ldots,RX
\]
all belong to $H$.

Suppose that $A\subseteq\{1,\ldots,n\}$ has no positive difference in $H$. Partition $A$ according to its residue modulo $X$.

Within one fixed residue class, write the elements as
\[
r+q_1X<r+q_2X<\cdots<r+q_sX.
\]
If
\[
1\le q_{i+1}-q_i\le R,
\]
then
\[
(q_{i+1}-q_i)X\in H,
\]
contrary to the assumption on $A$. Hence
\[
q_{i+1}-q_i\ge R+1.
\]
Since all elements are at most $n$, each residue class contains at most
\[
1+\frac{n}{X(R+1)}
\]
elements.

There are at most $X$ residue classes, so
\[
|A|
\le
X+\frac{n}{R+1}.
\]
Using
\[
X\le\sqrt n
\qquad\text{and}\qquad
R+1>\frac{\sqrt n}{4},
\]
we obtain
\[
|A|<5\sqrt n
\]
whenever $n\ge16$. For $n<16$, the trivial estimate
\[
|A|\le n\le4\sqrt n
\]
is sufficient. Thus one may take, for example,
\[
\boxed{C=5}.
\]
