ID: 0306

SOURCE: IMO Shortlist

YEAR: 2020

TOPIC: Algebra

SUBTOPICS: Functional Equations, Injectivity, Monotonicity, Boundedness

STATEMENT:
Let $\mathbb R_{>0}$ be the set of positive real numbers. Determine all functions
\[
f:\mathbb R_{>0}\to\mathbb R_{>0}
\]
such that
\[
f\bigl(x+f(xy)\bigr)+y=f(x)f(y)+1
\tag{1}
\]
for all positive real numbers $x,y$.

SOLUTION 1:
The unique solution is
\[
\boxed{f(x)=x+1}.
\]
It is immediate that this function satisfies (1).

We prove uniqueness in four steps.

\textbf{Step 1: $f$ is injective.}

Put $x=1$ in (1):
\[
f(1+f(y))+y=f(1)f(y)+1.
\]
Thus $y$ is uniquely determined by $f(y)$. Therefore
\[
f(y_1)=f(y_2)\Longrightarrow y_1=y_2.
\]

\textbf{Step 2: $f$ is strictly increasing.}

Fix $y>0$. From (1),
\[
f\bigl(x+f(xy)\bigr)=f(x)f(y)+1-y.
\]
The right side is injective as a function of $x$, because $f(y)>0$ and $f$ is injective. Since $f$ itself is injective,
\[
x_1+f(x_1y)\ne x_2+f(x_2y)
\qquad(x_1\ne x_2).
\]
Write
\[
z_i=x_i y.
\]
Then, for all $y>0$ and all distinct $z_1,z_2>0$,
\[
\frac{z_1-z_2}{y}\ne f(z_2)-f(z_1).
\]
Dividing by $z_1-z_2$,
\[
\frac1y\ne
\frac{f(z_2)-f(z_1)}{z_1-z_2}.
\]
As $1/y$ ranges over all positive real numbers, the quotient on the right cannot be positive. Hence
\[
\frac{f(z_1)-f(z_2)}{z_1-z_2}\ge0.
\]
Thus $f$ is nondecreasing, and injectivity makes it strictly increasing.

\textbf{Step 3: $f$ is affine.}

Since $f$ is increasing and positive, the right limit
\[
p=\lim_{x\to0^+}f(x)
\]
exists and is nonnegative. The right limit
\[
q=\lim_{t\to p^+}f(t)
\]
also exists.

Fix $y>0$ and let $x\to0^+$ in (1). Then
\[
f(xy)\to p
\]
and
\[
x+f(xy)\to p^+.
\]
Therefore
\[
q+y=p f(y)+1.
\tag{2}
\]
We cannot have $p=0$, because then (2) would give $q+y=1$ for every $y$. Thus
\[
f(y)=\frac{y+q-1}{p},
\]
so $f$ is affine:
\[
f(y)=ay+b.
\]

\textbf{Step 4: determination of the coefficients.}

Substituting $f(t)=at+b$ into (1), we get
\[
a(x+axy+b)+b+y=(ax+b)(ay+b)+1.
\]
Comparing the coefficients of $x$, $y$, and the constant term gives
\[
a-ab=0,
\qquad
1-ab=0,
\qquad
ab+b-b^2-1=0.
\]
The second equation gives $a,b>0$ and $ab=1$. The first then gives $b=1$, and therefore $a=1$.

Hence
\[
f(x)=x+1
\]
for every positive real $x$.

SOLUTION 2:
Put
\[
a=f(1)
\qquad\text{and}\qquad
\varphi(x)=f(x)-x-1.
\]
Equation (1) becomes
\[
\varphi\bigl(x+f(xy)\bigr)
=f(x)f(y)-f(xy)-x-y.
\tag{2}
\]
The right side is symmetric in $x,y$, so
\[
\varphi\bigl(x+f(xy)\bigr)
=
\varphi\bigl(y+f(xy)\bigr).
\]
Taking $(x,y)=(t,1/t)$ gives
\[
\varphi(a+t)=\varphi\left(a+\frac1t\right)
\qquad(t>0).
\tag{3}
\]

We first establish boundedness.

The function $f$ is bounded below by a positive constant. Indeed, fix any $y_0>1$. From (1),
\[
f(x)f(y_0)>y_0-1
\]
for every $x>0$. Hence the number
\[
M=\inf_{x>0}f(x)
\]
is positive, and
\[
f(x)\ge M
\qquad(x>0).
\tag{4}
\]
Taking the infimum over $x$ in the inequality
\[
f(x)f(y)>y-1
\]
gives
\[
f(y)\ge\frac{y-1}{M}
\qquad(y>0).
\tag{5}
\]

\textbf{Lemma 1.}
The function $f$, and hence $\varphi$, is bounded on every compact interval
\[
[p,q]\subset(0,\infty).
\]

\textit{Proof.}
The lower bound follows from (4). Let $z\in[p,q]$ and put
\[
s=f(z).
\]
Putting $y=1$ in (1),
\[
f(z+s)=as
\]
and then
\[
f(z+s+as)=a^2s.
\tag{6}
\]

Use (1) with
\[
x=z,
\qquad
y=1+\frac sz.
\]
Since $xy=z+s$ and $f(z+s)=as$,
\[
f(z+as)
=s f\left(1+\frac sz\right)-\frac sz.
\]
By (5),
\[
f(z+as)\ge\frac{s^2}{Mz}-\frac sz.
\tag{7}
\]

Next use (1) with
\[
x=z+as,
\qquad
y=\frac z{z+as}.
\]
Then $xy=z$, and (6) gives
\[
a^2s+\frac z{z+as}
=
f(z+as)f\left(\frac z{z+as}\right)+1.
\]
Using (4),
\[
a^2s\ge M f(z+as).
\]
Together with (7),
\[
a^2s\ge\frac{s^2}{z}-\frac{Ms}{z},
\]
and hence
\[
s\le M+a^2z\le M+a^2q.
\]
Thus $f$ is bounded above on $[p,q]$. \hfill$\square$

By Lemma 1, $\varphi$ is bounded on $[a,a+1]$. Equation (3) then shows that $\varphi$ is bounded on $[a+1,\infty)$ as well. Thus $\varphi$ is bounded on $[a,\infty)$.

Put
\[
C=\max\{a,3\}.
\]

\textbf{Lemma 2.}
For every $x\ge C$,
\[
\varphi(x)=0.
\]

\textit{Proof.}
Taking $y=x$ in (2),
\[
\varphi\bigl(x+f(x^2)\bigr)
=f(x)^2-f(x^2)-2x.
\]
Adding
\[
\varphi(x^2)=f(x^2)-x^2-1
\]
gives
\[
\varphi\bigl(x+f(x^2)\bigr)+\varphi(x^2)
=
\varphi(x)\bigl(f(x)+x+1\bigr).
\tag{8}
\]
For $x\ge C\ge3$,
\[
f(x)+x+1\ge4.
\]
Therefore
\[
|\varphi(x)|
\le
\frac14\left(
\left|\varphi\bigl(x+f(x^2)\bigr)\right|
+|\varphi(x^2)|
\right).
\tag{9}
\]
Let
\[
S=\sup_{x\ge C}|\varphi(x)|<\infty.
\]
Both arguments on the right of (9) are at least $C$, so taking the supremum yields
\[
S\le\frac S2.
\]
Hence $S=0$, proving the lemma. \hfill$\square$

Thus
\[
f(x)=x+1
\qquad(x\ge C).
\]
Finally, fix any $y>0$ and choose
\[
x>\max\left\{C,\frac Cy\right\}.
\]
Then $x$, $xy$, and $x+f(xy)$ are all at least $C$. Equation (1) becomes
\[
x+xy+2+y=(x+1)f(y)+1.
\]
The left side equals
\[
(x+1)(y+1)+1,
\]
so
\[
f(y)=y+1.
\]
Therefore the unique solution is
\[
\boxed{f(x)=x+1}.
\]
