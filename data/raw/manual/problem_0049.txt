ID: 0049

SOURCE: IMO Shortlist

YEAR: 2011

TOPIC: Geometry

SUBTOPICS: Tangential Polygons, Rotations, Polarity, Vectors

STATEMENT:
Let $ABCDEF$ be a convex hexagon all of whose sides are tangent to a circle $\omega$ with center $O$. Suppose that the circumcircle of triangle $ACE$ is concentric with $\omega$. Let $J$ be the foot of the perpendicular from $B$ to $CD$. The line through $B$ perpendicular to $DF$ meets $EO$ at $K$. Let $L$ be the foot of the perpendicular from $K$ to $DE$. Prove that
\[
DJ=DL.
\]

SOLUTION 1:
Let $r$ be the radius of $\omega$. Since the circumcircle of $ACE$ is centered at $O$, we have $OA=OC=OE$. The tangent length from any one of $A,C,E$ to $\omega$ is $\sqrt{OX^2-r^2}$, so these three tangent lengths are equal. It follows that
\[
AB=BC,
\qquad
CD=DE,
\qquad
EF=FA.
\tag{1}
\]
Moreover, $O$ lies on the internal angle bisectors at $A,C,E$, and $\sin(\angle X/2)=r/OX$ at each of these vertices. Since $OA=OC=OE$, the corresponding vertex angles are equal:
\[
\angle BCD=\angle DEF=\angle FAB.
\tag{2}
\]

Consider the rotation about $D$ that sends $C$ to $E$. Let $B'$ and $L'$ be the images of $B$ and $J$. Then
\[
DJ=DL',
\qquad
B'L'\perp DE,
\]
and triangles $B'ED$ and $BCD$ are congruent. Let
\[
K'=EO\cap B'L'.
\]
It is enough to prove
\[
K'B\perp DF.
\tag{3}
\]
Indeed, (3) would imply $K'=K$, and then the perpendicular foot from $K$ to $DE$ would be $L'=L$, giving $DJ=DL$.

Now consider the rotation about $F$ that sends $A$ to $E$, and let $B''$ be the image of $B$. Then triangles $FAB$ and $FEB''$ are congruent. By (1) and (2),
\[
EB''=AB=BC=EB'
\]
and
\[
\angle FEB''=\angle FAB=\angle BCD=\angle DEB'.
\]
Thus $B'$ and $B''$ are symmetric with respect to the angle bisector $EO$ of $\angle DEF$. Since $K'\in EO$ and
\[
K'B'\perp DE,
\]
reflection in $EO$ gives
\[
K'B''\perp EF.
\tag{4}
\]

Because $K'B'\perp DE$, the perpendicular components cancel when squared distances to $D$ and $E$ are subtracted, so
\[
K'D^2-K'E^2=B'D^2-B'E^2.
\tag{5}
\]
Similarly, (4) gives
\[
K'E^2-K'F^2=B''E^2-B''F^2.
\tag{6}
\]
Adding (5) and (6), and using $B'E=B''E$, we obtain
\[
\begin{aligned}
K'D^2-K'F^2
&=B'D^2-B''F^2\\
&=BD^2-BF^2,
\end{aligned}
\tag{7}
\]
because the two rotations preserve the corresponding distances.

For any points $X,Y$, the equality
\[
XD^2-XF^2=YD^2-YF^2
\]
is equivalent to
\[
XY\perp DF.
\]
Applying this to (7) with $X=K'$ and $Y=B$ proves (3). Therefore $K'=K$, $L'=L$, and
\[
DJ=DL.
\]

SOLUTION 2:
Let $R,S,T,U,V,W$ be the points where the sides
\[
AB,BC,CD,DE,EF,FA
\]
touch $\omega$, respectively. As in Solution 1,
\[
AR=AW=CS=CT=EU=EV.
\tag{1}
\]
Reflection in the line $BO$ sends $R$ to $S$. In view of (1), it also sends $A$ to $C$ and $W$ to $T$. Hence
\[
RS\parallel WT,
\]
and both lines are perpendicular to $OB$.

Let
\[
Z=UV\cap WT.
\]
The lines $UV$ and $WT$ are not parallel. Otherwise the hexagon would be symmetric about $BO$, and the line through $B$ perpendicular to $DF$ would coincide with $EO$, so the point $K$ would not be uniquely defined. We use the standard pole-polar fact that
\[
D,F,Z\text{ are collinear}.
\tag{2}
\]
Indeed, with respect to $\omega$, the point $D$ is the pole of the chord $TU$, and $F$ is the pole of $VW$. Put $Q=TU\cap VW$. By La Hire's theorem, both $D$ and $F$ lie on the polar of $Q$. For the complete quadrilateral with vertices $T,U,V,W$, the diagonal points $Q=TU\cap VW$ and $Z=TW\cap UV$ are conjugate; hence $Z$ also lies on the polar of $Q$. Thus $D,F,Z$ are collinear.

Place the origin at $O$ and identify every point with its position vector. Since $K\in EO$ and $UV$ is the chord of contact from $E$, we have
\[
OK\perp UV,
\]
so, because $Z,U\in UV$,
\[
K\cdot(Z-U)=0.
\tag{3}
\]
Also $OB\perp WT$, and $Z,T\in WT$, so
\[
B\cdot(Z-T)=0.
\tag{4}
\]
By the definition of $K$ and (2),
\[
BK\perp DF=DZ.
\]
Therefore
\[
(K-B)\cdot(D-Z)=0,
\]
or
\[
K\cdot(D-Z)=B\cdot(D-Z).
\tag{5}
\]
Adding (3) to the left side of (5), and (4) to its right side, gives
\[
K\cdot(D-U)=B\cdot(D-T).
\tag{6}
\]

The two tangent segments from $D$ to $\omega$ are equal, so
\[
DU=DT.
\]
Since also $OU=OT$, expansion of the two squared distances yields
\[
D\cdot(D-U)=D\cdot(D-T).
\tag{7}
\]
Subtracting (7) from (6), we obtain
\[
(K-D)\cdot(D-U)=(B-D)\cdot(D-T),
\]
or, in directed-vector notation,
\[
\overrightarrow{DK}\cdot\overrightarrow{UD}
=
\overrightarrow{DB}\cdot\overrightarrow{TD}.
\tag{8}
\]
The orthogonal projection of $\overrightarrow{DK}$ onto $DU$ has length $DL$, and the orthogonal projection of $\overrightarrow{DB}$ onto $DT$ has length $DJ$. Hence (8) becomes
\[
DL\cdot DU=DJ\cdot DT.
\]
Since $DU=DT$, we conclude that
\[
DL=DJ.
\]
