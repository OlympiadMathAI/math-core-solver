ID: 0094

SOURCE: IMO Shortlist

YEAR: 2013

TOPIC: Algebra

SUBTOPICS: Polynomial Functional Equations, Root Propagation, Complex Roots, Finite Differences

STATEMENT:
Let $m\ne0$ be an integer. Find all polynomials $P(x)$ with real coefficients such that
\[
(x^3-mx^2+1)P(x+1)+(x^3+mx^2+1)P(x-1)
=2(x^3-mx+1)P(x)
\tag{1}
\]
for all real numbers $x$.

SOLUTION 1:
The answer is
\[
P(x)=tx
\]
for an arbitrary real constant $t$.

The zero polynomial is included by taking $t=0$. Suppose now that $P$ is nonzero, and write
\[
P(x)=a_nx^n+\cdots+a_0,
\qquad a_n\ne0.
\]
Comparing the coefficients of $x^{n+1}$ in (1) gives
\[
a_n(n-2m)(n-1)=0.
\tag{2}
\]
Hence
\[
n=1
\qquad\text{or}\qquad
n=2m.
\tag{3}
\]
If $n=1$, write $P(x)=tx+s$. The polynomial $x$ satisfies (1), whereas substitution of the constant polynomial $1$ leaves the nonzero difference $2mx$. By linearity of (1), $s=0$, and all linear solutions are $P(x)=tx$.

Assume for a contradiction that $n=2m$. Then $m\ge1$ and $n\ge2$. Consider
\[
H(x)=xP(x+1)-(x+1)P(x).
\]
Its leading term is $(n-1)a_nx^n$, so $H$ has degree $n$ and therefore has a complex root $r$. The relation $H(r)=0$ allows us to choose a complex number $k$ so that
\[
S(x)=P(x)-kx
\]
has both $r$ and $r+1$ as roots. Explicitly, if $r\notin\{0,-1\}$, take
\[
k=\frac{P(r)}r=\frac{P(r+1)}{r+1};
\]
if $r=0$, take $k=P(1)$; and if $r=-1$, take $k=-P(-1)$. Since both $P$ and $kx$ satisfy (1), so does $S$. Moreover, $S$ is nonzero because $\deg P=n\ge2$.

Set
\[
A(x)=x^3-mx^2+1,
\qquad
B(x)=x^3+mx^2+1.
\]
Equation (1), applied to $S$, shows the following:

\[
\begin{array}{ll}
\text{(i)}&S(s-1)=S(s)=0\text{ and }A(s)\ne0\Longrightarrow S(s+1)=0,\\[1mm]
\text{(ii)}&S(s)=S(s+1)=0\text{ and }B(s)\ne0\Longrightarrow S(s-1)=0.
\end{array}
\tag{4}
\]
Because $S$ has only finitely many roots, there exist integers $a\ge0$ and $b\ge1$ such that
\[
r-a,r-a+1,\ldots,r+b
\]
are roots of $S$, while $r-a-1$ and $r+b+1$ are not. By (4),
\[
B(r-a)=0,
\qquad
A(r+b)=0.
\]
Let
\[
c=a+b\ge1,
\qquad
\rho=r-a.
\]
Then $\rho$ is a common root of the integer polynomials
\[
B(x)
\qquad\text{and}\qquad
A(x+c).
\]
Thus these two cubics have a nonconstant common divisor over $\mathbb{Q}$.

The cubic $B(x)=x^3+mx^2+1$ has no rational root. Indeed, the rational-root theorem leaves only $\pm1$, but
\[
B(-1)=m>0,
\qquad
B(1)=m+2>0.
\]
Hence $B$ is irreducible over $\mathbb{Q}$. It follows that $B(x)$ divides $A(x+c)$. Since both are monic cubics,
\[
B(x)=A(x+c).
\]
Subtracting gives
\[
0=A(x+c)-B(x)
=(3c-2m)x^2+c(3c-2m)x+c^2(c-m).
\]
Therefore
\[
3c-2m=0,
\qquad
c-m=0.
\]
These equations imply $m=0$, contradicting the hypothesis. Thus the case $n=2m$ is impossible, and the only solutions are $P(x)=tx$.

SOLUTION 2:
Again, $P(x)=tx$ is easily checked to work. We prove that every solution has this form.

Multiplying (1) by $x$ and regrouping, using the fact that the polynomial $x$ itself satisfies (1), gives
\[
(x^3-mx^2+1)Q(x)=(x^3+mx^2+1)Q(x-1),
\tag{2}
\]
where
\[
Q(x)=xP(x+1)-(x+1)P(x).
\tag{3}
\]
If $\deg P\ge2$, then $\deg Q=\deg P$, because the leading coefficient of $Q$ is $(\deg P-1)$ times the leading coefficient of $P$.

Let $R_Q$ be the multiset of complex roots of $Q$, counted with multiplicity. Let
\[
X=\{x_1,x_2,x_3\}
\]
and
\[
Y=\{y_1,y_2,y_3\}
\]
be the multisets of roots of
\[
A(x)=x^3-mx^2+1
\]
and
\[
B(x)=x^3+mx^2+1,
\]
respectively. Equation (2) gives the multiset identity
\[
X\sqcup R_Q=Y\sqcup(R_Q+1),
\tag{4}
\]
where $R_Q+1=\{r+1:r\in R_Q\}$, with multiplicities preserved.

We use (4) to decompose the roots into chains. For each occurrence of a root $r$ of $Q$, draw a directed edge from $r$ to $r+1$. At every complex number, identity (4) says that the number of incoming edges together with its multiplicity in $Y$ equals the number of outgoing edges together with its multiplicity in $X$. The graph is finite and has no directed cycle, since every edge increases the real part by $1$. Hence it is a disjoint union of three directed paths, each beginning at an occurrence from $Y$ and ending at an occurrence from $X$. After relabelling, these paths have the form
\[
y_i,y_i+1,\ldots,y_i+k_i=x_i
\qquad(i=1,2,3)
\tag{5}
\]
for some integers $k_i\ge0$. In particular, $A$ and $B$ have the same number of real roots.

For an integer parameter $a$, define
\[
F_a(x)=x^3+ax^2+1.
\]
Its critical points are $0$ and $-2a/3$, and
\[
F_a(0)=1,
\qquad
F_a(-2a/3)=1+\frac{4a^3}{27}.
\]
It follows that, for integral $a$,
\[
F_a\text{ has }
\begin{cases}
3\text{ real roots},&a\le-2,\\
1\text{ real root},&a\ge-1.
\end{cases}
\tag{6}
\]
Now $B=F_m$ and $A=F_{-m}$. Since (5) forces them to have the same number of real roots, (6) and $m\ne0$ imply
\[
m=1
\qquad\text{or}\qquad
m=-1.
\tag{7}
\]

Let $\alpha$ be the unique real root of
\[
F_1(x)=x^3+x^2+1,
\]
and let $\beta$ be the unique real root of
\[
F_{-1}(x)=x^3-x^2+1.
\]
We have
\[
F_1(-3/2)=-1/8<0<F_1(-1)=1,
\]
so
\[
-\frac32<\alpha<-1.
\]
Also,
\[
F_{-1}(-1)=-1<0<F_{-1}(-1/2)=\frac58,
\]
so
\[
-1<\beta<-\frac12.
\]
Thus $0<\beta-\alpha<1$, and the two real roots cannot differ by an integer. This contradicts (5), for either choice $m=\pm1$.

Therefore $\deg P\le1$. Writing $P(x)=tx+s$ in (1), the $tx$ part satisfies the equation and the constant part contributes $2msx$. Since $m\ne0$, we must have $s=0$. Hence all solutions are
\[
P(x)=tx,
\qquad t\in\mathbb{R}.
\]
