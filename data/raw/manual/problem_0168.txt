ID: 0168

SOURCE: IMO Shortlist

YEAR: 2015

TOPIC: Number Theory

SUBTOPICS: Factorials, Divisibility, Prime Factorization, Inequalities

STATEMENT:
Let $a$ and $b$ be positive integers such that
\[
a!+b!\mid a!b!.
\]
Prove that
\[
3a\ge 2b+2.
\]

SOLUTION 1:
If $a>b$, then $a\ge b+1$, and hence
\[
3a\ge3b+3>2b+2.
\]
If $a=b$, the hypothesis excludes $a=b=1$, so $a\ge2$ and
\[
3a\ge2a+2.
\]

It remains to consider $a<b$. Put
\[
c=b-a.
\]
The desired inequality is equivalent to
\[
a\ge2c+2.
\tag{1}
\]
Assume, for contradiction, that
\[
a\le2c+1.
\tag{2}
\]

Define
\[
M=\frac{b!}{a!}=(a+1)(a+2)\cdots(a+c).
\]
Since
\[
a!+b!=a!(1+M)
\]
and $a!+b!\mid a!b!$, we obtain
\[
1+M\mid a!M.
\]
Because $\gcd(1+M,M)=1$,
\[
1+M\mid a!.
\tag{3}
\]

We must have $c<a$. Indeed, if $c\ge a$, then the first $a$ factors of $M$ are respectively larger than $1,2,\ldots,a$, so $M>a!$, contradicting (3).

The product of any $c$ consecutive integers is divisible by $c!$, so $c!\mid M$. Therefore
\[
\gcd(1+M,c!)=1,
\]
and (3) yields
\[
1+M\mid\frac{a!}{c!}
=(c+1)(c+2)\cdots a.
\tag{4}
\]

If $a\le2c$, then the number on the right of (4) is a product of at most $c$ positive integers, all at most $a$, while $M$ is a product of exactly $c$ integers, all greater than $a$. Thus
\[
\frac{a!}{c!}<M<1+M,
\]
contradicting (4).

The only remaining possibility under (2) is
\[
a=2c+1.
\]
Now $c+1$ divides $a+1=2(c+1)$, hence $c+1\mid M$. Consequently
\[
\gcd(1+M,c+1)=1.
\]
Dividing the right side of (4) by $c+1$, we get
\[
1+M\mid(c+2)(c+3)\cdots a.
\]
This is again a product of $c$ integers not exceeding $a$, whereas $M$ is a product of $c$ integers greater than $a$. The right-hand side is therefore smaller than $1+M$, a contradiction.

Thus (2) is impossible, so $a\ge2c+2$. Since $c=b-a$, this is precisely
\[
3a\ge2b+2.
\]

SOLUTION 2:
As above, only the case $a<b$ needs proof. Put
\[
c=b-a
\]
and suppose, for contradiction, that
\[
a\le2c+1.
\tag{1}
\]
Set
\[
N=1+(a+1)(a+2)\cdots(a+c).
\]
The original divisibility implies
\[
N\mid(a+c)!.
\tag{2}
\]

Let $p$ be any prime divisor of $N$. Since $N$ is coprime to
\[
(a+1)(a+2)\cdots(a+c),
\]
we cannot have $p\le c$, because $c!$ divides that product. We also cannot have
\[
a+1\le p\le a+c,
\]
because then $p$ itself is one of its factors. Thus
\[
c+1\le p\le a.
\tag{3}
\]

We claim that
\[
2p>a+c.
\tag{4}
\]
Otherwise, using (1) and (3),
\[
a+1\le2c+2\le2p\le a+c,
\]
so the factor $2p$ occurs among $a+1,\ldots,a+c$. This would give $p\mid N-1$, contradicting $p\mid N$.

By (4), the exponent of $p$ in $(a+c)!$ is exactly $1$. Hence, from (2), every prime factor of $N$ occurs in $N$ with exponent $1$.

If $a\le c+2$, the interval
\[
\left(\frac{a+c}{2},a\right]
\]
contains at most one integer, namely possibly $a$. By (3) and (4), every prime factor of $N$ lies in this interval. Since $N$ is squarefree, this would force $N=a$, but clearly
\[
N>1+(a+1)>a,
\]
a contradiction.

Therefore
\[
a\ge c+3.
\]
Now every prime factor $p$ of $N$ lies in
\[
[c+2,a],
\]
and occurs only once. Consequently
\[
N\mid(c+2)(c+3)\cdots a.
\tag{5}
\]
The product on the right has
\[
a-c-1\le c
\]
factors by (1), all at most $a$. On the other hand, $N-1$ is a product of $c$ factors all greater than $a$. Hence
\[
(c+2)(c+3)\cdots a<N,
\]
contradicting (5).

Thus the assumption (1) is false, and the desired inequality follows.
