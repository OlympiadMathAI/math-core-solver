ID: 0338

SOURCE: IMO Shortlist

YEAR: 2021

TOPIC: Algebra

SUBTOPICS: Functional Equations, Injectivity, Multiplicative Functions, Cauchy Equation

STATEMENT:
Determine all functions
\[
f:\mathbb R\to\mathbb R
\]
satisfying
\[
\begin{aligned}
&(f(a)-f(b))(f(b)-f(c))(f(c)-f(a))\\
&\qquad=
f(ab^2+bc^2+ca^2)
-
f(a^2b+b^2c+c^2a)
\end{aligned}
\tag{1}
\]
for all real numbers $a,b,c$.

SOLUTION 1:
The solutions are
\[
\boxed{f(x)=\alpha x+\beta}
\]
and
\[
\boxed{f(x)=\alpha x^3+\beta},
\]
where
\[
\alpha\in\{-1,0,1\},
\qquad
\beta\in\mathbb R.
\]

The identities
\[
\begin{aligned}
&ab^2+bc^2+ca^2-(a^2b+b^2c+c^2a)\\
&\qquad=(a-b)(b-c)(c-a)
\end{aligned}
\tag{2}
\]
and
\[
\begin{aligned}
&(ab^2+bc^2+ca^2)^3-(a^2b+b^2c+c^2a)^3\\
&\qquad=(a^3-b^3)(b^3-c^3)(c^3-a^3)
\end{aligned}
\tag{3}
\]
show directly that all the listed functions work.

Adding a constant to a solution preserves (1), so we may assume during the proof that
\[
f(0)=0.
\tag{4}
\]

We first prove a structural lemma.

\textbf{Lemma 1.}
Under normalization (4), either
\[
f\equiv0
\]
or $f$ is injective.

\textit{Proof.}
Let
\[
\Theta=\{(u,v)\in\mathbb R^2:f(u)=f(v)\}.
\]
If $(a,b)\in\Theta$, then applying (1) to $(a,b,x)$ gives
\[
H_{a,b}(x)\in\Theta
\]
for every real $x$, where
\[
H_{a,b}(x)=
\left(
ab^2+bx^2+xa^2,\,
a^2b+b^2x+x^2a
\right).
\tag{5}
\]

Suppose that $(a,b)\in\Theta$ with $a\ne b$. The difference and sum of the two coordinates in (5) are
\[
(a-b)(b-x)(x-a)
\]
and
\[
(x^2+ab)(a+b)+x(a^2+b^2).
\]
Neither is the zero polynomial in $x$, so we can choose $x$ for which both are nonzero. We thereby obtain
\[
(\alpha,\beta)\in\Theta
\]
with
\[
\alpha\ne\beta
\qquad\text{and}\qquad
\alpha\ne-\beta.
\]

Compare (1) for $(\alpha,1,0)$ and $(\beta,1,0)$. Their left sides are equal, and so
\[
f(\alpha^2)=f(\beta^2).
\]
Thus $\Theta$ contains two distinct nonnegative numbers. Repeatedly squaring and interchanging them if necessary, we obtain
\[
(\gamma,\delta)\in\Theta
\qquad\text{with}\qquad
\delta>5\gamma\ge0.
\]

If $\gamma=0$, then $\Theta$ already contains $(0,\delta)$ with $\delta\ne0$. Otherwise choose a real root $x<0$ of
\[
\delta\gamma^2+\gamma x^2+x\delta^2=0.
\]
Such a root exists because the discriminant
\[
\delta^4-4\delta\gamma^3
\]
is positive. Formula (5) gives
\[
H_{\delta,\gamma}(x)=(0,r)\in\Theta.
\]
Moreover,
\[
-r
=
(\delta-\gamma)(\gamma-x)(x-\delta)<0,
\]
so $r\ne0$.

Now $(0,r)\in\Theta$ implies, through (5),
\[
f(rx^2)=f(r^2x)
\qquad(x\in\mathbb R).
\]
Replacing $x$ by $-x$ shows
\[
f(r^2x)=f(-r^2x),
\]
and hence $f$ is even.

Apply (5) to $(a,-a)$ and choose
\[
x=\frac{1+\sqrt5}{2}a.
\]
The resulting pair is
\[
\left(0,(1+\sqrt5)a^3\right)\in\Theta.
\]
As $a$ ranges over the reals, the second coordinate ranges over all of $\mathbb R$. Thus
\[
f(y)=f(0)=0
\]
for every real $y$. This proves the lemma. $\square$

Assume from now on that $f$ is injective. Taking $c=0$ in (1) gives
\[
f(a)f(b)(f(a)-f(b))
=
f(a^2b)-f(ab^2).
\tag{6}
\]
Put
\[
\kappa=f(1)\ne0.
\]
Setting $b=1$ in (6),
\[
\kappa f(a)(f(a)-\kappa)
=
f(a^2)-f(a).
\tag{7}
\]

Subtract the version of (7) for $-a$ from the version for $a$. For $a\ne0$, injectivity gives
\[
f(a)\ne f(-a),
\]
and hence
\[
f(a)+f(-a)
=
\kappa-\kappa^{-1}
=:\lambda.
\tag{8}
\]
Use (6) for $(a,b)$ and $(-a,-b)$, together with (8). For nonzero $a\ne b$, we obtain
\[
(f(a)-f(b))
\left(
f(a)f(b)-f(-a)f(-b)
\right)=0.
\]
Injectivity removes the first factor, so
\[
f(a)f(b)
=
(\lambda-f(a))(\lambda-f(b)).
\]
If $\lambda\ne0$, this implies
\[
f(a)+f(b)=\lambda
\]
for every pair of distinct nonzero $a,b$, contradicting injectivity. Therefore
\[
\lambda=0,
\qquad
\kappa=\pm1.
\]
Thus $f$ is odd. Replacing $f$ by $-f$ if necessary, we may assume
\[
f(1)=1.
\tag{9}
\]

Equation (7) now gives
\[
f(a^2)=f(a)^2.
\tag{10}
\]
Adding equation (6) for $(a,b)$ and for $(a,-b)$, and using oddness, gives
\[
f(a)f(b)^2=f(ab^2).
\]
Together with (10), this yields
\[
f(ax)=f(a)f(x)
\]
first for $x\ge0$, and then, by oddness, for every real $x$. Hence $f$ is multiplicative. Also,
\[
f(x)>0
\qquad(x>0),
\tag{11}
\]
because $f(x)=f(\sqrt x)^2$ and injectivity excludes the value $0$.

We shall use the following standard fact.

\textbf{Lemma 2.}
A positive multiplicative function
\[
g:(0,\infty)\to(0,\infty)
\]
either has the form
\[
g(x)=x^\tau
\]
for some real constant $\tau$, or its graph is dense in
\[
(0,\infty)^2.
\]

\textit{Proof.}
Define
\[
h(t)=\log g(e^t).
\]
Then $h$ is additive. If $h(t)=\tau t$ for every $t$, the first alternative follows. Otherwise there are $u,v$ such that the vectors
\[
(u,h(u))
\qquad\text{and}\qquad
(v,h(v))
\]
are linearly independent. The graph of $h$ contains every rational linear combination of these two vectors, and is therefore dense in $\mathbb R^2$. Applying the coordinatewise exponential map proves the second alternative. $\square$

We exclude both a dense graph and a negative exponent. In either of those two cases, choose
\[
0<b<\frac1{10}
\qquad\text{with}\qquad
f(b)>1.
\]
Apply (1) to $(1,b,x)$. Put
\[
B=f(b)>1.
\]
Since
\[
x^2+b^2x+b>0
\]
for every real $x$, equation (1) gives
\[
\begin{aligned}
f(b^2+bx^2+x)
&=
f(x^2+b^2x+b)\\
&\quad+
(B-1)(f(x)-B)(f(x)-1)\\
&\ge
-\frac{(B-1)^3}{4}.
\end{aligned}
\tag{12}
\]
The quadratic
\[
b^2+bx^2+x
\]
takes every value in
\[
\left[b^2-\frac1{4b},\infty\right).
\]
Thus $f$ is bounded below on this interval. Since $f$ is odd, it is bounded above on the nonempty interval
\[
\left(0,\frac1{4b}-b^2\right).
\]
This contradicts density of the graph. It also contradicts
\[
f(x)=x^\tau
\]
when $\tau<0$, since that function is unbounded near $0$.

Therefore
\[
f(x)=x^\tau
\qquad(x>0)
\]
for some $\tau\ge0$. Injectivity gives
\[
\tau>0.
\tag{13}
\]

Finally, divide (1) by
\[
(a-b)(b-c)(c-a)
=
ab^2+bc^2+ca^2-(a^2b+b^2c+c^2a)
\]
and let the three distinct positive numbers $a,b,c$ tend to $1$. Using (13), the left side tends to
\[
\tau^3,
\]
while the right side tends to
\[
\tau\,3^{\tau-1}.
\]
Hence
\[
\tau^2=3^{\tau-1}.
\tag{14}
\]
Equivalently,
\[
3^{\tau/2-1/2}-\tau=0.
\]
The function on the left is strictly convex, so it has at most two positive zeros. Since
\[
\tau=1
\qquad\text{and}\qquad
\tau=3
\]
both satisfy (14), these are the only possibilities.

Under normalization (4), the injective solutions are therefore
\[
f(x)=\pm x
\qquad\text{and}\qquad
f(x)=\pm x^3,
\]
while the noninjective solution is $f\equiv0$. Restoring the arbitrary additive constant gives exactly the functions stated at the beginning.
