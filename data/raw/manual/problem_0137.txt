ID: 0137

SOURCE: IMO Shortlist

YEAR: 2014

TOPIC: Geometry

SUBTOPICS: Incenter and Excenter, Cyclic Quadrilaterals, Inversion, Homothety, Midpoints, Angle Bisectors

STATEMENT:
Let $ABC$ be a triangle with circumcircle $\Omega$ and incenter $I$. The line through $I$ perpendicular to $CI$ meets the segment $BC$ at $U$ and the arc $BC$ of $\Omega$ not containing $A$ at $V$. The line through $U$ parallel to $AI$ meets $AV$ at $X$, and the line through $V$ parallel to $AI$ meets $AB$ at $Y$. Let $W$ and $Z$ be the midpoints of $AX$ and $BC$, respectively.

Prove that if the points $I,X,Y$ are collinear, then the points $I,W,Z$ are also collinear.

SOLUTION 1:
Angles are interpreted modulo $180^\circ$ whenever a directed-angle reading is needed.

Put
\[
\alpha=\frac{\angle A}{2},\qquad
\beta=\frac{\angle B}{2},\qquad
\gamma=\frac{\angle C}{2},
\]
so that
\[
\alpha+\beta+\gamma=90^\circ.
\]
Since $IU\perp IC$ and $U\in BC$, we have
\[
\angle IUC=90^\circ-\gamma=\alpha+\beta.
\]
As $I,U,V$ are collinear,
\[
\angle BIV=\angle IUC-\angle IBC=\alpha.
\]
On the other hand, $VY\parallel AI$, and hence
\[
\angle BYV=\angle BAI=\alpha.
\]
Therefore the points $B,Y,I,V$ are concyclic.

Assume from now on that $I,X,Y$ are collinear. We first prove that
\[
\angle YIA=90^\circ.
\tag{1}
\]
Let
\[
N=XU\cap AB.
\]
We give an affine calculation proving that $X$ is the midpoint of $NU$. Take $A$ as the origin and denote the position vectors of $I$ and $V$ by $\mathbf i$ and $\mathbf v$. These two vectors are linearly independent, since
\[
\angle AIC=90^\circ+\beta\ne90^\circ,
\]
whereas $IV\perp IC$.

For a real number $s$, write
\[
U=(1-s)\mathbf v+s\mathbf i.
\]
Because $X\in AV$ and $UX\parallel AI$, comparison of the coefficients of $\mathbf v$ gives
\[
X=(1-s)\mathbf v.
\tag{2}
\]
Also, $Y$ lies on the line through $V$ parallel to $AI$, and $I,X,Y$ are collinear. These two conditions give
\[
Y=\mathbf v-\frac{s}{1-s}\mathbf i.
\tag{3}
\]
Finally, $N\in AY$ and $NX\parallel AI$. Using (2) and (3), we obtain
\[
N=(1-s)Y=(1-s)\mathbf v-s\mathbf i.
\tag{4}
\]
Consequently,
\[
\overrightarrow{UX}=-s\mathbf i
\qquad\text{and}\qquad
\overrightarrow{XN}=-s\mathbf i.
\]
Thus $X$ is the midpoint of $UN$.

Now
\[
\angle BIU=\alpha=\angle BNU,
\]
so $B,U,I,N$ are concyclic. Since $BI$ bisects $\angle UBN=\angle ABC$, the equal inscribed angles
\[
\angle UBI=\angle IBN
\]
subtend equal chords; hence
\[
IU=IN.
\]
Thus $IUN$ is isosceles, and its median $IX$ is perpendicular to $UN$. But $UN\parallel AI$, and $I,X,Y$ are collinear. Therefore $IY\perp AI$, proving (1).

Let $S$ be the midpoint of $VC$, and let
\[
T=AX\cap SI.
\]
Set
\[
x=\angle BAV=\angle BCV.
\]
The triangle $CIV$ is right-angled at $I$, so its hypotenuse midpoint $S$ satisfies
\[
SI=SC.
\]
Hence
\[
\angle CIS=\angle ICS=\gamma+x.
\]
Using $\angle CIA=90^\circ+\beta$, we get
\[
\angle TIA
=180^\circ-\angle AIS
=90^\circ-\beta-(\gamma+x)
=\alpha-x.
\]
Since $T\in AX\subset AV$,
\[
\angle TAI=\angle VAI=\alpha-x.
\]
Therefore
\[
TI=TA.
\tag{5}
\]
By (1), triangle $AIX$ is right-angled at $I$. Its hypotenuse midpoint is the unique point of $AX$ equidistant from $A$ and $I$. In view of (5), this point is $T$. Thus
\[
T=W.
\tag{6}
\]

It remains to locate the line $IS$. Since $B,Y,I,V$ are cyclic and $IY\perp AI$ while $VY\parallel AI$,
\[
\angle VBI=\angle VYI=90^\circ.
\]
Thus $BV$ is the external angle bisector of $\angle ABC$. Since $V$ lies on the circumcircle of $ABC$, this implies
\[
VA=VC.
\]
Consequently,
\[
\angle VAC=\angle VCA,
\]
or, in terms of $\alpha,\gamma,x$,
\[
2\alpha-x=2\gamma+x.
\]
Hence
\[
\alpha=\gamma+x.
\tag{7}
\]
Because $SI=SC$, equations (7) and $\angle ICS=\gamma+x$ give
\[
\angle SCI=\alpha
\qquad\text{and therefore}\qquad
\angle VSI=2\alpha.
\]
On the other hand,
\[
\angle BVC=180^\circ-\angle BAC=180^\circ-2\alpha.
\]
Since $V,S,C$ are collinear, these two angle relations imply
\[
IS\parallel BV.
\tag{8}
\]
In triangle $VCB$, the line through the midpoint $S$ of $VC$ parallel to $VB$ meets $CB$ at its midpoint $Z$. Thus (8) gives $I,S,Z$ collinear. Together with (6), which says that $W=T\in IS$, this proves that $I,W,Z$ are collinear.

SOLUTION 2:
We first record two facts from the configuration. Put
\[
\alpha=\frac{\angle A}{2},\qquad
\beta=\frac{\angle B}{2},\qquad
\gamma=\frac{\angle C}{2}.
\]
As in Solution 1,
\[
\angle BIV=\alpha=\angle BYV,
\]
so $B,Y,I,V$ are concyclic.

We also need the following consequence of the hypothesis that $I,X,Y$ are collinear. Let $N=XU\cap AB$. Using affine coordinates with origin $A$ and basis vectors $\overrightarrow{AI},\overrightarrow{AV}$, one finds, exactly as in Solution 1, that
\[
UX=XN.
\]
Furthermore,
\[
\angle BIU=\angle BNU=\alpha,
\]
so $B,U,I,N$ are cyclic. Since $BI$ bisects $\angle UBN$, this gives $IU=IN$. Therefore the median $IX$ of the isosceles triangle $IUN$ is perpendicular to $UN\parallel AI$. Because $I,X,Y$ are collinear,
\[
IY\perp AI.
\tag{1}
\]

We now prove a lemma.

\textbf{Lemma.} Let $ABC$ be any triangle with circumcircle $\Gamma$ and incenter $I$. Let the line through $I$ perpendicular to $AI$ meet $AB$ at $Y$, and let the circumcircle of $BYI$ meet $\Gamma$ again at $V\ne B$. If the $A$-excircle touches $BC$ at $E$, then
\[
\angle BAV=\angle CAE.
\tag{2}
\]

\textbf{Proof.} Let $J$ be the $A$-excenter. Consider the transformation $\rho$ obtained by first applying the inversion centered at $A$ with squared radius
\[
AB\cdot AC
\]
and then reflecting across the angle bisector $AI$. This transformation interchanges $B$ and $C$.

The triangles $ACJ$ and $AIB$ are similar, because
\[
\angle JAC=\angle BAI
\qquad\text{and}\qquad
\angle JCA=90^\circ+\gamma=\angle BIA.
\]
Thus
\[
AI\cdot AJ=AB\cdot AC,
\]
so $\rho$ also interchanges $I$ and $J$.

Let $Y'=\rho(Y)$. Then $Y'\in AC$. If $\alpha=\angle A/2$, the right triangle $AIY$ gives
\[
AY=\frac{AI}{\cos\alpha}.
\]
Therefore
\[
AY'
=\frac{AB\cdot AC}{AY}
=\frac{AI\cdot AJ}{AI/\cos\alpha}
=AJ\cos\alpha.
\]
Since $\angle JAC=\alpha$, this means that $Y'$ is the foot of the perpendicular from $J$ to $AC$. Hence the image under $\rho$ of the circumcircle of $BYI$ is the circle with diameter $JC$.

Because $\Gamma$ passes through the center $A$ of the inversion, its image under $\rho$ is the line $BC$. Hence $V'=\rho(V)$ is the second intersection, other than $C$, of $BC$ with the circle having diameter $JC$. But $JE\perp BC$, so that second intersection is precisely $E$. Thus $\rho(V)=E$.

The transformation $\rho$ sends every line through $A$ to its reflection across $AI$. Therefore the line $AV$ is reflected to the line $AE$, while $AB$ is reflected to $AC$. This proves (2). \hfill$\square$

Return to the original problem. Let the incircle of $ABC$ touch $BC$ at $D$, and let the $A$-excircle touch $BC$ at $E$. The homothety centered at $A$ that maps the $A$-excircle to the incircle sends $E$ to a point $F$ on $AE$. The tangent to the incircle at $F$ is parallel to $BC$.

Since the tangent at $D$ is $BC$, the radii $ID$ and $IF$ are opposite. Hence $DF$ is a diameter of the incircle, and $I$ is the midpoint of $DF$. If $s$ is the semiperimeter of $ABC$, then
\[
BD=s-AC,
\qquad
BE=s-AB.
\]
Therefore
\[
BD+BE=BC,
\]
so the midpoint $Z$ of $BC$ is also the midpoint of $DE$. In triangle $DFE$, the segment joining the midpoints $I$ of $DF$ and $Z$ of $DE$ is parallel to $FE$. Consequently,
\[
IZ\parallel FE\parallel AE.
\tag{3}
\]

Let
\[
K=IY\cap AE.
\]
By the lemma,
\[
\angle BAV=\angle CAE,
\]
so the rays $AV$ and $AE$ are symmetric about $AI$. By (1), the line $IY$ is perpendicular to $AI$ and is therefore invariant under this reflection. Since
\[
X=AV\cap IY
\qquad\text{and}\qquad
K=AE\cap IY,
\]
the points $X$ and $K$ are reflections of each other across $AI$. Thus $I$ is the midpoint of $XK$.

Now $W$ is the midpoint of $AX$. In triangle $AXK$, the segment joining the midpoints $W$ of $AX$ and $I$ of $XK$ is parallel to $AK$. Hence
\[
IW\parallel AK\parallel AE.
\tag{4}
\]
Both lines in (3) and (4) pass through $I$, so they coincide. Therefore $I,W,Z$ are collinear.
