ID: 0214

SOURCE: IMO Shortlist

YEAR: 2017

TOPIC: Algebra

SUBTOPICS: Functional Inequalities, Monotonicity, Level Sets, Extremal Intervals

STATEMENT:
Let
\[
f:\mathbb R\to\mathbb R
\]
satisfy the following condition: whenever real numbers $x,y$ obey
\[
\bigl(f(x)+y\bigr)\bigl(f(y)+x\bigr)>0,
\]
we have
\[
f(x)+y=f(y)+x.
\]
Prove that
\[
f(x)+y\le f(y)+x
\]
whenever
\[
x>y.
\]

SOLUTION 1:
Define
\[
g(x)=x-f(x).
\]
The desired conclusion becomes
\[
g(x)\le g(y)
\qquad\text{whenever }x<y,
\tag{1}
\]
so we must prove that $g$ is nondecreasing.

The hypothesis on $f$ has the following equivalent contrapositive form:
\[
g(x)\ne g(y)
\quad\Longrightarrow\quad
x+y\text{ lies between }g(x)\text{ and }g(y).
\tag{2}
\]
Indeed,
\[
f(x)+y=x+y-g(x),
\qquad
f(y)+x=x+y-g(y).
\]
If $g(x)\ne g(y)$, the two expressions are unequal, so their product cannot be positive; hence $x+y$ lies between their two zeros $g(x)$ and $g(y)$.

The transformed function
\[
g^*(t)=-g(-t)
\]
also satisfies (2), and proving (1) for $g^*$ is equivalent to proving it for $g$.

If $g(x)=2x$ for every $x$, then $g$ is increasing and we are done. Otherwise fix $x$ with
\[
X=g(x)\ne2x.
\]

\textbf{Lemma 1.}
If $X<2x$, then on the interval
\[
(X-x,x]
\]
the function $g$ takes at most two values: $X$ and possibly one value $Y>X$.

If $X>2x$, then on
\[
[x,X-x)
\]
it takes at most two values: $X$ and possibly one value $Y<X$.

\textit{Proof.}
We prove the first assertion. Take
\[
a\in(X-x,x)
\]
with $g(a)\ne X$. If $g(a)<X$, then (2) gives
\[
g(a)\le a+x\le X,
\]
so $a\le X-x$, a contradiction. Hence
\[
g(a)>X
\qquad\text{and}\qquad
X\le a+x\le g(a).
\tag{3}
\]

If $a,b\in(X-x,x)$ both satisfy $g(a)\ne X$ and $g(b)\ne X$, then
\[
a+b<\min\{a+x,b+x\}\le\min\{g(a),g(b)\}.
\]
Thus $a+b$ cannot lie between $g(a)$ and $g(b)$ unless
\[
g(a)=g(b).
\]
This proves the first assertion. The second follows by applying the first one to $g^*$. $\square$

\textbf{Lemma 2.}
If $X<2x$, then $g$ is constant on
\[
(X-x,x).
\]
If $X>2x$, then $g$ is constant on
\[
(x,X-x).
\]

\textit{Proof.}
Again prove the first assertion. Suppose that two values occur. By Lemma 1, there are
\[
a,b\in(X-x,x)
\]
such that
\[
g(a)=X,
\qquad
g(b)=Y>X.
\]
Choose
\[
u\in(X-x,x)
\]
so small that
\[
u<\min\{X-a,X-b\}.
\]
Lemma 1 gives
\[
g(u)\in\{X,Y\}.
\]
If $g(u)=X$, then applying (2) to $u,b$ gives
\[
X\le u+b,
\]
contradicting $u<X-b$. If $g(u)=Y$, then applying (2) to $u,a$ gives
\[
X\le u+a,
\]
contradicting $u<X-a$. Thus only one value can occur. The second assertion follows from $g^*$. $\square$

\textbf{Lemma 3.}
If $X<2x$, then
\[
g(a)=X
\qquad(X-x<a<x).
\]
If $X>2x$, then
\[
g(a)=X
\qquad(x<a<X-x).
\]

\textit{Proof.}
We prove the first assertion. By Lemmas 1 and 2, the only other possibility is that
\[
g(a)=Y>X
\]
for every $a\in(X-x,x)$.

Choose
\[
X-x<a<b<x.
\]
Applying (2) to $b,x$ gives
\[
Y\ge b+x.
\tag{4}
\]
In particular,
\[
Y>b+x>2a.
\]
Now apply the second part of Lemma 2 at the point $a$, where $g(a)=Y>2a$. It says that $g$ is constant on
\[
(a,Y-a).
\]
But (4) implies that both $b$ and $x$ lie in this interval, whereas
\[
g(b)=Y\ne X=g(x).
\]
This contradiction proves the first assertion. The second again follows from $g^*$. $\square$

We can now finish the proof. Suppose, for contradiction, that
\[
x<y
\qquad\text{but}\qquad
X=g(x)>g(y)=Y.
\]
By (2),
\[
Y\le x+y\le X.
\tag{5}
\]
Since $x<y$,
\[
X>2x
\qquad\text{and}\qquad
Y<2y.
\]
Lemma 3 therefore gives
\[
g(t)=X
\qquad(x<t<X-x)
\]
and
\[
g(t)=Y
\qquad(Y-y<t<y).
\]
By (5),
\[
Y-y\le x<y\le X-x.
\]
Thus the two open intervals overlap on $(x,y)$, forcing $X=Y$, a contradiction.

Hence $g$ is nondecreasing, which is exactly the desired inequality.

SOLUTION 2:
Again set
\[
g(x)=x-f(x).
\]
As in Solution 1, the hypothesis becomes
\[
g(x)\ne g(y)
\quad\Longrightarrow\quad
x+y\text{ lies between }g(x)\text{ and }g(y).
\tag{1}
\]
We must prove that $g$ is nondecreasing.

If $g$ is constant, the conclusion is immediate. Assume that $g$ takes at least two values.

\textbf{Claim 1.}
For every real number $c$, the level set
\[
\{x:g(x)=c\}
\]
is bounded.

\textit{Proof.}
Choose $y$ with
\[
g(y)\ne c.
\]
If $g(x)=c$, then (1) places $x+y$ between the two fixed numbers $c$ and $g(y)$. Hence $x$ lies in a fixed bounded interval. $\square$

In particular, $g$ takes infinitely many values.

\textbf{Claim 2.}
If
\[
g(u)<g(v)<g(w),
\]
then
\[
u<w.
\]

\textit{Proof.}
From (1),
\[
u+v\le g(v)\le v+w,
\]
so $u\le w$. Equality would give $g(u)=g(w)$, impossible. $\square$

\textbf{Claim 3.}
If
\[
x<y
\qquad\text{and}\qquad
X=g(x)>g(y)=Y,
\]
then
\[
g(a)\in\{X,Y\}
\qquad(x\le a\le y).
\]

\textit{Proof.}
If
\[
Y<g(a)<X,
\]
Claim 2 applied to the points $y,a,x$ gives $y<x$. If
\[
g(a)<Y<X,
\]
Claim 2 applied to $a,y,x$ gives $a<x$. If
\[
Y<X<g(a),
\]
Claim 2 applied to $y,x,a$ gives $y<a$. All three conclusions contradict $a\in[x,y]$. $\square$

Assume, for contradiction, that such a descent $x<y$ with $X>Y$ exists.

Call an interval a \emph{two-value interval} if $g$ takes only the values $X$ and $Y$ on it. Define
\[
r=\inf\left\{
a\le x:
g(t)\in\{X,Y\}\text{ for every }t\in(a,y]
\right\},
\]
and
\[
s=\sup\left\{
b\ge y:
g(t)\in\{X,Y\}\text{ for every }t\in[x,b)
\right\}.
\]
Claim 3 shows that these sets are nonempty. Claim 1 shows that
\[
-\infty<r\le x<y\le s<+\infty.
\tag{2}
\]
Moreover,
\[
g(t)\in\{X,Y\}
\qquad(r<t<s).
\tag{3}
\]

Put
\[
\Delta=\frac{y-x}{2}.
\]

We first prove that
\[
g(t)=X
\qquad(r<t<r+\Delta).
\tag{4}
\]
Suppose instead that some such $t$ satisfies $g(t)=Y$. By the definition of $r$, there exists
\[
r'\in(r-\Delta,r]
\]
with
\[
g(r')\notin\{X,Y\}.
\]
We claim that
\[
g(r')<Y.
\]
Indeed, if $Y<g(r')<X$, Claim 2 applied to $y,r',x$ gives $y<x$. If $X<g(r')$, Claim 2 applied to $y,x,r'$ gives $y<r'\le x$. Both are impossible.

Applying (1) to the pairs $(r',y)$ and $(t,x)$ gives
\[
r'+y\le Y\le t+x.
\]
Using the locations of $r'$ and $t$,
\[
r-\Delta+y<r'+y\le t+x<r+\Delta+x.
\]
Thus
\[
y-x<2\Delta,
\]
contrary to the definition of $\Delta$. This proves (4).

Applying the same argument to
\[
g^*(u)=-g(-u),
\]
we obtain the symmetric conclusion
\[
g(t)=Y
\qquad(s-\Delta<t<s).
\tag{5}
\]

Choose
\[
s-\Delta<s_1<s_2<s
\]
and put
\[
\delta=\frac{s_2-s_1}{2}.
\]
By the definition of $r$, choose
\[
r'\in(r-\delta,r]
\]
with
\[
g(r')\notin\{X,Y\}.
\]
As above,
\[
g(r')<Y.
\]
Choose any
\[
t\in(r,r+\delta).
\]
By (4),
\[
g(t)=X,
\]
and by (5),
\[
g(s_1)=g(s_2)=Y.
\]

Apply (1) to the pairs $(r',s_2)$ and $(t,s_1)$. We get
\[
r'+s_2\le Y\le t+s_1.
\]
Therefore
\[
r-\delta+s_2<r'+s_2\le t+s_1<r+\delta+s_1,
\]
which implies
\[
s_2-s_1<2\delta.
\]
This contradicts the definition of $\delta$.

Hence no descent is possible, and $g$ is nondecreasing. Translating back to $f$ gives
\[
f(x)+y\le f(y)+x
\qquad(x>y).
\]
