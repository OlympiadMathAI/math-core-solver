ID: 0293

SOURCE: IMO Shortlist

YEAR: 2019

TOPIC: Number Theory

SUBTOPICS: Integer Polynomials, Euler Totient Function, Induction, Pigeonhole Principle

STATEMENT:
A set $S$ of integers is called \emph{rootiful} if, for every positive integer $n$ and every
\[
a_0,a_1,\ldots,a_n\in S,
\]
all integer roots of the polynomial
\[
a_0+a_1x+\cdots+a_nx^n
\]
also belong to $S$.

Find all rootiful sets of integers that contain every number of the form
\[
2^a-2^b,
\]
where $a$ and $b$ are positive integers.

SOLUTION 1:
The only such set is
\[
\boxed{\mathbb Z}.
\]

Clearly $\mathbb Z$ itself is rootiful. Let $S$ be any rootiful set containing every number
\[
2^a-2^b
\qquad(a,b\ge1).
\]
We prove that $S=\mathbb Z$.

First,
\[
0=2^1-2^1\in S
\qquad\text{and}\qquad
2=2^2-2^1\in S.
\]
The integer $-1$ is a root of
\[
2x+2,
\]
so $-1\in S$. The integer $1$ is a root of
\[
2x^2-x-1,
\]
so $1\in S$.

If $t\in S$, then $-t$ is a root of
\[
x+t,
\]
whose coefficients belong to $S$. Hence
\[
t\in S\Longrightarrow -t\in S.
\tag{1}
\]
It is therefore enough to prove that every positive integer lies in $S$.

We first show that every positive integer has a positive multiple in $S$. Let
\[
n=2^\alpha u,
\]
where $u$ is odd. Euler's theorem gives
\[
u\mid2^{\varphi(u)}-1.
\]
Therefore
\[
n
\mid
2^{\alpha+\varphi(u)+1}-2^{\alpha+1}.
\]
The number on the right belongs to $S$, so $S$ contains a multiple of $n$.

We now use induction on $n$. Suppose that
\[
0,1,\ldots,n-1\in S.
\]
Choose a positive multiple $N\in S$ of $n$, and write its base-$n$ expansion as
\[
N=d_kn^k+d_{k-1}n^{k-1}+\cdots+d_1n+d_0,
\]
where
\[
0\le d_i<n.
\]
Since $n\mid N$, we have $d_0=0$. By the induction hypothesis, all digits $d_i$ belong to $S$, and by (1), so does $-N$.

Thus $n$ is an integer root of
\[
d_kx^k+d_{k-1}x^{k-1}+\cdots+d_1x-N,
\]
all of whose coefficients lie in $S$. Since $S$ is rootiful,
\[
n\in S.
\]
The induction is complete, so $S$ contains every integer. Therefore
\[
S=\mathbb Z.
\]

SOLUTION 2:
As in Solution 1, every such rootiful set contains
\[
0,\qquad1,\qquad-1.
\]
We prove that every integer $k$ with $|k|>2$ is an integer root of a nonzero polynomial all of whose coefficients have the form
\[
2^a-2^b
\qquad(a,b\ge1).
\]
Together with the numbers already known to lie in $S$, this will prove $S=\mathbb Z$.

It is enough to treat $k>2$. If $k$ is a root of
\[
c_nx^n+\cdots+c_1x+c_0,
\]
then $-k$ is a root of
\[
(-1)^nc_nx^n+\cdots-c_1x+c_0,
\]
and the negative of a number $2^a-2^b$ is again of the same form.

Fix $k>2$. We seek two different exponent vectors
\[
(a_0,a_1,\ldots,a_n)
\qquad\text{and}\qquad
(b_0,b_1,\ldots,b_n)
\]
such that
\[
\sum_{i=0}^{n}2^{a_i}k^i
=
\sum_{i=0}^{n}2^{b_i}k^i.
\tag{1}
\]
Then $k$ is a root of
\[
\sum_{i=0}^{n}(2^{a_i}-2^{b_i})x^i.
\]

For each $i=0,1,\ldots,n$, choose $a_i$ subject to
\[
1\le a_i\le1+\left\lfloor(n-i)\log_2k\right\rfloor.
\]
Every summand then satisfies
\[
2^{a_i}k^i\le2k^n,
\]
so every resulting sum is a positive integer at most
\[
2(n+1)k^n.
\tag{2}
\]

On the other hand, the number of possible exponent vectors is
\[
\prod_{i=0}^{n}
\left(
1+\left\lfloor(n-i)\log_2k\right\rfloor
\right).
\]
Using $1+\lfloor x\rfloor\ge x$, we obtain
\[
\prod_{i=0}^{n}
\left(
1+\left\lfloor(n-i)\log_2k\right\rfloor
\right)
\ge
n!(\log_2k)^n.
\tag{3}
\]
For sufficiently large $n$,
\[
n!(\log_2k)^n>2(n+1)k^n.
\tag{4}
\]
By (2)--(4), there are more exponent vectors than possible values of the corresponding sums. The pigeonhole principle therefore gives two different vectors producing the same sum, as in (1).

The resulting polynomial is nonzero, all of its coefficients are of the form $2^a-2^b$, and it has the integer root $k$. Hence every positive integer $k>2$ belongs to $S$, and so does $-k$.

Thus every integer belongs to $S$, and the unique rootiful set satisfying the condition is
\[
\boxed{\mathbb Z}.
\]
