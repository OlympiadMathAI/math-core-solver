ID: 0051

SOURCE: IMO Shortlist

YEAR: 2011

TOPIC: Number Theory

SUBTOPICS: Divisor Function, Prime Factorization, Extremal Construction

STATEMENT:
For every positive integer $d$, let $f(d)$ be the smallest positive integer having exactly $d$ positive divisors. Prove that, for every integer $k\ge 0$,
\[
f(2^k)\mid f(2^{k+1}).
\]

SOLUTION 1:
For a positive integer $n$, let $\tau(n)$ denote the number of its positive divisors. Write
\[
n=\prod_p p^{a(p)},
\]
where $p$ ranges over the primes, the integers $a(p)$ are nonnegative, and all but finitely many of them are zero. Then
\[
\tau(n)=\prod_p\bigl(a(p)+1\bigr).
\]

Consequently, $\tau(n)$ is a power of $2$ if and only if, for each prime $p$, there is a nonnegative integer $b(p)$ such that
\[
a(p)=2^{b(p)}-1
=1+2+2^2+\cdots+2^{b(p)-1}.
\]
In that case,
\[
n=\prod_p\prod_{r=0}^{b(p)-1}p^{2^r},
\qquad
\tau(n)=2^k,
\qquad
k=\sum_p b(p).
\tag{1}
\]

Let
\[
\mathcal S=\left\{p^{2^r}:p\text{ prime},\ r\ge0\right\}.
\]
Formula (1) associates to $n$ a finite subset
\[
T=\left\{p^{2^r}:0\le r<b(p)\right\}\subset\mathcal S.
\]
This subset has $k$ elements and is downward closed under divisibility: if $t\in T$, $s\in\mathcal S$, and $s\mid t$, then $s\in T$.

Conversely, every finite subset $T\subset\mathcal S$ with this property determines an integer $n=\prod_{t\in T}t$ satisfying
\[
\tau(n)=2^{|T|}.
\]

Let $T_k$ be the set of the $k$ smallest elements of $\mathcal S$. It is downward closed: whenever $p^{2^r}\in T_k$, every element
\[
p,p^2,p^4,\ldots,p^{2^{r-1}}
\]
is smaller and therefore also belongs to $T_k$.

Among all $k$-element subsets of $\mathcal S$, the product is minimized by the set of the $k$ smallest elements. Hence
\[
f(2^k)=\prod_{t\in T_k}t.
\]
Since
\[
T_k\subset T_{k+1},
\]
we immediately obtain
\[
f(2^k)\mid f(2^{k+1}).
\]

SOLUTION 2:
We use the prime-exponent description from Solution 1. Write
\[
f(2^k)=\prod_p p^{\,2^{b(p)}-1},
\qquad
\sum_p b(p)=k,
\tag{1}
\]
where all but finitely many $b(p)$ are zero.

We first record a consequence of the minimality of $f(2^k)$. For any two distinct primes $p,q$ with $b(q)>0$, one has
\[
p^{2^{b(p)}}>q^{2^{b(q)-1}}.
\tag{2}
\]
Indeed, put
\[
m=p^{2^{b(p)}},
\qquad
\ell=q^{2^{b(q)-1}}.
\]
The number
\[
f(2^k)\frac{m}{\ell}
\]
still has exactly $2^k$ divisors: multiplying by $m$ increases $b(p)$ by $1$, while dividing by $\ell$ decreases $b(q)$ by $1$. By the definition of $f(2^k)$,
\[
f(2^k)\frac{m}{\ell}\ge f(2^k),
\]
so $m\ge\ell$. Equality is impossible because $p\ne q$, proving (2).

Now write
\[
f(2^{k+1})=\prod_p p^{\,2^{s(p)}-1},
\qquad
\sum_p s(p)=k+1.
\tag{3}
\]
Since
\[
\sum_p s(p)>\sum_p b(p),
\]
there is a prime $p$ such that
\[
s(p)>b(p).
\tag{4}
\]

Let $q\ne p$ be any prime with $b(q)>0$. Apply (2) first to the exponent pattern $s(\cdot)$ and then to the exponent pattern $b(\cdot)$. We obtain
\[
q^{2^{s(q)}}
>
p^{2^{s(p)-1}}
\ge
p^{2^{b(p)}}
>
q^{2^{b(q)-1}}.
\]
Therefore
\[
s(q)\ge b(q).
\]
For $q=p$, this follows from (4), and for primes with $b(q)=0$ it is automatic. Thus
\[
s(q)\ge b(q)
\qquad\text{for every prime }q.
\]
It follows that every prime exponent in $f(2^{k+1})$ is at least the corresponding exponent in $f(2^k)$:
\[
2^{s(q)}-1\ge2^{b(q)}-1.
\]
Hence
\[
f(2^k)\mid f(2^{k+1}).
\]
