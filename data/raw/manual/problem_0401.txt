ID: 0401

SOURCE: IMO Shortlist

YEAR: 2023

TOPIC: Algebra

SUBTOPICS: Polynomial Sequences, Monotonicity, Extremal Principle, Polynomial Factorization

STATEMENT:
Let $k\ge2$ be an integer. Determine all sequences of positive integers
\[
a_1,a_2,a_3,\ldots
\]
for which there exists a monic polynomial $P$ of degree $k$ with nonnegative integer coefficients such that
\[
P(a_n)=a_{n+1}a_{n+2}\cdots a_{n+k}
\tag{1}
\]
for every integer $n\ge1$.

SOLUTION 1:
The required sequences are precisely the arithmetic progressions of positive integers with nonnegative common difference. More explicitly,
\[
\boxed{a_n=a_1+(n-1)d\quad(n\ge1)}
\]
for some integer $d\ge0$, and the corresponding polynomial is
\[
\boxed{P(x)=(x+d)(x+2d)\cdots(x+kd)}.
\]

Every such sequence and polynomial clearly satisfy (1). We prove the converse.

Because $P$ is monic and has nonnegative coefficients, it is strictly increasing on the positive real axis.

If $(a_n)$ is eventually constant, say equal to $c$, then
\[
P(c)=c^k.
\]
Since
\[
P(x)=x^k+\text{terms with nonnegative coefficients},
\]
this forces
\[
P(x)=x^k.
\]
Equation (1), used backwards, then shows that every $a_n=c$. This is the case $d=0$.

Assume from now on that the sequence is not eventually constant. Comparing (1) for $n$ and $n+1$ gives
\[
\begin{aligned}
a_n<a_{n+1}
&\Longleftrightarrow
a_{n+1}<a_{n+k+1},\\
a_n>a_{n+1}
&\Longleftrightarrow
a_{n+1}>a_{n+k+1},\\
a_n=a_{n+1}
&\Longleftrightarrow
a_{n+1}=a_{n+k+1}.
\end{aligned}
\tag{2}
\]

We first show that $(a_n)$ is nondecreasing. Suppose that
\[
a_{r-1}>a_r
\]
for some $r\ge2$. By (2),
\[
a_r>a_{r+k}.
\]
Starting with $r_0=r$, choose $r_{j+1}$ to be the smallest index greater than $r_j$ such that
\[
a_{r_{j+1}}<a_{r_j}.
\]
Then
\[
r_j<r_{j+1}\le r_j+k.
\]
By minimality,
\[
a_{r_{j+1}-1}>a_{r_{j+1}},
\]
and the values
\[
a_{r_0}>a_{r_1}>a_{r_2}>\cdots
\]
form an infinite strictly decreasing sequence of positive integers, a contradiction.

Thus $(a_n)$ is nondecreasing. If
\[
a_n=a_{n+1}
\]
for some $n$, then (2) and monotonicity give
\[
a_n=a_{n+1}=\cdots=a_{n+k+1}.
\]
Repeating the argument makes the sequence eventually constant, contrary to assumption. Hence
\[
a_n<a_{n+1}
\qquad(n\ge1).
\tag{3}
\]

Write
\[
P(x)=x^k+bx^{k-1}+\text{terms of degree at most }k-2,
\]
where $b$ is a nonnegative integer.

We need a uniform comparison. There exists $A>0$ such that, for every $k$-tuple of positive integers
\[
(c_1,\ldots,c_k),
\]
and every $x\ge A$,

\[
c_1+\cdots+c_k>b
\quad\Longrightarrow\quad
P(x)<\prod_{i=1}^k(x+c_i),
\tag{4}
\]
and
\[
c_1+\cdots+c_k<b
\quad\Longrightarrow\quad
P(x)>\prod_{i=1}^k(x+c_i).
\tag{5}
\]

For (5), there are only finitely many positive integer tuples whose sum is smaller than $b$, and each comparison follows from the coefficient of $x^{k-1}$.

For (4), if $b+1\ge k$, reduce the $c_i$, if necessary, to positive integers $d_i\le c_i$ with
\[
d_1+\cdots+d_k=b+1.
\]
There are only finitely many such tuples, and for each one the product has $x^{k-1}$-coefficient $b+1>b$. If $b+1<k$, simply use
\[
\prod_{i=1}^k(x+c_i)\ge(x+1)^k,
\]
whose $x^{k-1}$-coefficient $k$ is greater than $b$. This proves the uniform comparison.

By (3), $a_n\to\infty$. For sufficiently large $n$, put
\[
c_i=a_{n+i}-a_n
\qquad(1\le i\le k).
\]
Equation (1) reads
\[
P(a_n)=\prod_{i=1}^k(a_n+c_i).
\]
The comparisons (4)-(5) force
\[
\sum_{i=1}^k(a_{n+i}-a_n)=b
\tag{6}
\]
for all sufficiently large $n$.

Subtracting (6) at $n$ from the same equality at $n+1$ gives
\[
a_{n+k+1}-a_{n+1}
=
k(a_{n+1}-a_n).
\tag{7}
\]
Let
\[
d=\min\{a_{n+1}-a_n:n\ge N\},
\]
where $N$ is large enough for (7) to hold, and choose $n\ge N$ attaining this minimum. Equation (7) becomes
\[
\sum_{i=1}^k(a_{n+i+1}-a_{n+i})=kd.
\]
Every summand is at least $d$, so all of them equal $d$. Repeating this argument,
\[
a_{m+1}-a_m=d
\qquad(m\ge n).
\tag{8}
\]

For infinitely many values $x=a_m$, equation (1) and (8) give
\[
P(x)=(x+d)(x+2d)\cdots(x+kd).
\]
Hence this is an identity of polynomials.

Finally, we extend (8) backwards. Suppose the arithmetic progression has already been established from $a_n$ onward. Equation (1) at $n-1$ gives
\[
\prod_{i=1}^k(a_{n-1}+id)
=
\prod_{i=0}^{k-1}(a_n+id).
\]
Since $0<a_{n-1}<a_n$, termwise comparison shows that equality is possible only when
\[
a_{n-1}=a_n-d.
\]
Descending induction proves that the whole sequence is the required arithmetic progression.

SOLUTION 2:
We again prove that every nonconstant solution is an arithmetic progression.

The eventually constant case is immediate: if the tail is constantly $c$, then $P(c)=c^k$, so the nonnegative lower coefficients of $P$ all vanish. Thus $P(x)=x^k$, and (1) forces the whole sequence to be constant.

Assume that the sequence is not eventually constant.

\textbf{Claim 1.}
There are infinitely many indices $n$ such that
\[
a_n\le\min(a_{n+1},\ldots,a_{n+k}).
\tag{2}
\]

Indeed, otherwise every sufficiently large $a_n$ would be larger than at least one of the next $k$ terms. Repeatedly moving to such a smaller later term would produce an infinite strictly decreasing sequence of positive integers.

If
\[
P(x)=x^k,
\]
then at every index satisfying (2), equation (1) and the inequalities $a_{n+i}\ge a_n$ force
\[
a_n=a_{n+1}=\cdots=a_{n+k}.
\]
The recurrence then makes the sequence eventually constant, a contradiction. Therefore
\[
P(x)>x^k
\qquad(x>0).
\tag{3}
\]

\textbf{Claim 2.}
For every $M>0$, all sufficiently large terms satisfy
\[
a_n>M.
\tag{4}
\]

Suppose instead that $a_i\le M$ for infinitely many $i$. For each such $i$,
\[
a_{i+1}\cdots a_{i+k}=P(a_i)\le P(M),
\]
so the $k$-tuple
\[
(a_{i+1},\ldots,a_{i+k})
\]
has only finitely many possible values. Two such tuples must coincide. Since each following term is uniquely determined by the preceding $k$ terms and (1), the sequence is eventually periodic.

Multiply
\[
a_n^k<P(a_n)=a_{n+1}\cdots a_{n+k}
\]
over one full period. By periodicity, the products on the two sides are equal, a contradiction. This proves (4).

Write
\[
P(x)=x^k+bx^{k-1}+Q(x),
\qquad
\deg Q\le k-2.
\]
Choose $M$ so large that
\[
Q(x)<x^{k-1}
\qquad(x>M).
\tag{5}
\]
By Claims 1 and 2, infinitely many indices $n$ satisfy both (2) and $a_n>M$. Call them good indices.

For a good index, if some
\[
a_{n+i}\ge a_n+b+1,
\]
then all the other $k-1$ factors are at least $a_n$, and
\[
P(a_n)=a_{n+1}\cdots a_{n+k}
\ge
(a_n+b+1)a_n^{k-1}
>
a_n^k+ba_n^{k-1}+Q(a_n),
\]
contradicting (5). Hence
\[
a_n\le a_{n+i}\le a_n+b
\qquad(1\le i\le k).
\tag{6}
\]

There are only finitely many possible difference tuples
\[
(a_{n+1}-a_n,\ldots,a_{n+k}-a_n).
\]
Thus, for infinitely many good indices, one fixed tuple
\[
(b_1,\ldots,b_k),
\qquad
0\le b_i\le b,
\]
occurs. For these indices,
\[
a_{n+i}=a_n+b_i
\qquad(1\le i\le k),
\]
and
\[
P(a_n)=\prod_{i=1}^k(a_n+b_i).
\]
Because the values $a_n$ are unbounded, we obtain the polynomial identity
\[
P(x)=\prod_{i=1}^k(x+b_i).
\tag{7}
\]

We now determine the $b_i$. Fix
\[
1\le i<j\le k
\]
and an index belonging to the infinite family above. The term
\[
a_{n+j}=a_n+b_j
\]
is one of the factors in
\[
P(a_{n+i})
=
\prod_{\ell=1}^k(a_n+b_i+b_\ell).
\]
Therefore
\[
a_n+b_j
\mid
\prod_{\ell=1}^k(b_i+b_\ell-b_j).
\]
Since $a_n$ is unbounded, the product on the right must be $0$. Hence
\[
b_j=b_i+b_\ell
\tag{8}
\]
for some $\ell$.

In particular, the tuple $(b_1,\ldots,b_k)$ is nondecreasing.

If $b_1=0$, then at every selected index
\[
a_{n+1}=a_n.
\]
The identity
\[
a_{n+k+1}P(a_n)=a_{n+1}P(a_{n+1})
\]
gives
\[
a_{n+k+1}=a_n.
\]
For every $i$, the integer $a_n$ then divides
\[
P(a_{n+i})=\prod_{\ell=1}^k(a_n+b_i+b_\ell).
\]
Reducing modulo $a_n$ and using unboundedness, we get
\[
b_i+b_\ell=0
\]
for some $\ell$. Since all $b_i$ are nonnegative, every $b_i=0$, so $P(x)=x^k$, contrary to (3). Therefore
\[
b_1>0.
\]

Equation (8) now shows that
\[
b_1<b_2<\cdots<b_k.
\]
For each $j=2,\ldots,k$,
\[
b_j=b_1+b_\ell
\]
for some $\ell\le k-1$, since $\ell=k$ would make the right side larger than $b_k$. The $k-1$ values
\[
b_2,\ldots,b_k
\]
are therefore exactly
\[
b_1+b_1,\ b_1+b_2,\ldots,b_1+b_{k-1}.
\]
Comparing them in increasing order gives
\[
b_i=ib_1
\qquad(1\le i\le k).
\tag{9}
\]

Set
\[
d=b_1.
\]
Then
\[
P(x)=(x+d)(x+2d)\cdots(x+kd),
\]
and infinitely many length-$k+1$ blocks of the sequence are arithmetic progressions with common difference $d$.

Starting from any such block, equation (1) at $n+1$ gives
\[
a_{n+k+1}=a_n+(k+1)d.
\]
Thus the arithmetic progression extends forwards.

For the backward step, put
\[
x=a_{n-1},
\qquad
y=a_n.
\]
Equation (1) at $n-1$ becomes
\[
\prod_{i=1}^k(x+id)
=
\prod_{i=0}^{k-1}(y+id).
\tag{10}
\]
If $x\ge y$, every factor on the left is strictly larger than the corresponding factor on the right, so this is impossible. Hence $x<y$.

If $x>y-d$, every left-hand factor in (10) is larger than its corresponding right-hand factor; if $x<y-d$, every one is smaller. Equality is therefore possible only for
\[
x=y-d.
\]
Thus
\[
a_{n-1}=a_n-d.
\]
The arithmetic progression extends both forwards and backwards through the whole sequence. This proves the classification.
