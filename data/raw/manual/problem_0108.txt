ID: 0108

SOURCE: IMO Shortlist

YEAR: 2013

TOPIC: Geometry

SUBTOPICS: Excircles, Contact Lengths, Arc Midpoints, Circumcenters, Cyclic Quadrilaterals

STATEMENT:
Let the excircle of triangle $ABC$ opposite $A$ touch side $BC$ at $A_1$. Define $B_1$ and $C_1$ analogously. Suppose that the circumcenter of triangle $A_1B_1C_1$ lies on the circumcircle of triangle $ABC$. Prove that triangle $ABC$ is right-angled.

SOLUTION 1:
Let $\Omega$ and $\Gamma$ be the circumcircles of $ABC$ and $A_1B_1C_1$, respectively, and let $Q$ be the center of $\Gamma$. By hypothesis,
\[
Q\in\Omega.
\]
Let $A_0$ be the midpoint of the arc $CB$ of $\Omega$ containing $A$. Define $B_0$ and $C_0$ cyclically.

We first prove a lemma.

\textbf{Lemma.} We have
\[
A_0B_1=A_0C_1.
\]
Moreover, $A,A_0,B_1,C_1$ are concyclic, and $A,A_0$ lie on the same side of $B_1C_1$. The analogous statements hold cyclically.

\textbf{Proof.} If $A=A_0$, then $AB=AC$, so symmetry gives $AB_1=AC_1$, and all the assertions are immediate.

Assume $A\ne A_0$. Since $A_0$ is the midpoint of the arc $CB$ containing $A$,
\[
A_0B=A_0C.
\]
The standard excircle contact-length equalities give
\[
BC_1=CB_1.
\]
Also,
\[
\angle C_1BA_0
=
\angle ABA_0
=
\angle ACA_0
=
\angle B_1CA_0.
\]
Hence
\[
\triangle A_0BC_1\cong\triangle A_0CB_1.
\]
It follows that
\[
A_0C_1=A_0B_1.
\]
The corresponding exterior angles also give
\[
\angle A_0C_1A=
\angle A_0B_1A,
\]
so $A,A_0,B_1,C_1$ are concyclic. In their cyclic order, $AA_0$ and $B_1C_1$ are opposite sides, so $A,A_0$ lie on the same side of $B_1C_1$. This proves the lemma. $\square$

The contact triangle $A_1B_1C_1$ is obtuse-angled; equivalently, its three vertices lie in the interior of a semicircle of $\Gamma$. Relabel cyclically if necessary so that
\[
\angle A_1B_1C_1>90^\circ.
\]
Then the center $Q$ and the vertex $B_1$ lie on opposite sides of $A_1C_1$. From the positions of the contact points on the sides of $ABC$, the points $B$ and $B_1$ also lie on opposite sides of $A_1C_1$. Thus $Q$ and $B$ lie on the same side of $A_1C_1$.

The perpendicular bisector of $A_1C_1$ meets $\Omega$ in two points, one on each side of $A_1C_1$. The point $Q$ lies on this perpendicular bisector because it is the center of $\Gamma$. By the cyclic version of the lemma,
\[
B_0A_1=B_0C_1,
\]
so $B_0$ lies on the same perpendicular bisector; moreover, $B$ and $B_0$ are on the same side of $A_1C_1$. Therefore
\[
Q=B_0.
\tag{1}
\]

The lemma also shows that $A_0$ and $C_0$ lie on the perpendicular bisectors of $B_1C_1$ and $A_1B_1$, respectively. In view of (1),
\[
QA_0
\quad\text{and}\quad
QC_0
\]
are precisely those perpendicular bisectors. Hence reflection about $QA_0$ interchanges $B_1,C_1$, while reflection about $QC_0$ interchanges $A_1,B_1$. Therefore
\[
\begin{aligned}
\angle C_1B_0A_1
&=\angle C_1B_0B_1+\angle B_1B_0A_1\\
&=2\angle A_0B_0B_1+2\angle B_1B_0C_0\\
&=2\angle A_0B_0C_0\\
&=180^\circ-\angle ABC.
\end{aligned}
\tag{2}
\]
The last equality follows from the definitions of the arc midpoints $A_0,C_0$.

On the other hand, the cyclic version of the lemma for the vertex $B$ says that
\[
B,B_0,C_1,A_1
\]
are concyclic. Thus
\[
\angle C_1B_0A_1
=
\angle C_1BA_1
=
\angle ABC.
\tag{3}
\]
Comparing (2) and (3),
\[
\angle ABC=180^\circ-\angle ABC,
\]
so
\[
\angle ABC=90^\circ.
\]
Therefore triangle $ABC$ is right-angled.

SOLUTION 2:
Let $\Gamma$ be the circumcircle of $A_1B_1C_1$, let $Q$ be its center, and let $\Omega$ be the circumcircle of $ABC$. By hypothesis,
\[
Q\in\Omega.
\]

First suppose that $Q$ coincides with a vertex of $ABC$, say $Q=B$. Since $B$ is the center of the circle through $A_1,B_1,C_1$,
\[
BC_1=BA_1.
\]
Let $s$ be the semiperimeter of $ABC$. In terms of the usual excircle contact lengths, this equality is
\[
s-BC=s-AB,
\]
so $AB=BC$ and triangle $ABC$ is isosceles at $B$. Also,
\[
BC_1=B_1C
\]
for every triangle. Since $B$ is the center of $\Gamma$,
\[
BB_1=BC_1=B_1C.
\]
By the isosceles relation $AB=BC$, the analogous contact lengths also give
\[
B_1A=B_1C.
\]
Thus $B_1$ is equidistant from $A,B,C$, so $B_1$ is the center of $\Omega$. Because $B_1\in AC$, the segment $AC$ is a diameter of $\Omega$, and hence
\[
\angle ABC=90^\circ.
\]

We may therefore assume
\[
Q\notin\{A,B,C\}.
\]
We use the following elementary lemma.

\textbf{Lemma.} Let $XYZ$ and $X'Y'Z'$ be triangles satisfying
\[
XY=X'Y',
\qquad
YZ=Y'Z'.
\]
Then:

\begin{enumerate}
\item If $XZ\ne X'Z'$ and
\[
\angle YZX=\angle Y'Z'X',
\]
then
\[
\angle ZXY+
\angle Z'X'Y'=180^\circ.
\]
\item If
\[
\angle YZX+
\angle X'Z'Y'=180^\circ,
\]
then
\[
\angle ZXY=
\angle Y'X'Z'.
\]
\end{enumerate}

\textbf{Proof.} Superimpose the two triangles so that $Y=Y'$ and $Z=Z'$. After reflecting one triangle in $YZ$ if necessary, in the first case $X,X'$ lie on the same side of $YZ$, and in the second case they lie on opposite sides. The angle hypothesis makes $X,Z,X'$ collinear. Also $X\ne X'$. Since
\[
YX=YX',
\]
triangle $XX'Y$ is isosceles, and the two conclusions follow from its equal base angles. $\square$

After a cyclic relabelling, suppose that $Q$ lies in the interior of the arc $AB$ of $\Omega$ not containing $C$. The six triangles
\[
QBA_1,\quad QA_1C,\quad QCB_1,\quad QB_1A,\quad QC_1A,\quad QBC_1
\]
have the same orientation. The point $Q$ cannot be the circumcenter of $ABC$, so not all of $QA,QB,QC$ are equal. Relabel $A$ and $B$ if necessary so that
\[
QC\ne QB.
\]

Apply part 1 of the lemma to triangles $QB_1C$ and $QC_1B$. We have
\[
QB_1=QC_1
\]
because $Q$ is the center of $\Gamma$, and
\[
B_1C=C_1B
\]
by the excircle contact-length equalities. Moreover,
\[
\angle B_1CQ=
\angle C_1BQ,
\]
because these are inscribed angles standing over the same side of chord $QA$ in $\Omega$. Since $QC\ne QB$, the lemma gives
\[
\angle CQB_1+
\angle BQC_1=180^\circ.
\tag{1}
\]

We claim that
\[
QC=QA.
\tag{2}
\]
Suppose instead that $QC\ne QA$. Applying part 1 of the lemma in the same way to triangles $QA_1C$ and $QC_1A$ gives
\[
\angle A_1QC+
\angle C_1QA=180^\circ.
\tag{3}
\]
Adding (1) and (3) yields
\[
\angle A_1QB_1+
\angle BQA=360^\circ,
\]
which is impossible because both angles lie strictly between $0^\circ$ and $180^\circ$. Thus (2) holds.

Now triangles $QA_1C$ and $QC_1A$ have all three corresponding sides equal:
\[
QA_1=QC_1,
\qquad
A_1C=C_1A,
\qquad
QC=QA.
\]
Hence they are congruent, and
\[
\angle A_1QC=
\angle C_1QA.
\tag{4}
\]

Next apply part 2 of the lemma to triangles $QA_1B$ and $QB_1A$. We have
\[
QA_1=QB_1,
\qquad
A_1B=B_1A.
\]
Also, $A$ and $B$ lie on opposite sides of chord $QC$ of $\Omega$, so
\[
\angle A_1BQ+
\angle QAB_1=180^\circ.
\]
The lemma therefore gives
\[
\angle BQA_1=
\angle B_1QA.
\tag{5}
\]

Combining (1) and (5),
\[
\bigl(
\angle B_1QC+
\angle B_1QA
\bigr)
+
\bigl(
\angle C_1QB-
\angle BQA_1
\bigr)
=180^\circ.
\]
This is equivalent to
\[
\angle CQA+
\angle A_1QC_1=180^\circ.
\tag{6}
\]
With the common orientation of the rays around $Q$,
\[
\angle A_1QC_1
=
\angle A_1QC+\angle CQC_1
\]
and
\[
\angle CQA
=
\angle CQC_1+\angle C_1QA.
\]
Equation (4) therefore gives
\[
\angle A_1QC_1=\angle CQA.
\]
Thus (6) becomes
\[
2\angle CQA=180^\circ.
\]
Thus
\[
\angle CQA=90^\circ.
\]
Since $A,B,C,Q$ lie on $\Omega$, the chord $CA$ subtends equal angles at $Q$ and $B$. Therefore
\[
\angle CBA=90^\circ,
\]
and triangle $ABC$ is right-angled.
