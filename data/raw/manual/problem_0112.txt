ID: 0112

SOURCE: IMO Shortlist

YEAR: 2013

TOPIC: Number Theory

SUBTOPICS: Decimal Expansions, Perfect Squares, p-adic Valuations, Infinite Sequences

STATEMENT:
Determine whether there exist an infinite sequence of nonzero decimal digits
\[
a_1,a_2,a_3,\ldots
\]
and a positive integer $N$ such that, for every integer $k>N$, the decimal number
\[
a_ka_{k-1}\cdots a_1
\]
is a perfect square.

SOLUTION 1:
The answer is no.

Assume that such a sequence exists. Let
\[
y_k=a_ka_{k-1}\cdots a_1
\]
be the corresponding $k$-digit integer. For every $k>N$, write
\[
y_k=x_k^2
\]
with $x_k$ a positive integer.

For $k>N$, let $\gamma_k=v_5(x_k)$. We first prove that
\[
2\gamma_k\ge k
\qquad(k>N).
\tag{1}
\]
Suppose instead that $2\gamma_n<n$ for some $n>N$. Since
\[
y_{n+1}=10^n a_{n+1}+y_n,
\]
we have
\[
y_{n+1}
=5^{2\gamma_n}
\left(
2^n5^{n-2\gamma_n}a_{n+1}
+
\frac{y_n}{5^{2\gamma_n}}
\right).
\]
The first term inside the parentheses is divisible by $5$, whereas
\[
\frac{y_n}{5^{2\gamma_n}}
=
\left(\frac{x_n}{5^{\gamma_n}}\right)^2
\]
is not. Hence
\[
v_5(y_{n+1})=2\gamma_n,
\qquad
\gamma_{n+1}=\gamma_n.
\]
Because $2\gamma_n<n<n+1$, the same argument applies repeatedly, giving
\[
\gamma_n=\gamma_{n+1}=\gamma_{n+2}=\cdots=\gamma
\]
for some fixed $\gamma$.

For every $k\ge n$,
\[
(x_{k+1}-x_k)(x_{k+1}+x_k)
=x_{k+1}^2-x_k^2
=y_{k+1}-y_k
=a_{k+1}10^k.
\tag{2}
\]
At least one of $x_{k+1}-x_k$ and $x_{k+1}+x_k$ is not divisible by $5^{\gamma+1}$; otherwise their sum $2x_{k+1}$ would be divisible by $5^{\gamma+1}$, contradicting $v_5(x_{k+1})=\gamma$. Since the product in (2) is divisible by $5^k$, the other factor is divisible by $5^{k-\gamma}$. Therefore
\[
5^{k-\gamma}
\le x_{k+1}+x_k
<2x_{k+1}
<2\cdot10^{(k+1)/2},
\]
because $y_{k+1}<10^{k+1}$. Squaring and rearranging gives
\[
\left(\frac52\right)^k<40\cdot5^{2\gamma},
\]
which is impossible for all sufficiently large $k$. This proves (1).

Now choose
\[
k>\max\left\{\frac N2,2\right\}.
\]
By (1),
\[
\gamma_{2k+1}\ge k+1,
\qquad
\gamma_{2k+2}\ge k+1.
\]
Thus both $y_{2k+1}$ and $y_{2k+2}$ are divisible by $5^{2k+2}$. From
\[
y_{2k+2}-y_{2k+1}=a_{2k+2}10^{2k+1}
\]
it follows that $5\mid a_{2k+2}$. Since $a_{2k+2}$ is a nonzero decimal digit,
\[
a_{2k+2}=5.
\]
Consequently,
\[
(x_{2k+2}-x_{2k+1})(x_{2k+2}+x_{2k+1})
=5\cdot10^{2k+1}
=2^{2k+1}5^{2k+2}.
\]
Set
\[
A_k=\frac{x_{2k+2}}{5^{k+1}},
\qquad
B_k=\frac{x_{2k+1}}{5^{k+1}}.
\]
Then $A_k,B_k$ are positive integers and
\[
(A_k-B_k)(A_k+B_k)=2^{2k+1}.
\tag{3}
\]
Neither $A_k$ nor $B_k$ is even: otherwise the corresponding $x_j$ would be divisible by $10$, making $y_j=x_j^2$ divisible by $10$, contrary to $a_1\ne0$. Hence $A_k$ and $B_k$ are odd. Exactly one of $A_k-B_k$ and $A_k+B_k$ is divisible by $4$, so (3), together with $A_k+B_k>A_k-B_k>0$, yields
\[
A_k-B_k=2,
\qquad
A_k+B_k=2^{2k}.
\]
Therefore
\[
A_k=2^{2k-1}+1
\]
and
\[
\begin{aligned}
x_{2k+2}
&=5^{k+1}(2^{2k-1}+1)\\
&=10^{k+1}2^{k-2}+5^{k+1}\\
&>10^{k+1}.
\end{aligned}
\]
It follows that
\[
y_{2k+2}=x_{2k+2}^2>10^{2k+2},
\]
but $y_{2k+2}$ has exactly $2k+2$ digits and is therefore smaller than $10^{2k+2}$. This contradiction proves that no such sequence exists.

SOLUTION 2:
Again suppose that the required sequence exists, and define
\[
y_k=a_ka_{k-1}\cdots a_1=x_k^2
\qquad(k>N)
\]
as above. Then
\[
y_{k+1}-y_k
=(x_{k+1}-x_k)(x_{k+1}+x_k)
=10^k a_{k+1}.
\tag{1}
\]
Put
\[
p_k=x_{k+1}-x_k,
\qquad
q_k=x_{k+1}+x_k.
\]
Because $a_1\ne0$, neither $x_k$ nor $x_{k+1}$ is divisible by $10$. Hence $p_k$ and $q_k$ cannot both be divisible by $20$. They have the same parity, and (1) is even, so both are even. It follows that at least one of the following holds:
\[
\text{one of }p_k,q_k\text{ is divisible by }5^k,
\tag{2}
\]
\[
\text{one of }p_k,q_k\text{ is divisible by }2^{k-1}.
\tag{3}
\]

We also have
\[
x_{k+1}^2=x_k^2+10^k a_{k+1}>2x_k^2,
\]
because $x_k^2=y_k<10^k$. Thus $x_{k+1}/x_k>\sqrt2$ and consequently
\[
1<\frac{q_k}{p_k}
=1+\frac{2}{x_{k+1}/x_k-1}
<1+\frac{2}{\sqrt2-1}<6.
\tag{4}
\]
If (2) holds, then the two positive numbers $p_k,q_k$, whose ratio is smaller than $6$, satisfy
\[
p_kq_k\ge\frac{5^{2k}}6.
\]
But $p_kq_k=10^k a_{k+1}<10^{k+1}$, which would imply
\[
\left(\frac52\right)^k<60.
\]
This is impossible for all sufficiently large $k$. Therefore, for every sufficiently large $k$, (3) must hold.

For such $k$, the prime factors in (1) can be distributed so that, in some order,
\[
\{p_k,q_k\}
=
\left\{
2^{k-1}5^{r_k}b_k,
\ 2\cdot5^{k-r_k}c_k
\right\},
\tag{5}
\]
where
\[
0\le r_k\le k,
\qquad
b_k,c_k\ge1,
\qquad
b_kc_k=a_{k+1}.
\]
In particular, $1\le b_k,c_k\le9$. Comparing the two quantities in (5) and using (4), we obtain
\[
6>
\frac{2^{k-1}5^{r_k}b_k}{2\cdot5^{k-r_k}c_k}
\ge
\frac1{36}\left(\frac25\right)^k5^{2r_k},
\]
and also
\[
6>
\frac{2\cdot5^{k-r_k}c_k}{2^{k-1}5^{r_k}b_k}
\ge
\frac49\left(\frac52\right)^k5^{-2r_k}.
\]
Hence there are constants $c_1<c_2$ such that
\[
\alpha k+c_1<r_k<\alpha k+c_2,
\qquad
\alpha:=\frac12\log_5\frac52<1,
\tag{6}
\]
for every sufficiently large $k$. It follows that, for a fixed constant
\[
C=c_2-c_1+1-\alpha>0,
\]
we have
\[
(k+1)-r_{k+1}\le k-r_k+C
\tag{7}
\]
for all sufficiently large $k$.

We need a simple congruence.

\textbf{Lemma.} For every positive integer $s$,
\[
5^{s+2^s}\equiv5^s\pmod{10^s}.
\tag{8}
\]

\textbf{Proof.} Euler's theorem gives $5^{2^s}\equiv1\pmod{2^s}$. Therefore
\[
5^{s+2^s}-5^s=5^s(5^{2^s}-1)
\]
is divisible by both $5^s$ and $2^s$, hence by $10^s$. $\square$

From (5), regardless of which of $p_k,q_k$ is the first displayed quantity,
\[
\begin{aligned}
x_{k+1}
&=\frac{p_k+q_k}{2}\\
&=5^{r_k}2^{k-2}b_k+5^{k-r_k}c_k.
\end{aligned}
\]
By (6), $r_k\le k-2$ for all sufficiently large $k$, so
\[
x_{k+1}\equiv5^{k-r_k}c_k\pmod{10^{r_k}}.
\]
After squaring,
\[
y_{k+1}\equiv5^{2(k-r_k)}c_k^2\pmod{10^{r_k}}.
\tag{9}
\]

Choose $K_0$ so that (6), (7), and (9) hold for every $k\ge K_0$. Let $s$ be large, and let $k\ge K_0$ be the least index for which
\[
2(k-r_k)\ge s+2^s.
\]
Such a $k$ exists by (6), and for large $s$ we have $k>K_0$. Define
\[
d=2(k-r_k)-(s+2^s)\ge0.
\]
By the minimality of $k$ and (7),
\[
d\le2C.
\tag{10}
\]
Moreover, (6) gives
\[
2(k-r_k)<\left(\frac2\alpha-2\right)r_k-\frac{2c_1}{\alpha}.
\]
Since $2(k-r_k)>2^s$, this implies $r_k>s$ for all sufficiently large $s$. Hence (9) is valid modulo $10^s$. Using the lemma,
\[
\begin{aligned}
y_{k+1}
&\equiv5^{s+2^s+d}c_k^2\\
&\equiv5^{s+d}c_k^2
\pmod{10^s}.
\end{aligned}
\tag{11}
\]
By (10) and $c_k^2\le81$, the factor $5^dc_k^2$ is bounded independently of $s$. Therefore, for all sufficiently large $s$,
\[
0<5^{s+d}c_k^2<10^{s-1}.
\]
Equation (11) then says that among the last $s$ decimal digits of $y_{k+1}$, the $s$th digit from the right is $0$. That digit is precisely $a_s$, contradicting the assumption that every $a_s$ is nonzero.

Thus the required infinite sequence does not exist.
