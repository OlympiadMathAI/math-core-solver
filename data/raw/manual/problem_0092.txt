ID: 0092

SOURCE: IMO Shortlist

YEAR: 2013

TOPIC: Algebra

SUBTOPICS: Periodic Sequences, Combinatorial Inequalities, Double Counting, Directed Graphs

STATEMENT:
Let $n$ be a positive integer, and let $a_1,a_2,\ldots,a_n$ be positive integers. Extend the sequence periodically by defining
\[
a_{n+i}=a_i
\]
for all $i\ge1$. Suppose that
\[
a_1\le a_2\le\cdots\le a_n\le a_1+n
\tag{1}
\]
and
\[
a_{a_i}\le n+i-1
\qquad (i=1,2,\ldots,n).
\tag{2}
\]
Prove that
\[
a_1+a_2+\cdots+a_n\le n^2.
\]

SOLUTION 1:
We first prove that
\[
a_i\le n+i-1
\qquad (i=1,\ldots,n).
\tag{3}
\]
Suppose otherwise, and let $i$ be the smallest index for which $a_i\ge n+i$. Let $r\in\{1,\ldots,n\}$ be congruent to $a_i$ modulo $n$. By periodicity,
\[
a_{a_i}=a_r.
\]
If $r\ge i$, then monotonicity gives
\[
a_{a_i}=a_r\ge a_i\ge n+i,
\]
contrary to (2). Hence $r<i$. But an integer at least $n+i$ whose residue modulo $n$ belongs to $\{1,\ldots,i-1\}$ must be at least $2n+1$. Therefore
\[
a_i\ge2n+1.
\]
By (1),
\[
a_1+n\ge a_n\ge a_i\ge2n+1,
\]
so $a_1\ge n+1$. Thus index $1$ also violates (3), and the minimality of $i$ forces $i=1$. This is impossible because the residue $r$ would have to satisfy $1\le r<i=1$. Hence (3) is proved.

In particular, $a_1\le n$. If $a_n\le n$, then every $a_i\le n$ and the result is immediate. Assume therefore that $a_n>n$, and let $t$ be the unique index such that
\[
a_1\le\cdots\le a_t\le n<a_{t+1}\le\cdots\le a_n.
\tag{4}
\]
Here $1\le t\le n-1$. Since $1\le a_1\le n$ and (2) with $i=1$ gives $a_{a_1}\le n$, relation (4) implies
\[
a_1\le t.
\]
Consequently, by (1),
\[
a_n\le a_1+n\le n+t.
\tag{5}
\]

For $i=1,\ldots,t$, let
\[
b_i=\#\{j\in\{t+1,\ldots,n\}:a_j\ge n+i\}.
\]
We claim that
\[
a_i+b_i\le n
\qquad (i=1,\ldots,t).
\tag{6}
\]
Indeed, $a_i\le n$, and (2) gives $a_{a_i}\le n+i-1$. If $a_j\ge n+i$, then $a_j>a_{a_i}$, so monotonicity forces $j>a_i$. Hence there are at most $n-a_i$ possible indices $j$, proving $b_i\le n-a_i$.

By (4) and (5), every $a_j$ with $j>t$ is an integer between $n+1$ and $n+t$. Therefore
\[
a_j=n+\sum_{i=1}^t\mathbf 1_{\{a_j\ge n+i\}},
\]
and summing over $j=t+1,\ldots,n$ gives
\[
a_{t+1}+\cdots+a_n=n(n-t)+b_1+\cdots+b_t.
\]
Using (6), we obtain
\[
\begin{aligned}
a_1+\cdots+a_n
&=n(n-t)+\sum_{i=1}^t(a_i+b_i)\\
&\le n(n-t)+nt=n^2.
\end{aligned}
\]

SOLUTION 2:
We use a lattice-cell interpretation. In columns $1,\ldots,n$, shade the bottom $a_i$ cells of column $i$ dark. Thus the total number of dark cells is
\[
a_1+\cdots+a_n.
\]
Let
\[
S=\{(i,j):1\le i\le n,\ 1\le j\le n\}
\]
be the $n\times n$ square formed by the first $n$ rows and columns.

To the left of $S$, start each row $j\in\{1,\ldots,n\}$ at column $-n+1$ and shade its first $a_j$ cells light. Equivalently, the light shape is obtained by reflecting the dark shape across the diagonal and translating it $n$ units to the left. A cell $(i,j)\in S$ is light exactly when
\[
a_j\ge n+i.
\tag{3}
\]

We claim that no cell of $S$ is both dark and light. Suppose such a cell exists in column $i$, and let
\[
h=\min(a_i,n)
\]
be the row of the highest dark cell of that column lying in $S$. If $(i,j)$ is both dark and light, then $j\le h$ and $a_j\ge n+i$. Since the sequence is nondecreasing, $a_h\ge a_j$, so $(i,h)$ is also light.

If $a_i\le n$, then $h=a_i$. By (3), the lightness of $(i,a_i)$ says
\[
a_{a_i}\ge n+i,
\]
contradicting (2).

If $a_i\ge n+1$, then $h=n$. The lightness of $(i,n)$ says $a_n\ge n+i$. From (1), $a_n\le a_1+n$, so $i\le a_1$. Also every term of the period is at least $a_1$, while (2) with $i=1$ gives $a_{a_1}\le n$; hence
\[
a_1\le a_{a_1}\le n.
\]
Since $i\le a_1$, monotonicity gives
\[
a_i\le a_{a_1}\le n,
\]
contradicting $a_i\ge n+1$. The claim follows.

It remains to relate the dark cells above $S$ to the light cells inside $S$. The map
\[
(i,j)\longmapsto(j-n,i)
\tag{4}
\]
sends a dark cell with $j>n$ to a light cell with positive column, and the inverse map sends every such light cell back to a dark cell above $S$. There cannot be a light cell in a column greater than $n$: if such a cell existed, then some $a_j>2n$, hence $a_n>2n$, and the cell $(n,n)$ would be both dark and light, contrary to the claim. Thus (4) is a bijection between the dark cells above $S$ and the light cells inside $S$.

Consequently,
\[
\begin{aligned}
\#\{\text{all dark cells}\}
&=\#\{\text{dark cells in }S\}
 +\#\{\text{light cells in }S\}\\
&\le n^2,
\end{aligned}
\]
because no cell of $S$ receives both colors. Hence $a_1+\cdots+a_n\le n^2$.

SOLUTION 3:
As in Solution 1, we first establish
\[
a_i\le n+i-1
\qquad (i=1,\ldots,n).
\tag{3}
\]
For completeness, if $i$ were the smallest counterexample and $r\in\{1,\ldots,n\}$ were congruent to $a_i$ modulo $n$, then $a_{a_i}=a_r\le n+i-1$ would force $r<i$. Since $a_i\ge n+i$, this would imply $a_i\ge2n+1$, hence $a_1\ge n+1$, contradicting the minimality of $i$ exactly as in Solution 1.

Define
\[
c_i=\max(a_i,i)
\qquad (i=1,\ldots,n),
\]
and extend $c_i$ periodically. Then
\[
c_1\le c_2\le\cdots\le c_n\le c_1+n.
\tag{4}
\]
We also have
\[
c_{c_i}\le n+i-1
\qquad (i=1,\ldots,n).
\tag{5}
\]
Indeed, if $c_i=i$, then $c_{c_i}=c_i=i$. If $c_i=a_i$, let $r\in\{1,\ldots,n\}$ be congruent to $a_i$ modulo $n$. By (3), $a_i\le n+i-1<2n$, so $r$ is either $a_i$ or $a_i-n$. Hence
\[
c_{c_i}=c_r=\max(a_{a_i},r)\le n+i-1
\]
by (2) and (3). Thus the sequence $c_i$ satisfies the same two hypotheses as $a_i$, and in addition
\[
c_i\ge i.
\tag{6}
\]

Place $n$ vertices on a circle, labelled cyclically by $1,2,\ldots,n$. From vertex $i$, draw directed arrows to the vertices carrying the successive labels
\[
i+1,i+2,\ldots,c_i,
\]
where labels are read modulo $n$. Applying the argument that proved (3) to the sequence $c_i$, using (4) and (5), gives $c_i\le n+i-1$. Hence no arrow is a loop and no arrow is repeated. The total number of arrows is
\[
\sum_{i=1}^n(c_i-i).
\tag{7}
\]

We show that no unordered pair of vertices carries arrows in both directions. Suppose $1\le i<j\le n$ and both $i\to j$ and $j\to i$ are drawn. Then
\[
j\le c_i,
\qquad
n+i\le c_j.
\tag{8}
\]
From (4),
\[
n+i\le c_j\le c_n\le c_1+n,
\]
so $i\le c_1$. By (5) with $i=1$, $c_{c_1}\le n$; hence monotonicity gives
\[
c_i\le c_{c_1}\le n.
\]
Now $j\le c_i\le n$, so again by monotonicity and (5),
\[
c_j\le c_{c_i}\le n+i-1,
\]
contradicting the second inequality in (8).

Therefore the number of arrows is at most $\binom n2$. From (7),
\[
\sum_{i=1}^n(c_i-i)\le\binom n2,
\]
so
\[
\sum_{i=1}^n c_i
\le \binom n2+\frac{n(n+1)}2=n^2.
\]
Finally, $a_i\le c_i$ for every $i$, and hence
\[
a_1+\cdots+a_n\le n^2.
\]
