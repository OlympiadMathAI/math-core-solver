ID: 0095

SOURCE: IMO Shortlist

YEAR: 2013

TOPIC: Combinatorics

SUBTOPICS: Bin Packing, Greedy Algorithms, Induction, Extremal Construction

STATEMENT:
Let $n$ be a positive integer. Find the smallest integer $k$ with the following property: given any real numbers $a_1,\ldots,a_d$ such that
\[
a_1+a_2+\cdots+a_d=n
\]
and
\[
0\le a_i\le1\qquad(i=1,2,\ldots,d),
\]
it is possible to partition these numbers into $k$ groups, some of which may be empty, such that the sum of the numbers in each group is at most $1$.

SOLUTION 1:
The answer is
\[
k=2n-1.
\]

For the lower bound, take
\[
d=2n-1,
\qquad
a_1=\cdots=a_{2n-1}=\frac{n}{2n-1}.
\]
Since
\[
2\cdot\frac{n}{2n-1}>1,
\]
no group can contain two of these numbers. Thus at least $2n-1$ groups are necessary.

We now prove that $2n-1$ groups always suffice, by induction on $d$. If $d\le2n-1$, place each number in its own group and leave the remaining groups empty.

Assume $d\ge2n$. Among the first $2n$ numbers, pair them as
\[
(a_1,a_2),(a_3,a_4),\ldots,(a_{2n-1},a_{2n}).
\]
Their total sum is at most $n$, so at least one of these $n$ pairs has sum at most $1$. Suppose, after relabelling, that
\[
a_i+a_j\le1.
\]
Replace $a_i,a_j$ by the single number $a_i+a_j$. The new collection still consists of numbers in $[0,1]$, still has total sum $n$, and has only $d-1$ terms. By the induction hypothesis it can be partitioned into $2n-1$ groups of sum at most $1$. Replacing the merged term by $a_i$ and $a_j$ in the same group gives the required partition of the original collection.

SOLUTION 2:
We prove the stronger statement that the numbers can be divided, in their given order, into $2n-1$ consecutive groups, each of sum at most $1$. If $n=1$, all the numbers may be placed in one group, so assume $n\ge2$. Zero terms may be discarded and inserted back anywhere at the end, so assume all $a_i$ are positive.

Lay consecutive segments of lengths $a_1,\ldots,a_d$ on a segment of length $n$. Let
\[
p_0=0,
\qquad
p_j=a_1+\cdots+a_j
\quad(1\le j\le d),
\]
so $p_d=n$. Mark the $n-1$ integer points
\[
1,2,\ldots,n-1.
\]
Assign a marked point lying at a boundary to the segment on its right. Since every segment has length at most $1$, no segment receives two marked points. Let the distinct segments containing the marked points be
\[
S_{i_1},S_{i_2},\ldots,S_{i_{n-1}},
\qquad
i_1<i_2<\cdots<i_{n-1}.
\]
Now form the consecutive groups
\[
\begin{aligned}
&\{a_1,\ldots,a_{i_1-1}\},\ \{a_{i_1}\},\
\{a_{i_1+1},\ldots,a_{i_2-1}\},\ \{a_{i_2}\},\ \ldots,\\
&\{a_{i_{n-1}}\},\
\{a_{i_{n-1}+1},\ldots,a_d\}.
\end{aligned}
\]
There are $2n-1$ groups, with empty groups allowed.

Every singleton has sum at most $1$. Each remaining group corresponds to segments lying between two consecutive integer marks, or between an endpoint and the nearest integer mark, so its total length is at most $1$. Hence all group sums are at most $1$.

SOLUTION 3:
Again the answer is $2n-1$, with the same lower-bound example as in Solution 1. We prove the upper bound by a greedy construction.

First put every number greater than $1/2$ into a separate group. All remaining numbers are at most $1/2$. Read them in any order and build groups greedily: keep adding unused numbers to the current group until its sum first becomes greater than $1/2$, then begin a new group. Since the final number added is at most $1/2$, every completed group has sum at most $1$. The final group may have sum at most $1/2$.

If the last two groups have total sum at most $1$, merge them. Repeat this if applicable. Let $m$ be the number of groups left. If $m=1$, there is nothing to prove. Otherwise, each of the first $m-2$ groups has sum greater than $1/2$, and the last two groups together have sum greater than $1$. Therefore
\[
n>\frac{m-2}{2}+1=\frac m2.
\]
Thus $m<2n$, and since $m$ is an integer,
\[
m\le2n-1.
\]
Hence $2n-1$ groups always suffice.
