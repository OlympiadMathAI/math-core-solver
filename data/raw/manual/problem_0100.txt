ID: 0100

SOURCE: IMO Shortlist

YEAR: 2013

TOPIC: Combinatorics

SUBTOPICS: Graph Distances, Minimal Covers, Extremal Set Systems, Pigeonhole Principle

STATEMENT:
In a country, some pairs of cities are connected by direct two-way flights, and every city can be reached from every other city by a sequence of flights. The distance between two cities is the minimum number of flights needed to travel from one to the other.

It is known that, for every city, at most $100$ cities are at distance exactly $3$ from it. Prove that no city has more than $2550$ cities at distance exactly $4$ from it.

SOLUTION 1:
For cities $u,v$, let $d(u,v)$ denote their distance, and define
\[
S_i(u)=\{w:d(u,w)=i\}.
\]
Suppose for a contradiction that some city $x$ satisfies
\[
D:=S_4(x),
\qquad
|D|\ge2551.
\]
Let
\[
A=S_1(x).
\]
Call a subset $A'\subseteq A$ substantial if every city of $D$ is at distance $3$ from at least one member of $A'$, equivalently,
\[
D\subseteq\bigcup_{a\in A'}S_3(a).
\]
The set $A$ is substantial. Choose a substantial subset $A^*$ of minimum size, and write
\[
m=|A^*|.
\]

Since
\[
m(101-m)\le2550
\]
for every integer $m$, we have
\[
\frac{2551}{m}>101-m.
\]
The sets $S_3(a)\cap D$, with $a\in A^*$, cover $D$. Hence, by averaging, some $a\in A^*$ satisfies
\[
|S_3(a)\cap D|\ge102-m.
\tag{1}
\]

By hypothesis $|S_3(a)|\le100$. Every city in $S_3(a)$ has distance at most $4$ from $x$, because $d(x,a)=1$. Therefore the set
\[
T=\{c\in S_3(a):d(x,c)\le3\}
\]
consists exactly of the elements of $S_3(a)$ not lying in $D$. From (1),
\[
|T|\le100-(102-m)=m-2.
\tag{2}
\]
If $m=1$, inequality (2) is already impossible. Hence assume $m\ge2$.

We now construct $m-1$ distinct elements of $T$. Let
\[
A_a=A^*\setminus\{a\}.
\]
By the minimality of $A^*$, for each $y\in A_a$ there exists a city $d_y\in D$ that is at distance $3$ from $y$ but not at distance $3$ from any other member of $A^*$. Choose a shortest path
\[
x-y-b_y-c_y-d_y.
\tag{3}
\]
Since $d(x,d_y)=4$, the intermediate cities satisfy
\[
d(x,b_y)=2,
\qquad
d(x,c_y)=3.
\tag{4}
\]

All $2(m-1)$ cities $b_y,c_y$ are distinct. A city $b_y$ cannot equal any $c_z$ because their distances from $x$ are $2$ and $3$. If $b_y=b_z$ for distinct $y,z$, then
\[
x-y-b_z-c_z-d_z
\]
is a shortest four-flight path to $d_z$ passing through $y$, contradicting the defining property of $d_z$. The same argument shows $c_y\ne c_z$ for $y\ne z$.

Fix $y\in A_a$. We have
\[
d(a,y)\le2
\]
through the path $a-x-y$. Also
\[
d(a,d_y)\ge d(x,d_y)-d(x,a)=3.
\]
Equality cannot hold, since then $d_y$ would be at distance $3$ from $a\ne y$. Hence
\[
d(a,d_y)>3.
\]
Along the path
\[
y-b_y-c_y-d_y,
\]
consecutive distances from $a$ differ by at most $1$. The first is at most $2$ and the last is greater than $3$, so one of $b_y,c_y$ has distance exactly $3$ from $a$. By (4), that city also has distance at most $3$ from $x$, and hence belongs to $T$.

As all the $b_y,c_y$ are distinct, this produces $m-1$ distinct elements of $T$, contradicting (2). Therefore
\[
|S_4(x)|\le2550
\]
for every city $x$.
