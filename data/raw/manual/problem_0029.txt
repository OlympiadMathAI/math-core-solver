ID: 0029

SOURCE: IMO Shortlist

YEAR: 2011

TOPIC: Algebra

SUBTOPICS: Divisibility, Extremal Sets, Diophantine Systems

STATEMENT:
For a set
\[
A=\{a_1,a_2,a_3,a_4\}
\]
of four distinct positive integers, let
\[
s_A=a_1+a_2+a_3+a_4,
\]
and let $p_A$ be the number of pairs $(i,j)$ with $1\le i<j\le4$ for which
\[
a_i+a_j\mid s_A.
\]
Among all such sets $A$, determine those for which $p_A$ is maximal.

SOLUTION 1:
The maximal value is
\[
\boxed{4}.
\]
It is attained exactly by the sets
\[
\boxed{\{d,5d,7d,11d\}}
\qquad\text{and}\qquad
\boxed{\{d,11d,19d,29d\}},
\]
where $d$ is any positive integer.

Order the elements as
\[
a_1<a_2<a_3<a_4.
\]
For any pair of indices $\{i,j\}$, let $\{k,\ell\}$ be the complementary pair. Then
\[
a_i+a_j\mid s_A
\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad
 a_i+a_j\mid a_k+a_\ell.
\tag{1}
\]
Now
\[
a_2+a_4>a_1+a_3
\qquad\text{and}\qquad
 a_3+a_4>a_1+a_2.
\]
Hence neither $a_2+a_4$ nor $a_3+a_4$ can divide its positive complementary sum. Thus at least two of the six pairs fail, and
\[
p_A\le4.
\tag{2}
\]

Assume that equality holds. Then the four remaining pairs all work. In particular,
\[
a_1+a_4=a_2+a_3,
\tag{3}
\]
because each of these two equal-sum candidates divides the other. Moreover, there are integers $m,n\ge2$ such that
\[
m(a_1+a_2)=a_3+a_4,
\qquad
n(a_1+a_3)=a_2+a_4.
\tag{4}
\]
Using (3), a direct comparison gives
\[
\begin{aligned}
&(a_3+a_4)(a_1+a_3)-(a_2+a_4)(a_1+a_2)\\
&\qquad=2(a_3-a_2)(a_2+a_3)>0,
\end{aligned}
\]
so
\[
m>n.
\tag{5}
\]

From (3),
\[
a_4=a_2+a_3-a_1.
\]
Substituting this into the second equation of (4),
\[
n(a_1+a_3)=2a_2+a_3-a_1.
\tag{6}
\]
If $n\ge3$, then
\[
n(a_1+a_3)>3a_3>2a_2+a_3>2a_2+a_3-a_1,
\]
contradicting (6). Therefore
\[
n=2.
\tag{7}
\]
Equation (6) now becomes
\[
3a_1+a_3=2a_2.
\tag{8}
\]
On the other hand, substituting (3) into the first equation of (4) gives
\[
(m+1)a_1+(m-1)a_2=2a_3.
\tag{9}
\]
Twice (8), together with (9), yields
\[
(m+7)a_1=(5-m)a_2.
\tag{10}
\]
Since $a_1,a_2>0$ and $m>n=2$, relation (10) forces
\[
m\in\{3,4\}.
\]

If $m=3$, then (10) gives $a_2=5a_1$. Equations (8) and (3) then give
\[
a_3=7a_1,
\qquad
a_4=11a_1.
\]
Thus
\[
A=\{d,5d,7d,11d\}.
\]

If $m=4$, then (10) gives $a_2=11a_1$, and similarly
\[
a_3=19a_1,
\qquad
a_4=29a_1.
\]
Thus
\[
A=\{d,11d,19d,29d\}.
\]

Finally, the pair sums in the first family are
\[
6d,8d,12d,12d,16d,18d
\]
and $s_A=24d$, while those in the second family are
\[
12d,20d,30d,30d,40d,48d
\]
and $s_A=60d$. In each case exactly four pair sums divide $s_A$. Hence these and only these sets maximize $p_A$.
