ID: 0109

SOURCE: IMO Shortlist

YEAR: 2013

TOPIC: Number Theory

SUBTOPICS: Divisibility, Functional Equations, Inequality Bounds, Prime Substitution

STATEMENT:
Let $\mathbb{Z}_{>0}$ be the set of positive integers. Find all functions
\[
f:\mathbb{Z}_{>0}\to\mathbb{Z}_{>0}
\]
such that
\[
m^2+f(n)\mid m f(m)+n
\tag{1}
\]
for all positive integers $m,n$.

SOLUTION 1:
The only solution is
\[
f(n)=n\qquad(n\in\mathbb{Z}_{>0}).
\]

Set $m=n=2$ in (1). Then
\[
4+f(2)\mid 2f(2)+2.
\]
The right-hand side is positive and satisfies
\[
2f(2)+2<2\bigl(4+f(2)\bigr).
\]
Hence it must equal $4+f(2)$; otherwise it would be either smaller than the divisor or at least twice the divisor. Thus
\[
2f(2)+2=4+f(2),
\]
so
\[
f(2)=2.
\tag{2}
\]

Now put $m=2$ in (1). Using (2), we get
\[
4+f(n)\mid 4+n,
\]
and therefore
\[
f(n)\le n
\tag{3}
\]
for every positive integer $n$.

Next put $m=n$ in (1). We obtain
\[
n^2+f(n)\mid nf(n)+n.
\]
Since all quantities are positive,
\[
nf(n)+n\ge n^2+f(n),
\]
which is equivalent to
\[
(n-1)(f(n)-n)\ge0.
\]
Consequently $f(n)\ge n$ for every $n\ge2$. Together with (3), this gives $f(n)=n$ for every $n\ge2$. For $n=1$, (3) and positivity give $f(1)=1$ as well.

Finally, the function $f(n)=n$ satisfies (1), because
\[
m^2+f(n)=m^2+n=m f(m)+n.
\]

SOLUTION 2:
We again prove that the only solution is $f(n)=n$.

Fix a positive integer $n$ and substitute $m=f(n)$ in (1). Then
\[
f(n)^2+f(n)
\mid
f(n)f(f(n))+n.
\]
The divisor and the first term on the right are both divisible by $f(n)$, so
\[
f(n)\mid n
\tag{2}
\]
for every positive integer $n$.

Now fix an arbitrary positive integer $m$. Choose a prime $p$ so large that
\[
p>2m^2
\qquad\text{and}\qquad
p>m f(m).
\]
Set
\[
n=p-m f(m),
\]
which is positive. Equation (1) gives
\[
m^2+f(n)
\mid
m f(m)+n=p.
\]
Since $m^2+f(n)>1$ and $p$ is prime, we must have
\[
m^2+f(n)=p,
\]
so
\[
f(n)=p-m^2.
\tag{3}
\]
By (2),
\[
p-m^2\mid p-m f(m).
\]
Moreover,
\[
0<p-m f(m)<p<2(p-m^2),
\]
where the last inequality follows from $p>2m^2$. Therefore the positive multiple $p-m f(m)$ of $p-m^2$ is smaller than twice $p-m^2$, and hence
\[
p-m f(m)=p-m^2.
\]
Thus $f(m)=m$. Since $m$ was arbitrary, $f$ is the identity function, which clearly works.

SOLUTION 3:
Put $m=1$ in (1). Since a positive divisor cannot exceed the positive number it divides,
\[
1+f(n)\le f(1)+n.
\]
Hence
\[
f(n)\le n+c,
\qquad
c:=f(1)-1\ge0.
\tag{2}
\]

Suppose, for contradiction, that $f(n)\ne n$ for some fixed positive integer $n$. Choose $m$ so large that
\[
m\ge n,
\qquad
m\ge c+1,
\qquad
m>|f(n)-n|.
\]
Using (2),
\[
\begin{aligned}
m f(m)+n
&\le m(m+c)+n\\
&\le 2m^2\\
&<2\bigl(m^2+f(n)\bigr).
\end{aligned}
\]
By (1), the positive integer $m f(m)+n$ is a multiple of $m^2+f(n)$, so the above strict upper bound forces
\[
m f(m)+n=m^2+f(n).
\]
Therefore
\[
f(n)-n=m(f(m)-m).
\tag{3}
\]
The left-hand side of (3) is nonzero and has absolute value smaller than $m$, whereas the right-hand side is divisible by $m$. This is impossible.

Thus $f(n)=n$ for every positive integer $n$, and this function satisfies the required divisibility.
