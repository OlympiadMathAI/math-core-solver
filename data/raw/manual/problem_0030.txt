ID: 0030

SOURCE: IMO Shortlist

YEAR: 2011

TOPIC: Algebra

SUBTOPICS: Exponential Sums, Sequences, Linear Independence

STATEMENT:
Determine all sequences
\[
(x_1,x_2,\ldots,x_{2011})
\]
of positive integers such that, for every positive integer $n$, there exists an integer $a$ satisfying
\[
x_1^n+2x_2^n+\cdots+2011x_{2011}^n=a^{n+1}+1.
\tag{1}
\]

SOLUTION 1:
Put
\[
K=2+3+\cdots+2011=2023065.
\]
The sequence
\[
\boxed{(x_1,x_2,\ldots,x_{2011})=(1,K,K,\ldots,K)}
\]
works, because for every $n\ge1$,
\[
1^n+\sum_{i=2}^{2011}iK^n
=1+K\cdot K^n
=K^{n+1}+1.
\]
We prove that there is no other sequence.

For each $n$, choose a positive integer $y_n$ such that
\[
\sum_{i=1}^{2011}i x_i^n=y_n^{n+1}+1.
\tag{2}
\]
We may take $y_n>0$, since the left side is greater than $1$. Let
\[
C=\sum_{i=1}^{2011}i x_i.
\]
Then
\[
\sum_{i=1}^{2011}i x_i^n<C^{n+1},
\]
so (2) implies
\[
y_n<C.
\]
Thus the sequence $(y_n)$ is bounded. Consequently, some positive integer $y$ occurs as $y_n$ for infinitely many values of $n$. Clearly $y>1$.

Let
\[
m=\max\{x_1,x_2,\ldots,x_{2011}\}.
\]
For $1\le j\le m$, define
\[
c_j=\sum_{\substack{1\le i\le2011\\x_i=j}}i.
\]
Then $c_j\ge0$, $c_m>0$, and
\[
\sum_{j=1}^m c_j=1+2+\cdots+2011=1+K.
\tag{3}
\]
For infinitely many $n$, equation (2) becomes
\[
c_1+c_2 2^n+\cdots+c_m m^n=1+y\,y^n.
\tag{4}
\]

We use the following lemma.

\textbf{Lemma.} Let $b_1,\ldots,b_N$ be integers. If
\[
b_1+b_2 2^n+\cdots+b_NN^n=0
\]
for arbitrarily large positive integers $n$, then
\[
b_1=b_2=\cdots=b_N=0.
\]

Indeed, otherwise choose the largest $N$ with $b_N\ne0$. If $N=1$, the assertion is immediate. For $N\ge2$, division by $N^n$ gives
\[
|b_N|
\le
\left(|b_1|+\cdots+|b_{N-1}|\right)
\left(\frac{N-1}{N}\right)^n,
\]
which is impossible for arbitrarily large $n$.

Apply the lemma to (4), after moving the right side to the left and adjoining zero coefficients if necessary. Since $c_m>0$, the largest occurring base must be the same on both sides, so
\[
y=m.
\]
The lemma then gives
\[
c_1=1,
\qquad
c_m=m,
\qquad
c_j=0\quad(1<j<m).
\tag{5}
\]
Because $c_1=1$, the only index with $x_i=1$ is $i=1$. Hence every $x_i$ with $i\ge2$ equals $m$. Using (3) and (5),
\[
1+m=1+K,
\]
so
\[
m=K.
\]
Therefore the unique sequence is
\[
(1,K,K,\ldots,K),
\qquad K=2023065.
\]
