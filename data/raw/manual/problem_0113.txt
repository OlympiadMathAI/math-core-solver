ID: 0113

SOURCE: IMO Shortlist

YEAR: 2013

TOPIC: Number Theory

SUBTOPICS: Impartial Games, Coprimality, Prime Divisors, Recursive Winning Positions

STATEMENT:
Fix an integer $k\ge2$. Ana and Banana play the following game. Initially an integer $n\ge k$ is written on a blackboard. The players move alternately, with Ana moving first. A move replaces the current number $m$ by an integer $m'$ satisfying
\[
k\le m'<m
\qquad\text{and}\qquad
\gcd(m,m')=1.
\]
The first player who cannot move loses.

An integer $n\ge k$ is called \emph{good} if Banana has a winning strategy when the initial number is $n$, and \emph{bad} otherwise.

Let $n,n'\ge k$ be such that, for every prime $p\le k$,
\[
p\mid n\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad p\mid n'.
\]
Prove that either both $n,n'$ are good or both are bad.

SOLUTION 1:
Because the number on the board strictly decreases after every move, every game terminates. Thus every position is either good or bad, and
\[
\begin{aligned}
n\text{ is bad}
&\Longleftrightarrow
\text{there is a legal move from }n\text{ to a good number},\\
n\text{ is good}
&\Longleftrightarrow
\text{every legal move from }n\text{ leads to a bad number}.
\end{aligned}
\tag{1}
\]
In particular, any two good numbers have a nontrivial common divisor. Indeed, if two good numbers were coprime, the larger one could be moved to the smaller one, contradicting (1). Also, $k$ is good because no legal move is possible from $k$.

We write $n\longrightarrow x$ when $n>x\ge k$ and $\gcd(n,x)=1$.

\textbf{Claim 1.} If $n$ is good and $n'$ is a multiple of $n$, then $n'$ is good.

\textbf{Proof.} If $n'$ were bad, then by (1) there would be a move $n'\longrightarrow x$ to some good number $x$. Since $n\mid n'$, we would have $\gcd(n,x)=1$, contradicting the fact that two good numbers cannot be coprime. $\square$

\textbf{Claim 2.} If $rs\ge k$ is bad, then $r^2s$ is bad.

\textbf{Proof.} Since $rs$ is bad, there is a move $rs\longrightarrow x$ to a good number $x$. Then $x<rs\le r^2s$ and $\gcd(x,r^2s)=1$, so $r^2s\longrightarrow x$ is also a legal move to a good number. Hence $r^2s$ is bad. $\square$

\textbf{Claim 3.} If $p>k$ is prime and $n\ge k$ is bad, then $np$ is bad.

\textbf{Proof.} Suppose not, and choose a counterexample with $n$ as small as possible. Thus $n$ is bad but $np$ is good. Since $n$ is bad, there is a move $n\longrightarrow x$ to a good number $x$. The move $np\longrightarrow x$ cannot be legal, because a good number cannot move to a good number. Since $\gcd(n,x)=1$, it follows that $p\mid x$. Write
\[
x=p^r y,
\qquad r\ge1,
\qquad p\nmid y.
\]
We cannot have $y=1$, because then $x=p^r>k$ and $x\longrightarrow k$ would be a legal move to the good number $k$, making $x$ bad. Thus $y>1$.

Let $\alpha$ be the least positive integer for which $y^\alpha\ge k$. The numbers $np$ and $y^\alpha$ are coprime. Since $np$ is good, $y^\alpha$ cannot be good; hence it is bad. By the minimality of $\alpha$,
\[
y^\alpha<ky<py=\frac{x}{p^{r-1}}<\frac{n}{p^{r-1}},
\]
so
\[
p^{r-1}y^\alpha<n.
\tag{2}
\]
By the minimal choice of $n$, the assertion of Claim 3 is valid for every smaller bad starting number. Starting from the bad number $y^\alpha$ and applying this fact repeatedly, (2) shows that
\[
y^\alpha,
\ py^\alpha,
\ \ldots,
\ p^r y^\alpha
\]
are all bad. But $x=p^r y$ is good and divides $p^r y^\alpha$, so Claim 1 says that $p^r y^\alpha$ is good. This contradiction proves Claim 3. $\square$

Call two integers $a,b\ge k$ \emph{similar} if they are divisible by exactly the same primes not exceeding $k$. We must prove that similar numbers have the same status.

If $a$ and $b$ are similar, then $ab$ is similar to each of them. Hence it is enough to prove the following special case: if $c\ge k$ is similar to a multiple $d$ of $c$, then $c$ and $d$ have the same status.

Suppose otherwise, and choose a counterexample $(c_0,d_0)$ with $d_0$ minimal. By Claim 1, the only possible status pattern is
\[
c_0\text{ bad},
\qquad d_0\text{ good}.
\]
Since $d_0>c_0$, choose a prime divisor $p$ of $d_0/c_0$.

If $p\le k$, similarity gives $p\mid c_0$, so $p^2\mid d_0$. If $d_0/p$ were bad, Claim 2, applied with $r=p$ and $s=d_0/p^2$, would imply that $d_0$ is bad. Thus $d_0/p$ is good. Moreover, $c_0\mid d_0/p$, and $d_0/p$ is still similar to $c_0$. This contradicts the minimality of $d_0$.

Therefore $p>k$. If $d_0/p$ were bad, Claim 3 would imply that $d_0$ is bad. Hence $d_0/p$ is good. As before, $c_0\mid d_0/p$, the two numbers are similar, and minimality is contradicted.

Thus similar numbers always have the same status.

SOLUTION 2:
The game always terminates because the number on the board strictly decreases. Hence a position is bad exactly when it has a legal move to a good position, while it is good exactly when every legal move leads to a bad position. Consequently, any two good numbers have a nontrivial common divisor: otherwise the larger could be moved to the smaller. Also, $k$ is good because no move is possible from it.

Call a prime \emph{small} if it is at most $k$, and \emph{big} otherwise. Call two integers at least $k$ \emph{similar} if their sets of small prime divisors are equal.

\textbf{Claim.} If an integer $b\ge k$ has a small prime divisor, then there exists an integer $x$ such that
\[
k\le x\le b,
\]
$x$ is similar to $b$, and $x$ has no big prime divisor.

\textbf{Proof.} If $b$ has no big prime divisor, take $x=b$.

Otherwise, let $p$ be a small prime divisor of $b$, let $q$ be a big prime divisor of $b$, and let
\[
a=\prod_{\substack{\ell\mid b\\ \ell\text{ small prime}}}\ell
\]
be the product of the distinct small prime divisors of $b$. Let $t$ be the least nonnegative integer such that $x=p^t a\ge k$. Then $x$ has exactly the same small prime divisors as $b$ and has no big prime divisor.

If $t=0$, then $x=a<b$ because $q\mid b$. If $t>0$, minimality gives $x<pk$. Since $p\le a$ and $k<q$,
\[
x<pk\le ak<aq\le b.
\]
Thus $k\le x\le b$, as required. $\square$

Assume, for contradiction, that there exist similar integers $a,b\ge k$ such that $a$ is bad and $b$ is good. Choose such a pair with $\max\{a,b\}$ as small as possible.

Because $a$ is bad, there is a legal move $a\longrightarrow r$ to a good number $r$. The two good numbers $k$ and $r$ have a common prime divisor. Every prime divisor of $k$ is small, so $r$ has a small prime divisor. By the claim, there exists an integer $r'$ such that
\[
k\le r'\le r,
\]
$r'$ is similar to $r$, and $r'$ has no big prime divisor.

Since
\[
\max\{r,r'\}=r<a\le\max\{a,b\},
\]
the minimality of $(a,b)$ implies that $r'$ has the same status as $r$. Hence $r'$ is good.

The good numbers $r'$ and $b$ have a common prime divisor $p$. Because $r'$ has no big prime divisor, $p$ is small. Similarity of $a$ and $b$ gives $p\mid a$, and similarity of $r$ and $r'$ gives $p\mid r$. This contradicts the legality of the move $a\longrightarrow r$, which requires $\gcd(a,r)=1$.

Therefore no similar pair can have different statuses, and the result follows.
