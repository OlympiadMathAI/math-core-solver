ID: 0394

SOURCE: IMO Shortlist

YEAR: 2022

TOPIC: Number Theory

SUBTOPICS: Cyclic Orderings, Quadratic Congruences, Vieta's Formulas, Induction

STATEMENT:
Let $k$ be a positive integer, and let $S$ be a finite set of odd prime numbers.

Prove that, up to rotation and reflection, there is at most one way to arrange the elements of $S$ around a circle such that the product of every two neighbouring primes has the form
\[
x^2+x+k
\]
for some positive integer $x$.

SOLUTION 1:
We prove the stronger statement in which
\[
x\ge0
\]
is allowed.

Call an unordered pair of distinct primes
\[
\{p,q\}
\]
\emph{special} if
\[
pq=x^2+x+k
\tag{1}
\]
for some nonnegative integer $x$.

We need the following claim.

\textbf{Claim.}
Let $r$ be a prime.

\begin{enumerate}
\item[(a)] At most two primes smaller than $r$ form a special pair with $r$.
\item[(b)] If two such primes $p,q$ exist, then $\{p,q\}$ is itself special.
\end{enumerate}

\textit{Proof 1 of the claim.}
If a prime $p<r$ forms a special pair with $r$, then
\[
pr=x^2+x+k
\]
for a uniquely determined integer
\[
0\le x<r.
\]
In particular,
\[
x^2+x+k\equiv0\pmod r.
\tag{2}
\]
The quadratic congruence (2) has at most two solutions modulo $r$, proving part (a).

Now suppose
\[
p<q<r
\]
and
\[
pr=x^2+x+k,\qquad
qr=y^2+y+k
\tag{3}
\]
with $0\le x<y\le r-1$. The numbers $x$ and $y$ are the two roots of (2). By Vieta's formulas,
\[
x+y\equiv-1\pmod r.
\]
Since
\[
0<x+y<2r-1,
\]
we obtain
\[
x+y=r-1.
\tag{4}
\]

Put
\[
K=4k-1,\qquad
X=2x+1,\qquad
Y=2y+1.
\]
Then
\[
4pr=X^2+K,\qquad
4qr=Y^2+K,
\qquad
X+Y=2r.
\]
Multiplying the first two equations and using
\[
(X^2+K)(Y^2+K)
=
(XY-K)^2+K(X+Y)^2,
\]
we get
\[
4pq
=
\left(\frac{XY-K}{2r}\right)^2+K.
\tag{5}
\]
The square of the rational number in (5) is an integer, so the rational number itself is an integer. Let
\[
W=
\left|
\frac{XY-K}{2r}
\right|.
\]
Modulo $4$, equation (5) gives
\[
W^2\equiv1\pmod4,
\]
so $W$ is odd. Taking
\[
z=\frac{W-1}{2}\ge0,
\]
equation (5) becomes
\[
pq=z^2+z+k.
\]
Thus $\{p,q\}$ is special. This proves part (b). $\square$

For completeness, we give a second proof of part (b).

\textit{Proof 2 of part (b).}
Subtracting the two equations in (3) gives
\[
(x+y+1)(x-y)=r(p-q).
\]
By (4),
\[
x-y=p-q.
\]
Therefore
\[
x=\frac{r+p-q-1}{2},
\qquad
y=\frac{r+q-p-1}{2}.
\]
Substituting into
\[
k=pr-x^2-x
\]
gives
\[
k=
\frac{
2pq+2pr+2qr-p^2-q^2-r^2+1
}{4},
\tag{6}
\]
an expression symmetric in $p,q,r$.

Consequently,
\[
pq=z^2+z+k,
\qquad
z=\frac{p+q-r-1}{2}.
\]
If $z\ge0$, this proves the result. If $z<0$, replace $z$ by
\[
-z-1\ge0,
\]
which leaves $z^2+z$ unchanged. Thus $\{p,q\}$ is again special. $\square$

We now prove the theorem by induction on
\[
|S|.
\]
For $|S|\le3$, the assertion is immediate.

Assume the result for sets of $n$ primes, and let
\[
|S|=n+1.
\]
Let $r$ be the largest prime in $S$. In any valid circular arrangement, the two neighbours of $r$ are primes smaller than $r$ which form special pairs with it. By part (a) of the claim, these two neighbours are uniquely determined.

Remove $r$ from the circle. By part (b), its two former neighbours form a special pair with each other, so the remaining primes form a valid circular arrangement. By the induction hypothesis, that smaller arrangement is unique up to rotation and reflection. The uniquely determined edge joining the two neighbours of $r$ is then split by inserting $r$, which is also unique.

Therefore the original circular arrangement is unique up to rotation and reflection. This proves the result.
