ID: 0419

SOURCE: IMO Shortlist

YEAR: 2023

TOPIC: Number Theory

SUBTOPICS: Exponential Diophantine Equations, Prime Valuations, Factorization, Growth Estimates

STATEMENT:
Determine all pairs $(a,p)$ of positive integers, with $p$ prime, such that
\[
p^a+a^4
\]
is a perfect square.

SOLUTION 1:
The answer is
\[
\boxed{(a,p)\in\{(1,3),(2,3),(6,3),(9,3)\}}.
\]

Suppose
\[
p^a+a^4=b^2
\]
for some positive integer $b$. Then $b>a^2$ and
\[
p^a=(b-a^2)(b+a^2).
\]
Both factors are positive powers of $p$. Hence, for some integer $x$ with
\[
0\le x<a-x,
\]
we have
\[
b-a^2=p^x,
\qquad
b+a^2=p^{a-x}.
\]
Subtracting,
\[
2a^2=p^x\left(p^{a-2x}-1\right).
\tag{1}
\]

\textbf{Case 1: $p=2$.}

The case $x=0$ is impossible by parity in (1), so $x\ge1$. Since
\[
2^{a-2x}-1
\]
is odd, (1) gives
\[
x-1=2v_2(a)
\]
and
\[
2^{a-2x}-1
\]
is a square.

If $v_2(a)>0$, then $a-2x$ is even, so $2^{a-2x}$ is also a square. Two consecutive nonnegative squares can differ by $1$ only when they are $0$ and $1$, which would force $a-2x=0$, contrary to $x<a-x$.

If $v_2(a)=0$, then $x=1$ and
\[
a^2=2^{a-2}-1.
\]
For $a\ge4$, the right-hand side is congruent to $3$ modulo $4$, and the remaining small values do not work. Thus there is no solution with $p=2$.

\textbf{Case 2: $p$ is odd.}

Put
\[
m=v_p(a),
\qquad
a=p^m u,
\qquad
p\nmid u.
\]
Taking $p$-adic valuations in (1) gives
\[
x=2m.
\]
After cancelling $p^{2m}$,
\[
2u^2+1=p^{p^m u-4m}.
\tag{2}
\]

Suppose first that $p\ge5$.

If $m=0$, equation (2) becomes
\[
2u^2+1=p^u\ge5^u,
\]
which is impossible because
\[
5^u>2u^2+1
\qquad(u\ge1).
\]
This inequality follows by induction, starting from $u=1$.

If $m\ge1$, then
\[
p^m-4m\ge1.
\]
For $u=1$, equation (2) would say that a positive power of $p\ge5$ equals $3$. For $u\ge2$,
\[
p^m u-4m
=
(p^m-4m)+p^m(u-1)
\ge
1+5(u-1),
\]
and therefore
\[
p^{p^m u-4m}
\ge
5^{\,1+5(u-1)}
>
5^u
>
2u^2+1.
\]
Thus $p\ge5$ is impossible.

It remains to take $p=3$. Equation (2) becomes
\[
2u^2+1=3^{3^m u-4m}.
\tag{3}
\]

If $m=0$, then
\[
2u^2+1=3^u.
\]
For $u\ge3$,
\[
3^u>2u^2+1,
\]
as follows by induction from $u=3$. Checking $u=1,2$ gives
\[
a=1,\qquad a=2.
\]

If $m=1$, then
\[
2u^2+1=3^{3u-4}.
\]
The exponent is positive only for $u\ge2$. For $u\ge3$, we have $3u-4>u$ and hence
\[
3^{3u-4}>3^u>2u^2+1,
\]
while $u=2$ works and gives
\[
a=6.
\]

Finally, let $m\ge2$. Since
\[
3^m-4m\ge1,
\]
for $u\ge2$ its exponent satisfies
\[
3^m u-4m\ge9u-8>u,
\]
so the right-hand side of (3) is larger than $2u^2+1$. Thus $u=1$, and
\[
3=3^{3^m-4m}.
\]
Hence
\[
3^m-4m=1,
\]
whose only solution with $m\ge2$ is $m=2$. This gives
\[
a=9.
\]

A direct verification completes the proof:
\[
3^1+1^4=4,
\]
\[
3^2+2^4=25,
\]
\[
3^6+6^4=2025=45^2,
\]
and
\[
3^9+9^4=26244=162^2.
\]
