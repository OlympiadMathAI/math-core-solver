ID: 0280

SOURCE: IMO Shortlist

YEAR: 2019

TOPIC: Combinatorics

SUBTOPICS: Combinatorial Games, Invariants, Extremal Configurations, Induction

STATEMENT:
There are $60$ empty boxes $B_1,B_2,\ldots,B_{60}$ in a row on a table and an unlimited supply of pebbles. Given a positive integer $n$, Alice and Bob play the following game.

In the first round, Alice takes $n$ pebbles and distributes them among the $60$ boxes as she wishes. Each subsequent round consists of two steps:

\begin{enumerate}
\item[(a)] Bob chooses an integer $k$ with $1\le k\le59$ and splits the boxes into the two groups
\[
B_1,\ldots,B_k
\qquad\text{and}\qquad
B_{k+1},\ldots,B_{60}.
\]
\item[(b)] Alice chooses one of the two groups, adds one pebble to every box in that group, and removes one pebble from every box in the other group.
\end{enumerate}

Bob wins if, at the end of any round, some box contains no pebbles. Find the smallest $n$ for which Alice can prevent Bob from winning.

SOLUTION 1:
The answer is
\[
\boxed{960}.
\]

We solve the more general game with $N>1$ boxes. Put
\[
M=\left(\left\lfloor\frac N2\right\rfloor+1\right)
  \left(\left\lceil\frac N2\right\rceil+1\right)-1.
\tag{1}
\]
For $1\le i\le N$, let $V_i$ be the configuration in which box $B_j$ contains
\[
1+|j-i|
\]
pebbles. Its total number of pebbles is
\[
\frac{i(i+1)}2+
\frac{(N+1-i)(N+2-i)}2-1.
\tag{2}
\]
For $i=\lceil N/2\rceil$, this equals $M$.

We say that a configuration dominates another one if every box contains at least as many pebbles as the corresponding box in the other configuration.

\textbf{Alice's strategy.}
Alice starts with $V_{\lceil N/2\rceil}$. Suppose that the current configuration dominates $V_i$.

If Bob cuts after a box $B_k$ with $k\ge i$, Alice chooses the left group. The resulting configuration dominates $V_{i+1}$. Indeed, to the left of $i$ the required values increase by $1$, to the right of $k$ they decrease by $1$, and in the intermediate boxes Alice has even more pebbles than required. Similarly, if $k<i$, Alice chooses the right group and the resulting configuration dominates $V_{i-1}$.

Every $V_i$ has at least one pebble in every box. Hence Alice can play forever with $M$ pebbles.

\textbf{Bob's strategy below $M$.}
We use the following observation. Suppose Bob first cuts after $B_k$ and Alice chooses the left group, and in the next round Bob cuts farther to the right, after $B_\ell$ with $\ell>k$. If Alice now chooses the right group, then the combined effect of the two rounds is that every box
\[
B_{k+1},\ldots,B_\ell
\]
loses two pebbles and all other boxes are unchanged. Thus the new configuration is strictly smaller than the configuration before those two rounds. Consequently, in a strategy in which Bob moves his cut monotonically to the right, he may restart whenever Alice changes sides; such restarts cannot continue indefinitely without a box becoming empty.

We need a claim.

\textbf{Claim.} If, for some positive integer $k$, at least $2k$ boxes contain at most $k$ pebbles each, then Bob can force a win.

\textit{Proof.}
List $2k$ such boxes from left to right as
\[
C_1,C_2,\ldots,C_{2k}.
\]
Bob first cuts between $C_k$ and $C_{k+1}$. By symmetry, suppose Alice chooses the left group. Bob then successively moves the cut to just after
\[
C_{k+1},C_{k+2},\ldots,C_{2k-1}.
\]
If Alice always chooses the left group, the box $C_{2k}$ loses one pebble in each of $k$ rounds and becomes empty. If she changes sides, the preceding observation allows Bob to restart from a strictly smaller configuration, in which the same $2k$ selected boxes still contain at most $k$ pebbles. Thus Bob eventually wins. $\square$

Now suppose that no box is empty and that the claim cannot be applied. For every
\[
1\le k\le\left\lfloor\frac N2\right\rfloor,
\]
fewer than $2k$ boxes contain at most $k$ pebbles. Hence at least
\[
N+1-2k
\]
boxes contain at least $k+1$ pebbles. Counting the pebbles by horizontal layers, the total is at least
\[
N+\sum_{k=1}^{\lfloor N/2\rfloor}(N+1-2k)=M.
\tag{3}
\]
Thus every configuration with fewer than $M$ pebbles either already contains an empty box or satisfies the claim, so Bob can force a win.

For $N=60$, formula (1) gives
\[
M=31^2-1=960.
\]

SOLUTION 2:
We again work with $N>1$ boxes and use the number $M$ and the configurations $V_i$ defined in Solution 1.

\textbf{Alice's strategy.}
Let
\[
K=\left\lfloor\frac N2\right\rfloor+1.
\]
Alice starts with $V_K$. For each of Bob's $N-1$ possible cut positions separately, she alternates between choosing the left and the right group whenever that cut is used. The first time a particular cut is used, she chooses the group containing $B_K$.

At any moment, only the parity of the number of uses of each cut matters. For the boxes from left to right, the greatest possible total decreases under this strategy are
\[
\left\lfloor\frac N2\right\rfloor,
\left\lfloor\frac N2\right\rfloor-1,
\ldots,1,0,1,\ldots,
\left\lceil\frac N2\right\rceil-1.
\]
Each of these is one less than the corresponding initial entry of $V_K$. Hence no box can become empty.

\textbf{Bob's strategy.}
We use strong induction on the total number of pebbles. If fewer than $N$ pebbles are present, some box is already empty, which gives the induction base. Consider a configuration $X$ with at most $M-1$ pebbles. The two central configurations
\[
V_K
\qquad\text{and}\qquad
V_{N+1-K}
\]
contain $M$ pebbles each. Therefore $X$ has fewer pebbles than each of them in at least one box.

Suppose first that, relative to one of these two configurations, say $V_K$, a deficiency occurs in the half where the entries of $V_K$ are
\[
1,2,\ldots,\left\lceil\frac N2\right\rceil.
\]
The deficient box is not the one containing $1$, since otherwise Bob has already won. Bob makes a $K$-move. If Alice chooses the group on the deficient side, the total number of pebbles decreases; Bob then applies the induction hypothesis. If she chooses the other group, Bob follows with cuts after
\[
B_{K+1},B_{K+2},\ldots.
\]
By the monotone-cut observation from Solution 1, Alice may be assumed to keep choosing the same side. The deficient box then reaches zero before the sequence of cuts passes it.

The only remaining possibility occurs when $N=2m$ is even and all deficiencies, relative to both central configurations, lie on their longer sides. In that case $X$ dominates the configuration
\[
m,m-1,\ldots,2,1,1,2,\ldots,m,
\tag{4}
\]
with equality somewhere in each half. Bob cuts between the two central boxes. Whichever group Alice chooses, the side from which pebbles are removed acquires a deficiency in the favorable half of one of the central $V$-configurations. We are then in the preceding case.

Thus Bob wins whenever fewer than $M$ pebbles are used, while Alice survives with $M$. For $N=60$, this gives $M=960$.

SOLUTION 3:
We give another lower-bound strategy for Bob; Alice may use the $V$-shaped strategy from Solution 1.

For a configuration $C$, define $L(C)$ to be the greatest integer $L$ such that
\[
B_{i+1}\text{ contains at least }L-i\text{ pebbles}
\qquad(0\le i\le N-1).
\]
Define $R(C)$ symmetrically from the right:
\[
B_{N-i}\text{ contains at least }R-i\text{ pebbles}
\qquad(0\le i\le N-1).
\]
The configuration $C$ dominates some $V_i$ if and only if
\[
L(C)+R(C)\ge N+1.
\tag{1}
\]
Indeed, one may choose $i=L(C)$, with the inequalities from the left and right meeting exactly when (1) holds. Consequently, any configuration satisfying (1) contains at least $M$ pebbles.

Suppose now that $C$ has fewer than $M$ pebbles. Then
\[
L(C)+R(C)\le N.
\tag{2}
\]
Bob chooses a cut with at least $L(C)$ boxes on its left and at least $R(C)$ boxes on its right; such a cut exists by (2). If no box becomes empty, then after Alice's move the pair $(L,R)$ changes as follows:
\[
(L,R)\longmapsto(L+1,R-1)
\]
if she chooses the left group, and
\[
(L,R)\longmapsto(L-1,R+1)
\]
if she chooses the right group. In either case the sum remains at most $N$.

Bob repeats this rule. Once Alice has chosen one side, the required cut positions move monotonically. If she later changes sides, the monotone-cut observation from Solution 1 produces a strictly smaller configuration, so Bob restarts; by strong induction on the total number of pebbles, this cannot prevent him from winning. We may therefore assume that Alice always chooses the same side.

Then one of $L$ and $R$ decreases by $1$ in each round. Suppose it is $L$. Eventually $L=1$. If $B_2$ is still nonempty, the maximality in the definition of $L$ forces $B_1$ to contain exactly one pebble. Bob makes a cut after $B_1$; Alice's same-side response removes that pebble and empties $B_1$. The case in which $R$ decreases is symmetric.

Thus no configuration with fewer than $M$ pebbles is safe. Together with Alice's strategy, this again gives $960$ for $60$ boxes.
