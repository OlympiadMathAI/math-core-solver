ID: 0374

SOURCE: IMO Shortlist

YEAR: 2022

TOPIC: Combinatorics

SUBTOPICS: Chip-Firing, Modular Invariants, Graph Laplacians, Constructive Algorithms

STATEMENT:
Let $n\ge4$. There are $n$ children arranged around a circle and a total of $n$ coins distributed among them. Some children may initially have no coins.

A legal move consists of choosing a child with at least two coins; that child gives one coin to each of the two neighbouring children.

Determine all initial distributions from which it is possible, after finitely many legal moves, to reach the distribution in which every child has exactly one coin.

SOLUTION 1:
Number the children cyclically by
\[
1,2,\ldots,n,
\]
and let $c_i$ be the number of coins held by child $i$.

The answer is precisely the set of distributions satisfying
\[
\boxed{
\sum_{i=1}^n i c_i
\equiv
\frac{n(n+1)}2
\pmod n
}.
\tag{1}
\]

A move at child $i$ changes the weighted sum in (1) by
\[
(i-1)-2i+(i+1),
\]
where the indices are interpreted cyclically. This change is a multiple of $n$, including at the two wrap-around positions. Hence the weighted sum modulo $n$ is invariant. The final all-one distribution has weighted sum
\[
1+2+\cdots+n=\frac{n(n+1)}2,
\]
so condition (1) is necessary.

We now prove sufficiency.

First, never allow child $n$ to make a move, and repeatedly fire any other child having at least two coins. This process terminates. Indeed, the quantity
\[
\Phi=\sum_{i=1}^n i^2c_i
\]
strictly increases at every such move: the increase is $2$ for $2\le i\le n-1$, and it is $n^2+2$ when $i=1$. Since the total number of coins is $n$, the quantity $\Phi$ is bounded above.

We therefore reach a state
\[
(z_1,z_2,\ldots,z_{n-1},M),
\tag{2}
\]
where every $z_i$ is $0$ or $1$. We call this a \emph{semi-uniform state} of irregularity $M$. Necessarily $M\ge1$.

We use the following elementary operation.

\textbf{Long-passing lemma.}
Suppose that consecutive children hold
\[
a,\underbrace{1,\ldots,1}_{r\text{ terms}},b,
\underbrace{1,\ldots,1}_{r\text{ terms}},c,
\qquad b\ge2.
\]
Then legal moves can transform these holdings into
\[
a+1,\underbrace{1,\ldots,1}_{r\text{ terms}},b-2,
\underbrace{1,\ldots,1}_{r\text{ terms}},c+1.
\tag{3}
\]

\textit{Proof.}
For $r=0$, this is one legal move. For $r\ge1$, first fire the middle pile $b$, and then successively fire the two piles of size $2$ that move outward through the two strings of ones. This gives
\[
a+1,0,\underbrace{1,\ldots,1}_{r-1\text{ terms}},b,
\underbrace{1,\ldots,1}_{r-1\text{ terms}},0,c+1.
\]
Apply the induction hypothesis to the central substring beginning and ending with the two zeros. This yields (3). $\square$

We show that every semi-uniform state with $M\ge3$ can be changed into another semi-uniform state with smaller irregularity.

There are exactly $M-1$ zeroes among the other $n-1$ positions, so at least two zeroes exist. Choose the nearest zero on each side of the pile $M$. The intervening arc has the form
\[
0,\underbrace{1,\ldots,1}_{a},M,
\underbrace{1,\ldots,1}_{b},0.
\tag{4}
\]

If $a=b$, the long-passing lemma changes the two endpoint zeroes into ones and replaces $M$ by $M-2$. Thus the irregularity decreases.

Assume, after reversing the circle if necessary, that $a>b$. Long-pass from $M$ to the right-hand zero and to the position at the same distance on the left. The relevant part of the state becomes
\[
0,\underbrace{1,\ldots,1}_{a-b-1},2,
\underbrace{1,\ldots,1}_{b},M-2,
\underbrace{1,\ldots,1}_{b+1}.
\tag{5}
\]
Write
\[
\alpha=a-b-1,\qquad \beta=b,\qquad p=M-2.
\]
Focus on the substring
\[
0,1^\alpha,2,1^\beta,p.
\tag{6}
\]

If $\alpha<\beta$, long-passing from the pile $2$ to the left zero and to an equally distant $1$ on the right replaces the pair $(\alpha,\beta)$ by
\[
(\alpha,\beta-\alpha-1).
\]
The sum of the two parameters strictly decreases. Repeating, we eventually reach $\alpha\ge\beta$.

If $\alpha=\beta$, long-passing from the pile $2$ to the left zero and to the pile $p$ produces a semi-uniform state whose irregularity is
\[
p+1=M-1.
\]

If $\alpha>\beta$, the same operation, now using the pile $p$ and an equally distant $1$ on the left, produces a pile $p+1=M-1$ and leaves a substring
\[
0,1^u,2,1^v,0
\tag{7}
\]
between two zeroes. Whenever $u\ne v$, long-pass from the central $2$ to the nearer zero and to an equally distant $1$ on the other side. This replaces the larger of $u,v$ by
\[
|u-v|-1
\]
and strictly shortens the substring. Eventually $u=v$, after which one final long-pass to the two zeroes leaves only zeroes and ones in (7). We again obtain a semi-uniform state of irregularity $M-1$.

Thus repeated legal moves reduce every semi-uniform state to one with
\[
M\le2.
\]

If $M=1$, the state is the all-one distribution. If $M=2$, there is exactly one zero, say at child $k$ with $1\le k<n$. Relative to the all-one state, the weighted sum has changed by
\[
n-k\equiv-k\not\equiv0\pmod n,
\]
contradicting the invariant (1). Hence $M=2$ cannot occur when (1) holds.

Therefore every distribution satisfying (1) can reach the all-one distribution.

SOLUTION 2:
We give an algebraic proof that the same congruence condition is sufficient.

Let
\[
d_i=c_i-1
\qquad(1\le i\le n),
\]
with all indices interpreted modulo $n$. Since both the initial and target distributions contain $n$ coins,
\[
\sum_{i=1}^n d_i=0.
\tag{2}
\]
Condition (1) is equivalent to
\[
\sum_{i=1}^n i d_i\equiv0\pmod n.
\tag{3}
\]

We claim that there are integers $t_1,\ldots,t_n$ such that
\[
d_i=2t_i-t_{i-1}-t_{i+1}
\qquad(1\le i\le n).
\tag{4}
\]
Here $t_0=t_n$ and $t_{n+1}=t_1$.

To construct them, first seek integers
\[
s_i=t_i-t_{i-1}.
\]
Equation (4) becomes
\[
d_i=s_i-s_{i+1}.
\]
Set
\[
s_i=s_1-\sum_{j=1}^{i-1}d_j.
\]
Because of (2), we have $s_{n+1}=s_1$. We also need
\[
s_1+\cdots+s_n=0
\]
in order to recover cyclic values $t_i$. This is achieved by taking
\[
s_1=
\frac1n\sum_{j=1}^{n-1}(n-j)d_j.
\]
The numerator is divisible by $n$ because, modulo $n$, it equals
\[
-\sum_{j=1}^{n-1}j d_j,
\]
which is $0$ by (3). We may now define the $t_i$ successively. Adding the same integer to all of them does not affect (4), so choose this constant so that
\[
t_i\ge0
\qquad\text{for all }i,
\qquad
\min_i t_i=0.
\tag{5}
\]

Interpret $t_i$ as the number of times child $i$ is intended to move. If all these moves can be legally performed, equation (4) says that the resulting distribution is the all-one distribution.

Suppose that some moves remain. Let $r_i$ be the number of remaining moves at child $i$. The current number of coins at child $i$ is then
\[
1+2r_i-r_{i-1}-r_{i+1}.
\tag{6}
\]
Let
\[
S=\{i:r_i>0\}.
\]
By (5), $S$ is a nonempty proper subset of the cycle. Summing the expression after the $1$ in (6) over $i\in S$ gives
\[
\sum_{\substack{i\in S,\ j\notin S\\ i\sim j}} r_i>0,
\]
where $i\sim j$ means that $i$ and $j$ are neighbouring children. Therefore some $i\in S$ has more than one coin and can legally move.

Perform that move and reduce $r_i$ by $1$. Repeating this argument eventually carries out all prescribed moves, and (4) then gives the all-one state.

Thus the congruence in (1) is both necessary and sufficient.
