ID: 0142

SOURCE: IMO Shortlist

YEAR: 2014

TOPIC: Number Theory

SUBTOPICS: Exponential Diophantine Equations, LTE, p-adic Valuations, Congruences

STATEMENT:
Find all triples $(p,x,y)$ consisting of a prime number $p$ and positive integers $x,y$ such that
\[
x^{p-1}+y
\qquad\text{and}\qquad
x+y^{p-1}
\]
are both powers of $p$.

SOLUTION 1:
The complete answer is
\[
\boxed{(p,x,y)=(3,2,5),(3,5,2)}
\]
and
\[
\boxed{(p,x,y)=(2,r,2^k-r)}
\]
for arbitrary integers $k\ge1$ and $0<r<2^k$.

For $p=2$, both expressions equal $x+y$, so the condition is exactly that $x+y$ be a power of $2$. This gives the second family.

Assume henceforth that $p$ is odd. Write
\[
x^{p-1}+y=p^a,
\qquad
x+y^{p-1}=p^b.
\tag{1}
\]
The case $x=y$ is impossible. Indeed, then $x(x^{p-2}+1)$ is a power of $p$. If $p\mid x$, the second factor is congruent to $1$ modulo $p$. Hence the product has the same $p$-adic valuation as $x$ but is strictly larger than $x$, so it cannot be a pure power of $p$. If $p\nmid x$, the fact that $x$ divides a power of $p$ forces $x=1$, and the expression becomes $2$, again impossible for odd $p$.

By symmetry, assume $x<y$. Then $a\le b$. Substituting
\[
y=p^a-x^{p-1}
\]
into the second equation of (1) and reducing modulo $p^a$, we obtain
\[
0\equiv x^{(p-1)^2}+x
=x\left(x^{p(p-2)}+1\right)
\pmod{p^a}.
\tag{2}
\]
If $p\mid x$, then the second factor in (2) is not divisible by $p$, so $p^a\mid x$. This is impossible because
\[
0<x<x^{p-1}+y=p^a.
\]
Thus $p\nmid x$, and (2) gives
\[
p^a\mid x^{p(p-2)}+1.
\tag{3}
\]
Modulo $p$, Fermat's little theorem turns (3) into $x\equiv-1\pmod p$.

Let
\[
r=v_p(x+1).
\]
The lifting-the-exponent formula for the odd exponent $p(p-2)$ gives
\[
v_p\left(x^{p(p-2)}+1\right)
=v_p(x+1)+v_p(p(p-2))
=r+1.
\]
Hence
\[
a\le r+1.
\tag{4}
\]
On the other hand,
\[
p^r\le x+1\le x^{p-1}+y=p^a,
\]
so $r\le a$. Therefore $a=r$ or $a=r+1$.

If $a=r$, then
\[
p^a=x^{p-1}+y\ge x+1\ge p^r=p^a.
\]
Equality throughout would force $x=y=1$, which is impossible for odd $p$. Thus
\[
a=r+1.
\tag{5}
\]
Using $x\le y$ and $p\ge3$, we have $x^2+x\le x^{p-1}+y$. Consequently
\[
x=\frac{x^2+x}{x+1}
\le\frac{x^{p-1}+y}{x+1}
=\frac{p^a}{x+1}
\le\frac{p^a}{p^r}=p.
\]
Since $p\mid x+1$, it follows that
\[
x=p-1.
\]
Then $r=1$ and $a=2$. Equation (1) becomes
\[
p^2=(p-1)^{p-1}+y.
\]
For $p\ge5$, the first term on the right already exceeds $p^2$, a contradiction. Hence $p=3$, and then
\[
x=2,
\qquad
y=3^2-2^2=5.
\]
Indeed,
\[
2^2+5=9,
\qquad
2+5^2=27.
\]
Interchanging $x$ and $y$ gives the second exceptional triple.

SOLUTION 2:
We again separate the case $p=2$, which gives precisely all positive pairs with $x+y$ a power of $2$. Suppose $p$ is odd and write
\[
x^{p-1}+y=p^a,
\qquad
x+y^{p-1}=p^b.
\tag{1}
\]
As in Solution 1, $x=y$ is impossible. By symmetry assume $x<y$. Since the integer-valued function $t\mapsto t^{p-1}-t$ is strictly increasing for $t\ge1$, we have
\[
p^b-p^a=(y^{p-1}-y)-(x^{p-1}-x)>0,
\]
and therefore $a<b$.

First, neither $x$ nor $y$ is divisible by $p$. For if $p\mid x$, then the first equation forces $p\mid y$. Let $p^u\Vert x$ and $p^v\Vert y$, with $u\le v$. Since $(p-1)v>u$, the exact $p$-adic valuation of $x+y^{p-1}$ is $u$. But this number is a power of $p$ and is strictly larger than $x\ge p^u$, a contradiction.

Reducing (1) modulo $p$ gives
\[
x\equiv y\equiv-1\pmod p.
\tag{2}
\]
From (2), $x,y\ge p-1$, and therefore
\[
x^{p-1}+y\ge (p-1)^{p-1}+p-1>p.
\]
The same holds for the other expression. Since both are powers of $p$, they are divisible by $p^2$. Hence
\[
x^{p-1}\equiv-y,
\qquad
y^{p-1}\equiv-x
\pmod{p^2}.
\tag{3}
\]
Euler's theorem modulo $p^2$, together with (3), gives
\[
1\equiv x^{p(p-1)}
\equiv(-y)^p
\equiv xy
\pmod{p^2}.
\tag{4}
\]
Since $p\mid x-y$, equations (2) and (4) imply
\[
(x+y)^2=(x-y)^2+4xy\equiv4\pmod{p^2}.
\]
Now $x+y\equiv-2\pmod p$, so $p\nmid x+y-2$. Therefore
\[
p^2\mid x+y+2.
\tag{5}
\]

Subtracting the two equations in (1), we obtain
\[
p^b-p^a=(y-x)S,
\tag{6}
\]
where
\[
S=y^{p-2}+y^{p-3}x+\cdots+x^{p-2}-1.
\]
We claim that
\[
S\equiv-p\pmod{p^2}.
\tag{7}
\]
Pair the terms before the final $-1$ symmetrically. For every odd $r$, relation (2) makes $(x^r+1)^2$ divisible by $p^2$, and (4) then gives
\[
0\equiv y^r(x^r+1)^2
\equiv x^r+2+y^r
\pmod{p^2}.
\]
Thus every symmetric pair is congruent to $-2$ modulo $p^2$. There are $(p-1)/2$ such pairs, so their sum is $-(p-1)$; after subtracting $1$, (7) follows.

Thus $v_p(S)=1$. Since $a<b$, the left-hand side of (6) has valuation $a$, and therefore
\[
v_p(y-x)=a-1.
\tag{8}
\]
Reducing the first equation of (1) modulo $p^{a-1}$ and using $y\equiv x$ from (8), we get
\[
0\equiv x^{p-1}+x
=x(x+1)T
\pmod{p^{a-1}},
\]
where
\[
T=\frac{x^{p-2}+1}{x+1}
=x^{p-3}-x^{p-4}+\cdots-x+1.
\]
Here $T=1$ when $p=3$. In every case, $x\equiv-1\pmod p$ gives $T\equiv p-2\not\equiv0\pmod p$. Since $p\nmid x$, we conclude that
\[
p^{a-1}\mid x+1.
\tag{9}
\]

On the other hand, the same substitution that led to (2) in Solution 1 gives
\[
p^a\mid x^{p(p-2)}+1.
\]
If $r=v_p(x+1)$, LTE yields $a\le r+1$, while (9) gives $r\ge a-1$, and $x+1\le p^a$ gives $r\le a$. The possibility $r=a$ leads, as in Solution 1, to the impossible equality $x=y=1$. Hence $a=r+1$.

The final argument of Solution 1 now applies: it gives $x=p-1$, then $a=2$, and finally $p=3$, $x=2$, $y=5$. Interchanging $x$ and $y$ gives $(3,5,2)$. These, together with the $p=2$ family, are all solutions.
