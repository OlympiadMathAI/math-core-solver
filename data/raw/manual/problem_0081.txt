ID: 0081

SOURCE: IMO Shortlist

YEAR: 2012

TOPIC: Number Theory

SUBTOPICS: Greatest Common Divisors, Bezout's Identity, Closure Properties, Divisibility

STATEMENT:
Call a set $A$ of integers admissible if it has the following property: whenever $x,y\in A$ (possibly $x=y$), then
\[
x^2+kxy+y^2\in A
\]
for every integer $k$.

Determine all pairs $(m,n)$ of nonzero integers such that the only admissible set containing both $m$ and $n$ is the set of all integers.

SOLUTION 1:
The required pairs are exactly those satisfying
\[
\gcd(m,n)=1.
\]

First suppose that $d=\gcd(m,n)>1$. The set
\[
d\mathbb Z=\{dt:t\in\mathbb Z\}
\]
is admissible: if $d\mid x$ and $d\mid y$, then
\[
d\mid x^2+kxy+y^2
\]
for every integer $k$. This proper subset of $\mathbb Z$ contains both $m$ and $n$, so the required property fails.

Conversely, suppose that $\gcd(m,n)=1$, and let $A$ be an admissible set containing $m$ and $n$. We record two consequences of admissibility.

First, for every $x\in A$ and every integer $r$,
\[
rx^2\in A.
\tag{1}
\]
Indeed, put $y=x$ and choose $k=r-2$; then
\[
x^2+kx^2+x^2=rx^2.
\]

Second, for all $x,y\in A$,
\[
(x+y)^2\in A.
\tag{2}
\]
This follows by taking $k=2$.

Since $\gcd(m^2,n^2)=1$, Bezout's identity gives integers $a,b$ such that
\[
am^2+bn^2=1.
\]
By (1), both $am^2$ and $bn^2$ belong to $A$. Applying (2) to these two elements gives
\[
1=(am^2+bn^2)^2\in A.
\]
Finally, applying (1) with $x=1$ shows that every integer $r$ belongs to $A$. Hence $A=\mathbb Z$.

Therefore the answer is precisely
\[
\boxed{\gcd(m,n)=1}.
\]
