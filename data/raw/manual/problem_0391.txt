ID: 0391

SOURCE: IMO Shortlist

YEAR: 2022

TOPIC: Number Theory

SUBTOPICS: Factorials, Prime Valuations, LTE, Zsigmondy Theorem, Congruences

STATEMENT:
Find all triples of positive integers
\[
(a,b,p),
\]
where $p$ is prime, such that
\[
a^p=b!+p.
\]

SOLUTION 1:
The solutions are
\[
\boxed{(a,b,p)=(2,2,2)\quad\text{and}\quad(3,4,3)}.
\]
Both triples are checked directly.

Clearly
\[
a>1.
\]
We consider three cases.

\textbf{Case 1: $a<p$.}

If $a\le b$, then $a$ divides both $a^p$ and $b!$, and hence
\[
a\mid a^p-b!=p.
\]
This is impossible because
\[
1<a<p.
\]

If $a>b$, then
\[
b!\le(a-1)!<a^{a-1}<a^p-p,
\]
contrary to
\[
b!=a^p-p.
\]
Thus this case is impossible.

\textbf{Case 2: $a>p$.}

We have
\[
b!=a^p-p>p^p-p\ge p!,
\]
so
\[
b>p.
\]
Since
\[
p\mid a^p=b!+p,
\]
we have $p\mid a$. Moreover,
\[
b!=p\left(p^{p-1}\left(\frac ap\right)^p-1\right),
\]
so
\[
v_p(b!)=1.
\]
Therefore
\[
p<b<2p.
\tag{1}
\]

If $a<p^2$, then
\[
1<\frac ap<p<b.
\]
The integer $a/p$ divides both $a^p$ and $b!$, and hence divides their difference $p$. This is impossible.

If $a\ge p^2$, then
\[
a^p\ge p^{2p}.
\]
On the other hand, by (1),
\[
b!+p\le(2p-1)!+p.
\]
Pairing the factors of $(2p-1)!$ as
\[
(2p-1)!
=
p\prod_{i=1}^{p-1}i(2p-i)
<
p\left(p^2\right)^{p-1}
=
p^{2p-1},
\]
we obtain
\[
(2p-1)!+p<p^{2p},
\]
a contradiction. Hence this case is also impossible.

\textbf{Case 3: $a=p$.}

Now
\[
b!=p^p-p.
\tag{2}
\]
For $p=2$ and $p=3$, equation (2) gives respectively
\[
b!=2
\qquad\text{and}\qquad
b!=24,
\]
so
\[
b=2\qquad\text{and}\qquad b=4.
\]
The prime $p=5$ gives
\[
b!=3120,
\]
which is impossible because
\[
6!<3120<7!.
\]

Assume now that
\[
p\ge7.
\]
Equation (2) gives $b!\!>p!$, so
\[
b\ge p+1.
\tag{3}
\]
By the lifting-the-exponent lemma,
\[
\begin{aligned}
v_2(b!)
&=
v_2(p^p-p)\\
&=
v_2\left(p^{p-1}-1\right)\\
&=
2v_2(p-1)+v_2(p+1)-1\\
&=
v_2\left(\frac{p-1}{2}(p-1)(p+1)\right).
\end{aligned}
\tag{4}
\]
However, the three factors on the right of (4) all occur among the factors of $(p+1)!$. Since
\[
p+1\ge8,
\]
there is at least one further even factor in $(p+1)!$, and therefore
\[
v_2((p+1)!)
>
v_2\left(\frac{p-1}{2}(p-1)(p+1)\right).
\]
Together with (3), this contradicts (4).

Thus no further solutions exist, and the answer is
\[
\boxed{(2,2,2),(3,4,3)}.
\]

SOLUTION 2:
We first eliminate the cases $a\ne p$.

If $a<p$ and $a\le b$, then
\[
a\mid a^p-b!=p,
\]
which is impossible. If $a<p$ and $a>b$, then
\[
b!\le(a-1)!<a^p-p,
\]
also impossible.

If $a>p$, then $b!>p!$, so $b>p$. The equation implies $p\mid a$ and
\[
v_p(b!)=1,
\]
hence
\[
p<b<2p.
\]
If $a<p^2$, the integer $a/p$ lies strictly between $1$ and $p$ and divides both $a^p$ and $b!$, so it divides $p$, a contradiction. If $a\ge p^2$, then
\[
a^p\ge p^{2p}>(2p-1)!+p\ge b!+p,
\]
again a contradiction.

Therefore
\[
a=p
\]
and
\[
b!=p^p-p=p\left(p^{p-1}-1\right).
\tag{1}
\]
The cases $p=2,3$ give the two stated solutions.

Assume
\[
p\ge5.
\]
Then $b>p$. By Zsigmondy's theorem, the number
\[
p^{p-1}-1
\]
has a prime divisor $q$ which divides no number
\[
p^j-1
\qquad(1\le j<p-1).
\]
Thus
\[
\operatorname{ord}_q(p)=p-1,
\]
and consequently
\[
q\equiv1\pmod{p-1}.
\]
Also $q\ne p$, so
\[
q\ge2p-1.
\]
Since $q\mid b!$, we have
\[
b\ge q\ge2p-1.
\]
Therefore
\[
b!\ge(2p-1)!>p^p>p^p-p,
\]
contradicting (1).

Hence only
\[
\boxed{(2,2,2)\quad\text{and}\quad(3,4,3)}
\]
are possible.

SOLUTION 3:
We first eliminate the cases $a<p$ and $a>p$.

Indeed, if $a<p$ and $a\le b$, then
\[
a\mid a^p-b!=p,
\]
whereas if $a>b$, then
\[
b!<a^p-p.
\]
If $a>p$, the equation gives $p\mid a$ and
\[
p<b<2p.
\]
For $a<p^2$, the nontrivial divisor $a/p$ of both $a^p$ and $b!$ would divide $p$; for $a\ge p^2$, one has
\[
a^p\ge p^{2p}>(2p-1)!+p\ge b!+p.
\]
Thus
\[
a=p
\qquad\text{and}\qquad
b!=p^p-p.
\tag{1}
\]

The primes $p=2,3$ give the two stated solutions. For
\[
p=5,7,11,
\]
direct comparison with consecutive factorials gives
\[
6!<5^5-5<7!,
\]
\[
9!<7^7-7<10!,
\]
and
\[
14!<11^{11}-11<15!,
\]
so none of these primes gives another solution.

Assume now that
\[
p\ge13.
\]
Then $b>p$.

Let $q$ be an odd prime divisor of $p+1$. By LTE,
\[
\begin{aligned}
v_q(p^p-p)
&=
v_q\left(p^{p-1}-1\right)\\
&=
v_q(p^2-1)
+
v_q\left(\frac{p-1}{2}\right)\\
&=
v_q(p+1).
\end{aligned}
\]
But $b\ge p+1$, and the factorial $b!$ contains both the factor $p+1$ and the additional factor $q$. Hence
\[
v_q(b!)>v_q(p+1),
\]
contradicting (1). Therefore
\[
p+1=2^t
\tag{2}
\]
for some positive integer $t$.

Since $p+1=2^t$ and $p\ge13$, the number
\[
p-1=2\left(2^{t-1}-1\right)
\]
has an odd prime divisor. Let $q$ be any such divisor, and put
\[
d=v_q(p-1).
\]
Again by LTE,
\[
v_q(b!)
=
v_q(p^p-p)
=
2d.
\tag{3}
\]

We claim that $d=1$. If $d\ge2$, then
\[
p\ge q^d+1.
\]
Consequently,
\[
v_q(b!)\ge v_q(p!)\ge v_q((q^d)!)
=
1+q+\cdots+q^{d-1}.
\]
This is greater than $2d$ except possibly when $(q,d)=(3,2)$. In that remaining case,
\[
p\ge13
\]
and
\[
v_3(b!)\ge v_3(13!)=5>4=2d.
\]
Both alternatives contradict (3). Hence $d=1$.

If
\[
p>2q+1,
\]
then, since $(p-1)/q$ is an even integer, we have
\[
p>3q.
\]
Thus
\[
v_q(b!)\ge v_q(p!)\ge3,
\]
contradicting (3), which now equals $2$. Therefore
\[
p-1=2q.
\tag{4}
\]

Combining (2) and (4),
\[
p=2^t-1
\qquad\text{and}\qquad
q=2^{t-1}-1
\]
are both prime. Hence both $t$ and $t-1$ are prime. The only two consecutive prime integers are $2$ and $3$, which would give $p=7$, contrary to $p\ge13$.

No further solutions exist.

SOLUTION 4:
We first show that $a=p$.

If $a<p$, then either $a\le b$, in which case
\[
a\mid a^p-b!=p,
\]
or $a>b$, in which case
\[
b!<a^p-p.
\]
Both are impossible.

If $a>p$, then $p\mid a$ and
\[
p<b<2p.
\]
If $a<p^2$, the integer $a/p$ lies strictly between $1$ and $p$ and divides both $a^p$ and $b!$, so it would divide $p$, which is impossible. If $a\ge p^2$, then
\[
a^p\ge p^{2p}>(2p-1)!+p\ge b!+p,
\]
again impossible.

Thus
\[
a=p
\qquad\text{and}\qquad
b!=p^p-p.
\tag{1}
\]
The cases $p=2,3$ give
\[
(a,b,p)=(2,2,2),(3,4,3).
\]

Assume
\[
p\ge5.
\]
Then $b>p$, so
\[
b\ge p+1.
\tag{2}
\]
Put
\[
t=p+1.
\]
Modulo $t^2$, the binomial theorem gives
\[
\begin{aligned}
p^p-p
&=(t-1)^p-(t-1)\\
&\equiv -1+pt-t+1\\
&=(p-1)t\\
&=p^2-1
\not\equiv0\pmod{t^2}.
\end{aligned}
\tag{3}
\]

On the other hand, the three distinct integers
\[
2,\qquad \frac{p+1}{2},\qquad p+1
\]
all occur among the factors of $(p+1)!$, and their product is divisible by
\[
(p+1)^2=t^2.
\]
Therefore, by (2),
\[
t^2\mid(p+1)!\mid b!,
\]
contradicting (1) and (3).

Thus the only solutions are
\[
\boxed{(2,2,2)\quad\text{and}\quad(3,4,3)}.
\]
