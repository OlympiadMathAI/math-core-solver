ID: 0066

SOURCE: IMO Shortlist

YEAR: 2012

TOPIC: Combinatorics

SUBTOPICS: Monovariants, Termination, Lexicographic Order, Extremal Processes

STATEMENT:
Several positive integers are written in a row. Iteratively, Alice chooses two adjacent numbers $x$ and $y$ such that $x>y$ and $x$ is to the left of $y$, and replaces the pair $(x,y)$ by either $(y+1,x)$ or $(x-1,x)$. Prove that she can perform only finitely many such iterations.

SOLUTION 1:
Let the current row be
\[
a_1,a_2,\ldots,a_n,
\]
and let $M$ be the maximum of the initial row. Every operation preserves the maximum: the old entry $x$ remains as the second entry of the new pair, while the other new entry is at most $x$. Hence every entry remains a positive integer not exceeding $M$.

Consider the weighted sum
\[
S=a_1+2a_2+\cdots+na_n.
\]
Suppose the pair in positions $i,i+1$ is $(x,y)$, where $x>y$, and it is replaced by $(c,x)$, where
\[
c=y+1\quad\text{or}\quad c=x-1.
\]
The change in $S$ is
\[
\begin{aligned}
\Delta S
&=ic+(i+1)x-\bigl(ix+(i+1)y\bigr)\\
&=x-y+i(c-y).
\end{aligned}
\]
Because $x$ and $y$ are integers with $x>y$, we have $x-y\ge 1$. Also $c\ge y$: this is clear when $c=y+1$, and when $c=x-1$ it follows from $x\ge y+1$. Therefore
\[
\Delta S\ge 1.
\]
Thus $S$ increases by at least $1$ after every operation. On the other hand,
\[
S\le (1+2+\cdots+n)M=\frac{n(n+1)}2M.
\]
So $S$ cannot increase indefinitely, and only finitely many operations are possible.

SOLUTION 2:
Again let $M$ be the maximum of the initial row. Every row that occurs consists of positive integers not exceeding $M$, so only finitely many rows are possible.

Order integer $n$-tuples reverse lexicographically: we say
\[
(x_1,\ldots,x_n)<(y_1,\ldots,y_n)
\]
if, at the largest index $j$ for which $x_j\ne y_j$, one has $x_j<y_j$.

Suppose an operation is performed in positions $i,i+1$. All entries to the right of position $i+1$ remain unchanged, while the entry in position $i+1$ changes from $y$ to $x>y$. Therefore the new row is strictly larger than the old row in reverse lexicographic order.

Consequently no row can occur twice. Since only finitely many rows are possible, the process must terminate.

SOLUTION 3:
For a row
\[
a_1,a_2,\ldots,a_n,
\]
define the score $s_i$ of $a_i$ to be the number of entries $a_j$ that are strictly smaller than $a_i$. The score sequence is
\[
(s_1,s_2,\ldots,s_n).
\]
Each score belongs to $\{0,1,\ldots,n-1\}$, so there are at most $n^n$ possible score sequences.

Compare score sequences lexicographically, but regard a decrease at the first differing position as an improvement: a sequence $(u_1,\ldots,u_n)$ dominates $(v_1,\ldots,v_n)$ if, at the first index $j$ for which $u_j\ne v_j$, one has $u_j<v_j$.

Suppose an operation replaces $(x,y)$ in positions $i,i+1$ by $(a,x)$, where
\[
a=y+1\quad\text{or}\quad a=x-1.
\]
In either case
\[
y\le a\le x.
\]
For every $j<i$, the value in position $j$ is unchanged, while one of the other entries changes from $y$ to $a\ge y$. Hence its score cannot increase. If one of these earlier scores decreases, the new score sequence dominates the old one.

Assume therefore that all scores before position $i$ remain unchanged. At position $i$, the old value is $x$ and the new value is $a\le x$. Every entry counted in the new score of $a$ was counted in the old score of $x$, while the old entry $y<x$ was counted before but is not counted after the operation: it is either removed or becomes the entry $a$ itself. Thus the score in position $i$ decreases by at least $1$.

So after every operation the new score sequence dominates the old one. Score sequences never repeat, and there are only finitely many of them. Therefore the process terminates.
