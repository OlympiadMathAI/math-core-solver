ID: 0328

SOURCE: IMO Shortlist

YEAR: 2020

TOPIC: Number Theory

SUBTOPICS: Completely Additive Functions, Prime Valuations, Lucas Theorem, Extremal Prime Arguments

STATEMENT:
Determine all functions
\[
f:\mathbb Z_{>0}\to\mathbb Z_{\ge0}
\]
satisfying the following three conditions:

\begin{enumerate}
\item[(i)] $f(n)\ne0$ for at least one positive integer $n$;
\item[(ii)]
\[
f(xy)=f(x)+f(y)
\]
for all positive integers $x,y$;
\item[(iii)] there are infinitely many positive integers $n$ such that
\[
f(k)=f(n-k)
\]
for every integer $k$ with
\[
1\le k<n.
\]
\end{enumerate}

SOLUTION 1:
The solutions are
\[
\boxed{f(n)=c\,v_p(n)},
\]
where $p$ is a prime, $c$ is a positive integer, and $v_p(n)$ denotes the exponent of $p$ in the prime factorization of $n$.

Call a positive integer $n$ \emph{good} if
\[
f(k)=f(n-k)
\qquad(1\le k<n).
\]

Condition (ii) gives
\[
f(1)=0
\]
and, if
\[
n=p_1p_2\cdots p_t
\]
is written as a product of primes with multiplicity, then
\[
f(n)=f(p_1)+f(p_2)+\cdots+f(p_t).
\tag{1}
\]
In particular, if $f(n)=0$, then $f(q)=0$ for every prime divisor $q$ of $n$.

We first observe that every divisor of a good number is good. Suppose
\[
n=dm
\]
is good. For $1\le k<d$,
\[
\begin{aligned}
f(k)
&=f(mk)-f(m)\\
&=f(n-mk)-f(m)\\
&=f(m(d-k))-f(m)\\
&=f(d-k).
\end{aligned}
\]
Thus $d$ is good.

By condition (i), some prime has positive $f$-value. Let $p$ be the smallest such prime. Then
\[
f(r)=0
\qquad(1\le r<p),
\tag{2}
\]
because every prime divisor of $r$ is smaller than $p$.

Every good number $n>p$ is divisible by $p$. Indeed, if
\[
n=pk+r,
\qquad 0<r<p,
\]
then goodness gives
\[
f(pk)=f(n-pk)=f(r)=0,
\]
while
\[
f(pk)=f(p)+f(k)\ge f(p)>0,
\]
a contradiction.

Now let $n$ be good, and let $r$ be its largest divisor not divisible by $p$. Since every divisor of $n$ is good, either $r=1$ or, by the preceding paragraph,
\[
r<p.
\]
Thus every good number has the form
\[
r p^m,
\qquad 1\le r<p.
\tag{3}
\]

There are infinitely many good numbers. Since only finitely many values of $r$ occur in (3), the exponent $m$ is unbounded. Every divisor of a good number is good, so
\[
p^m
\]
is good for every positive integer $m$.

Let $q\ne p$ be any prime. By Fermat's little theorem,
\[
q\mid p^{q-1}-1.
\]
The number $p^{q-1}$ is good, and therefore
\[
f(1)=f(p^{q-1}-1).
\]
The left side is $0$, while
\[
f(p^{q-1}-1)\ge f(q)
\]
by (1) and nonnegativity. Hence
\[
f(q)=0.
\]
Thus $p$ is the only prime at which $f$ is nonzero. Writing
\[
c=f(p)>0,
\]
we obtain
\[
f(n)=c\,v_p(n).
\]

Conversely, let
\[
f(n)=c\,v_p(n)
\]
with $c>0$. Conditions (i) and (ii) are immediate. For every $m\ge1$ and
\[
1\le k<p^m,
\]
we have
\[
v_p(p^m-k)=v_p(k).
\]
Hence every $p^m$ is good, so condition (iii) also holds.

SOLUTION 2:
Again call $n$ good when
\[
f(k)=f(n-k)
\qquad(1\le k<n).
\]

Call a prime $p$ \emph{big} if
\[
f(p)>0.
\]
For a positive integer $m$, define its \emph{essence} by
\[
e(m)=\prod_{\substack{p\text{ big}}}p^{v_p(m)}.
\]
The quotient $m/e(m)$ has no big prime divisor, so
\[
f(m)=f(e(m)).
\tag{1}
\]

We use the following lemma.

\textbf{Lemma.}
If $n$ is good, then
\[
e(k)=e(n-k)
\qquad(1\le k<n).
\tag{2}
\]

\textit{Proof.}
Suppose otherwise, and choose the smallest $k$ for which (2) fails.

The number $k$ must be big. Otherwise
\[
f(k)=0,
\]
and goodness gives
\[
f(n-k)=0;
\]
then both $k$ and $n-k$ have essence $1$, contrary to the choice of $k$.

Put
\[
E=e(k),
\qquad
t=\frac{k}{E}.
\]
There are exactly $t-1$ multiples of $E$ in
\[
1,2,\ldots,k-1.
\]
For every $j<k$, minimality gives
\[
e(j)=e(n-j).
\]
Moreover,
\[
E\mid j
\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad
E\mid e(j)
\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad
E\mid e(n-j)
\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad
E\mid n-j.
\]
Thus the bijection
\[
j\longmapsto n-j
\]
shows that there are exactly $t-1$ multiples of $E$ in
\[
n-k+1,\ldots,n-1.
\]

The interval
\[
n-k,n-k+1,\ldots,n-1
\]
contains exactly $k=tE$ consecutive integers and hence exactly $t$ multiples of $E$. The missing multiple must therefore be $n-k$, so
\[
E\mid n-k.
\]

Using goodness and (1),
\[
\begin{aligned}
f(E)
&=f(k)\\
&=f(n-k)\\
&=f(E)+f\left(\frac{n-k}{E}\right).
\end{aligned}
\]
Hence
\[
f\left(\frac{n-k}{E}\right)=0.
\]
The quotient contains no big prime, so
\[
e(n-k)=E=e(k),
\]
contradicting the choice of $k$. $\square$

Suppose that at least two big primes exist, and let
\[
p_1<p_2
\]
be the two smallest. Choose a good number
\[
n>p_1p_2.
\]
Let $p_1^\alpha$ be the largest power of $p_1$ smaller than $n$. Then
\[
n\le p_1^{\alpha+1}<p_1^\alpha p_2.
\tag{3}
\]
By the lemma,
\[
e(n-p_1^\alpha)=e(p_1^\alpha)=p_1^\alpha,
\]
so
\[
p_1^\alpha\mid n.
\tag{4}
\]
Applying the lemma with $k=p_2$ gives
\[
p_2\mid n.
\tag{5}
\]
Equations (4)--(5) imply
\[
n\ge p_1^\alpha p_2,
\]
contradicting (3).

Thus there is at most one big prime. Condition (i) shows that there is exactly one, say $p$. Formula (1) then yields
\[
f(n)=f(p)\,v_p(n),
\]
which is the required form.

SOLUTION 3:
By complete additivity,
\[
f\left(\prod_p p^{\alpha_p}\right)
=
\sum_p\alpha_p f(p).
\tag{1}
\]

Let $n$ be good. For every
\[
1\le k\le n-1,
\]
we have
\[
\begin{aligned}
&\sum_{j=1}^{k}f(n-j)-\sum_{j=1}^{k}f(j)\\
&\qquad
=
f\left(\frac{(n-1)(n-2)\cdots(n-k)}{k!}\right)\\
&\qquad
=
f\binom{n-1}{k}.
\end{aligned}
\tag{2}
\]
For a good number, the left side of (2) is zero. Hence
\[
f\binom{n-1}{k}=0
\qquad(1\le k\le n-1).
\tag{3}
\]

Suppose that
\[
f(p)>0
\]
for a prime $p$. By (1), equation (3) implies
\[
p\nmid\binom{n-1}{k}
\qquad(1\le k\le n-2).
\tag{4}
\]

We use the following standard consequence of Lucas' theorem.

\textbf{Lemma.}
For a prime $p$, condition (4) holds if and only if
\[
n=a p^s
\]
for some integers
\[
s\ge0,
\qquad
1\le a<p.
\]

\textit{Proof.}
Write $n-1$ in base $p$. Lucas' theorem says that every binomial coefficient in the row of $n-1$ is nonzero modulo $p$ precisely when every non-leading base-$p$ digit of $n-1$ is $p-1$. Thus
\[
n-1=(a-1)p^s+(p-1)(1+p+\cdots+p^{s-1})
=ap^s-1
\]
for some $1\le a\le p$. If $a=p$, absorb one further factor of $p$, obtaining the stated form with $a=1$. The converse follows directly from Lucas' theorem. $\square$

If two distinct primes $p$ and $q$ satisfied
\[
f(p)>0,
\qquad
f(q)>0,
\]
then every good number would have both forms
\[
n=a p^s=b q^t,
\qquad
1\le a<p,\quad1\le b<q.
\tag{5}
\]
There are only finitely many integers satisfying (5). Indeed,
\[
p^s\mid b
\]
and
\[
q^t\mid a,
\]
so both exponents are bounded.

This contradicts the existence of infinitely many good numbers. Therefore only one prime $p$ has positive $f$-value, and (1) gives
\[
f(n)=c\,v_p(n)
\]
with
\[
c=f(p)>0.
\]

SOLUTION 4:
For an additive function $f$ and a prime $p$, define
\[
g(f,p)=\frac{f(p)}{\log p}.
\]

Suppose that $n$ is good and that at least two primes smaller than $n$ have positive $f$-value. Choose a prime $p_0<n$ for which
\[
g(f,p_0)
\]
is maximal among all primes smaller than $n$, and let $a_0$ be the largest integer such that
\[
p_0^{a_0}<n.
\]

We claim that
\[
f(k)<f(p_0^{a_0})
\qquad(1\le k<p_0^{a_0}).
\tag{1}
\]
Indeed, write
\[
k=\prod_i p_i^{\alpha_i}.
\]
Every $p_i<n$, and therefore
\[
\begin{aligned}
f(k)
&=\sum_i\alpha_i f(p_i)\\
&=\sum_i g(f,p_i)\alpha_i\log p_i\\
&\le
g(f,p_0)\log k\\
&<
g(f,p_0)a_0\log p_0\\
&=
f(p_0^{a_0}).
\end{aligned}
\]

Write
\[
n=b p_0^{a_0}+r,
\qquad
0\le r<p_0^{a_0}.
\]
If $r>0$, goodness gives
\[
f(r)=f(n-r)=f(bp_0^{a_0})\ge f(p_0^{a_0}),
\]
contradicting (1). Hence
\[
p_0^{a_0}\mid n.
\tag{2}
\]

It follows that
\[
n=p_0^{v_{p_0}(n)}n_1
\qquad\text{with}\qquad
1\le n_1\le p_0.
\tag{3}
\]
Indeed, $p_0^{a_0}$ is the largest power strictly below $n$, while the next power is at least $n$.

Define
\[
f_1(m)=f(p_0)v_{p_0}(m),
\qquad
f_2=f-f_1.
\]
Both functions are completely additive. They are also nonnegative, because
\[
f(m)
=
f_1(m)+f\left(\frac{m}{p_0^{v_{p_0}(m)}}\right)
\ge f_1(m).
\]
We claim that $(f_2,n)$ is also good.

For every $1\le k<n$,
\[
v_{p_0}(k)=v_{p_0}(n-k).
\tag{4}
\]
If $v_{p_0}(k)<v_{p_0}(n)$, this is standard. Otherwise, by (3),
\[
k=p_0^{v_{p_0}(n)}u
\]
with
\[
1\le u<n_1\le p_0,
\]
so both $u$ and $n_1-u$ are nonzero modulo $p_0$, and (4) again follows.

Thus
\[
f_1(k)=f_1(n-k).
\]
Since $f(k)=f(n-k)$, we also have
\[
f_2(k)=f_2(n-k),
\]
proving that $(f_2,n)$ is good.

The function $f_2$ is positive at some prime, because there were at least two positive primes for $f$ and
\[
f_2(p_0)=0.
\]
Repeat the preceding argument with $f_2$. We obtain a prime
\[
q_0\ne p_0
\]
such that
\[
n=q_0^{v_{q_0}(n)}n_2
\qquad\text{with}\qquad
1\le n_2\le q_0.
\tag{5}
\]

Since $q_0\mid n$ and $q_0\ne p_0$, equation (3) gives
\[
q_0\mid n_1,
\qquad
q_0\le n_1\le p_0.
\]
Similarly, (5) gives
\[
p_0\le n_2\le q_0.
\]
Therefore
\[
p_0=q_0,
\]
a contradiction.

Hence a good number larger than two positive primes cannot exist. Since there are infinitely many good numbers, the function can be positive at only one prime. Complete additivity then gives
\[
f(n)=c\,v_p(n)
\]
for some prime $p$ and positive integer $c$.
