ID: 0114

SOURCE: IMO Shortlist

YEAR: 2013

TOPIC: Number Theory

SUBTOPICS: Functional Equations, Rational Numbers, Floor and Ceiling Functions, Minimal Denominator

STATEMENT:
Determine all functions
\[
f:\mathbb{Q}\to\mathbb{Z}
\]
satisfying
\[
f\left(\frac{f(x)+a}{b}\right)
=
f\left(\frac{x+a}{b}\right)
\tag{1}
\]
for all $x\in\mathbb{Q}$, $a\in\mathbb{Z}$, and $b\in\mathbb{Z}_{>0}$.

SOLUTION 1:
The solutions are all integer-valued constant functions, the floor function, and the ceiling function.

Constant functions clearly satisfy (1). For the floor function, let
\[
q=\left\lfloor\frac{x+a}{b}\right\rfloor.
\]
Then
\[
bq\le x+a<b(q+1).
\]
Since the endpoints are integers and $a$ is an integer, the same inequalities hold after replacing $x$ by $\lfloor x\rfloor$. Thus
\[
\left\lfloor\frac{\lfloor x\rfloor+a}{b}\right\rfloor
=
\left\lfloor\frac{x+a}{b}\right\rfloor,
\]
so the floor function works. The ceiling function is verified analogously.

Now suppose that $f$ satisfies (1). We distinguish two cases.

\textbf{Case 1: $f(m)\ne m$ for some integer $m$.}

Write
\[
f(m)=C,
\qquad
\eta=\operatorname{sgn}(C-m)\in\{-1,1\},
\qquad
b_0=|C-m|.
\]
For any integer $r$, apply (1) with
\[
x=m,
\qquad a=rb_0-C,
\qquad b=b_0.
\]
Since $C=m+\eta b_0$, we obtain
\[
f(r)=f(r-\eta).
\]
It follows in both directions that
\[
f(r)=C
\qquad(r\in\mathbb{Z}).
\tag{2}
\]

Let $y=p/q\in\mathbb{Q}$, where $p\in\mathbb{Z}$ and $q>0$. In (1), take
\[
x=C-p,
\qquad a=p-C,
\qquad b=q.
\]
By (2), $f(C-p)=C$, and hence
\[
f\left(\frac pq\right)=f(0)=C.
\]
Thus $f$ is constant.

\textbf{Case 2: $f(m)=m$ for every integer $m$.}

Taking $b=1$ in (1), the left-hand argument $f(x)+a$ is an integer, so
\[
f(x+a)=f(x)+a
\qquad(x\in\mathbb{Q},\ a\in\mathbb{Z}).
\tag{3}
\]
Let
\[
\omega=f\left(\frac12\right)\in\mathbb{Z}.
\]
We first prove
\[
\omega\in\{0,1\}.
\tag{4}
\]
If $\omega\le0$, substitute
\[
x=\frac12,
\qquad a=-\omega,
\qquad b=1-2\omega
\]
in (1). This gives $f(0)=f(1/2)$, so $\omega=0$. If $\omega\ge1$, substitute
\[
x=\frac12,
\qquad a=\omega-1,
\qquad b=2\omega-1.
\]
Then $f(1)=f(1/2)$, so $\omega=1$. This proves (4).

We claim that
\[
f(x)=\omega
\qquad\text{for every }x\in\mathbb{Q}\text{ with }0<x<1.
\tag{5}
\]
Suppose not, and choose a reduced fraction $a/b\in(0,1)$ with $b$ minimal such that
\[
f\left(\frac ab\right)\ne\omega.
\tag{6}
\]

If $b$ is even, then $a$ is odd. Apply (1) with
\[
x=\frac12,
\qquad a_0=\frac{a-1}{2},
\qquad b_0=\frac b2.
\]
We get
\[
f\left(\frac{\omega+(a-1)/2}{b/2}\right)
=
f\left(\frac ab\right)
e\omega.
\tag{7}
\]
The argument on the left lies in $[0,1)$ when $\omega=0$ and in $(0,1]$ when $\omega=1$. If it is an endpoint, its value is $\omega$ because $f(0)=0$ and $f(1)=1$; otherwise its reduced denominator is smaller than $b$, so its value is $\omega$ by minimality. This contradicts (7). Therefore $b$ is odd.

Write $b=2k+1$. Applying (1) with
\[
x=\frac12,
\qquad a=k,
\qquad b=2k+1
\]
gives
\[
f\left(\frac{\omega+k}{b}\right)=\omega.
\tag{8}
\]
Since $\gcd(a,b)=1$, choose integers $1\le r<b$ and $m$ such that
\[
ra-mb=k+\omega.
\tag{9}
\]
The inequalities $0<a<b$ and $0<k+\omega<b$ imply
\[
0\le m\le r-1.
\tag{10}
\]
Now apply (1) with
\[
x=\frac{k+\omega}{b},
\qquad a=m,
\qquad b=r.
\]
Using (8) and (9), we obtain
\[
f\left(\frac{\omega+m}{r}\right)
=
f\left(\frac ab\right)
e\omega.
\tag{11}
\]
By (10), the argument on the left belongs to $[0,1)$ if $\omega=0$ and to $(0,1]$ if $\omega=1$. As before, its value must be $\omega$, either because it is an endpoint or because its denominator is smaller than $b$. This contradicts (11), proving (5).

If $\omega=0$, equations (3) and (5), together with $f(0)=0$, show that $f(x)=\lfloor x\rfloor$ for every rational $x$. If $\omega=1$, the same argument gives $f(x)=\lceil x\rceil$. Thus the listed functions are exactly all solutions.

SOLUTION 2:
The candidate functions are all integer-valued constant functions, the floor function, and the ceiling function. Constants plainly work. For the floor function,
\[
\left\lfloor\frac{\lfloor x\rfloor+a}{b}\right\rfloor
=
\left\lfloor\frac{x+a}{b}\right\rfloor,
\]
because the inequalities defining the right-hand side have integer endpoints; the ceiling function is checked similarly.

Now let $f$ be an arbitrary solution. Suppose first that $f(m)\ne m$ for some integer $m$. Write
\[
f(m)=C,
\qquad
\eta=\operatorname{sgn}(C-m),
\qquad
b_0=|C-m|.
\]
For every integer $r$, substitution of
\[
x=m,
\qquad a=rb_0-C,
\qquad b=b_0
\]
into (1) gives $f(r)=f(r-\eta)$. Thus $f$ is equal to $C$ on all integers. If $y=p/q\in\mathbb{Q}$ with $q>0$, substituting
\[
x=C-p,
\qquad a=p-C,
\qquad b=q
\]
then gives $f(y)=f(0)=C$. Therefore $f$ is constant.

It remains to treat the case
\[
f(m)=m
\qquad(m\in\mathbb{Z}).
\tag{2}
\]
Taking $b=1$ in (1) gives
\[
f(x+a)=f(x)+a
\qquad(x\in\mathbb{Q},\ a\in\mathbb{Z}).
\tag{3}
\]

Let
\[
S=\{x\in\mathbb{Q}:f(x)=0\}.
\]
For every $x\in\mathbb{Q}$, equation (3) shows that exactly one number in
\[
\ldots,x-2,x-1,x,x+1,x+2,\ldots
\]
belongs to $S$.

We first prove
\[
\frac pq\in S
\quad\Longrightarrow\quad
\frac p{q+1}\in S
\qquad(p\in\mathbb{Z},\ q>0).
\tag{4}
\]
Indeed, put $x=p/q$, $a=p$, and $b=q+1$ in (1). Since $f(p/q)=0$,
\[
f\left(\frac p{q+1}\right)
=
f\left(\frac pq\right)=0.
\]

It follows that if $x>y>0$ and $x\in S$, then $y\in S$. Write $x=p/q$ and $y=r/s$ with $p,q,r,s>0$. Since $ps>qr$, repeated use of (4) gives
\[
0=f\left(\frac pq\right)
=f\left(\frac{pr}{qr}\right)
=f\left(\frac{pr}{qr+1}\right)
=\cdots
=f\left(\frac{pr}{ps}\right)
=f\left(\frac rs\right).
\tag{5}
\]
Similarly,
\[
x<y<0,
\qquad x\in S
\quad\Longrightarrow\quad y\in S.
\tag{6}
\]

We have $0\in S$, while $1,-1\notin S$ by (2). Relations (5) and (6) therefore imply $S\subset(-1,1)$. Let
\[
\alpha=\sup S.
\]
Then $0\le\alpha\le1$.

Assume first that $0<\alpha<1$. By (5), every rational number in $(0,\alpha)$ belongs to $S$. If $x\in(\alpha,1)\cap\mathbb{Q}$, then $x\notin S$; since exactly one integer translate of $x$ lies in $S$ and $S\subset(-1,1)$, that translate must be $x-1$. Hence, by (3),
\[
f(x)=1
\qquad(\alpha<x<1).
\tag{7}
\]

Let $K$ be the unique positive integer satisfying
\[
K\alpha<1\le(K+1)\alpha.
\]
Since $K\alpha<1$,
\[
\alpha<\frac{1+\alpha}{K+1}.
\]
Choose a rational number
\[
\alpha<x<\frac{1+\alpha}{K+1}
\]
and put $y=(K+1)x-1$. Then $0<y<\alpha$, so $f(y)=0$, while (7) gives $f(x)=1$. Substituting $x=y$, $a=1$, and $b=K+1$ in (1), we get
\[
f\left(\frac1{K+1}\right)=1.
\tag{8}
\]
But $1/(K+1)\le\alpha$. By (5), (8) is possible only if
\[
\alpha=\frac1{K+1}
\qquad\text{and}\qquad
f(\alpha)=1.
\tag{9}
\]
Now put $x=\alpha$, $a=0$, and $b=K+1$ in (1). Using (9),
\[
f\left(\frac1{K+1}\right)
=
f\left(\frac1{(K+1)^2}\right).
\]
The left-hand side equals $1$, while the argument on the right lies in $(0,\alpha)$ and hence its value is $0$. This contradiction rules out $0<\alpha<1$.

Therefore $\alpha\in\{0,1\}$. If $\alpha=0$, the unique-zero property and (5)-(6) give
\[
S=(-1,0]\cap\mathbb{Q},
\]
and (3) yields $f(x)=\lceil x\rceil$. If $\alpha=1$, downward closure and the definition of the supremum give
\[
S=[0,1)\cap\mathbb{Q},
\]
and (3) yields $f(x)=\lfloor x\rfloor$. Together with the constant solutions, this completes the classification.
