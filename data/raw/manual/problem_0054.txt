ID: 0054

SOURCE: IMO Shortlist

YEAR: 2011

TOPIC: Number Theory

SUBTOPICS: Odd Parts, Modular Arithmetic, Two-Adic Valuation

STATEMENT:
For every positive integer $k$, let $t(k)$ be the largest odd divisor of $k$. Determine all positive integers $a$ for which there exists a positive integer $n$ such that all the numbers
\[
t(n+a+i)-t(n+i),
\qquad
i=0,1,\ldots,a-1,
\]
are divisible by $4$.

SOLUTION 1:
The answer is
\[
\boxed{a\in\{1,3,5\}}.
\]

Call a pair $(a,n)$ \emph{winning} if
\[
4\mid t(n+a+i)-t(n+i)
\qquad(i=0,\ldots,a-1).
\]

The pairs
\[
(1,1),\qquad(3,1),\qquad(5,4)
\]
are winning. Indeed:

- for $(1,1)$, the only difference is
\[
t(2)-t(1)=1-1=0;
\]

- for $(3,1)$, the three differences are
\[
t(4)-t(1)=0,\qquad
t(5)-t(2)=4,\qquad
t(6)-t(3)=0;
\]

- for $(5,4)$, the five differences are
\[
8,\ 0,\ 8,\ -4,\ 12.
\]

It remains to exclude every other positive integer $a$.

\textbf{Case 1: $a$ is even.}

Write
\[
a=2^\alpha d,
\]
where $\alpha\ge1$ and $d$ is odd. Since $a\ge2^\alpha$, among the $a$ consecutive integers
\[
n,n+1,\ldots,n+a-1
\]
there is one of the form
\[
n+i=2^{\alpha-1}e
\]
with $e$ odd. Then
\[
t(n+i)=e,
\]
whereas
\[
n+a+i
=2^{\alpha-1}(e+2d).
\]
The number $e+2d$ is odd, so
\[
t(n+a+i)=e+2d\equiv e+2\pmod4.
\]
Thus
\[
t(n+a+i)-t(n+i)\equiv2\pmod4,
\]
and no pair $(a,n)$ is winning.

\textbf{Case 2: $a$ is odd and $a>8$.}

Among the consecutive integers
\[
n,n+1,\ldots,n+a-5
\]
there is an integer congruent to $2$ modulo $4$. Hence, for some
\[
0\le i\le a-5,
\]
we may write
\[
n+i=2d
\]
with $d$ odd. Then
\[
t(n+i)=d,
\qquad
t(n+i+4)=d+2,
\]
so
\[
t(n+i+4)-t(n+i)\equiv2\pmod4.
\tag{1}
\]

Because $a$ is odd, both $n+a+i$ and $n+a+i+4$ are odd. Therefore
\[
t(n+a+i+4)-t(n+a+i)=4\equiv0\pmod4.
\tag{2}
\]
If both differences
\[
t(n+a+i)-t(n+i)
\]
and
\[
t(n+a+i+4)-t(n+i+4)
\]
were divisible by $4$, then their difference would be divisible by $4$. But by (1) and (2), that difference is congruent to $2$ modulo $4$, a contradiction. Thus $(a,n)$ cannot be winning.

\textbf{Case 3: $a=7$.}

Among any seven consecutive integers, at least one is congruent to $3$ or $6$ modulo $8$. Thus, for some
\[
0\le i\le6,
\]
the number $n+i$ has one of the forms
\[
8q+3
\quad\text{or}\quad
8q+6.
\]

If $n+i=8q+3$, then
\[
t(n+i)=8q+3\equiv3\pmod4,
\]
while
\[
t(n+i+7)=t(8q+10)=4q+5\equiv1\pmod4.
\]

If $n+i=8q+6$, then
\[
t(n+i)=4q+3\equiv3\pmod4,
\]
while
\[
t(n+i+7)=8q+13\equiv1\pmod4.
\]

In either case,
\[
t(n+i+7)-t(n+i)\not\equiv0\pmod4.
\]
Hence $a=7$ is impossible.

All positive integers other than $1,3,5$ fall into one of these three cases. Therefore the required values are exactly
\[
\boxed{1,3,5}.
\]
