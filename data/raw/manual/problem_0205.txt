ID: 0205

SOURCE: IMO Shortlist

YEAR: 2016

TOPIC: Number Theory

SUBTOPICS: Lattice Polygons, P-adic Valuations, Ptolemy's Theorem, Heron's Formula

STATEMENT:
Let $n$ be an odd positive integer. A cyclic polygon $\mathcal P$ of area $S$ is chosen in the Cartesian plane. All its vertices have integer coordinates, and the squares of all its side lengths are divisible by $n$. Prove that $2S$ is an integer divisible by $n$.

SOLUTION 1:
Let
\[
\mathcal P=A_1A_2\cdots A_k,
\qquad
A_{k+i}=A_i.
\]
By the shoelace formula,
\[
2S\in\mathbb Z.
\]

It is enough to prove divisibility by every prime power dividing $n$. Thus fix an odd prime $p$ and a positive integer $t$ such that
\[
p^t\mid n,
\]
and prove
\[
p^t\mid2S.
\]
All squared side lengths are divisible by $p^t$.

We use induction on the number $k\ge3$ of vertices.

\textbf{Base case: $k=3$.}

Write the three squared side lengths as
\[
p^t a,\qquad p^t b,\qquad p^t c
\]
for positive integers $a,b,c$. Heron's identity gives
\[
16S^2
=
p^{2t}
\left(
2ab+2bc+2ca-a^2-b^2-c^2
\right).
\]
Thus
\[
p^{2t}\mid16S^2.
\]
Since $p$ is odd and $2S$ is an integer,
\[
p^t\mid2S.
\]

\textbf{Induction step.}

Suppose $k\ge4$. If some diagonal has squared length divisible by $p^t$, that diagonal divides $\mathcal P$ into two smaller cyclic polygons satisfying the same condition. By the induction hypothesis, the doubled area of each is divisible by $p^t$, and hence so is $2S$.

We may therefore assume that
\[
p^t\nmid A_iA_j^2
\]
for every diagonal $A_iA_j$.

For any segment $UV$ joining two lattice points, define
\[
w(UV)=v_p(UV^2),
\]
where $UV^2$ denotes the squared length and $v_p$ is the usual $p$-adic valuation.

We claim that
\[
w(A_1A_m)>w(A_1A_{m+1})
\qquad(2\le m\le k-1).
\tag{1}
\]

For $m=2$, the segment $A_1A_2$ is a side while $A_1A_3$ is a diagonal, so
\[
w(A_1A_2)\ge t>w(A_1A_3).
\]

Assume now that
\[
w(A_1A_{m-1})>w(A_1A_m)
\]
for some $3\le m\le k-1$. Apply Ptolemy's theorem to the cyclic quadrilateral
\[
A_1A_{m-1}A_mA_{m+1}:
\]
\[
A_1A_{m+1}\cdot A_{m-1}A_m
+
A_1A_{m-1}\cdot A_mA_{m+1}
=
A_1A_m\cdot A_{m-1}A_{m+1}.
\]
After squaring and rearranging,
\[
\begin{aligned}
&A_1A_{m+1}^2\,A_{m-1}A_m^2\\
&\quad=
A_1A_{m-1}^2\,A_mA_{m+1}^2
+
A_1A_m^2\,A_{m-1}A_{m+1}^2\\
&\qquad
-
2A_1A_{m-1}\,A_mA_{m+1}\,A_1A_m\,A_{m-1}A_{m+1}.
\end{aligned}
\tag{2}
\]
The first three terms in (2) are integers, so the final cross term is an integer as well.

Put
\[
U=A_1A_{m-1}^2\,A_mA_{m+1}^2,
\qquad
V=A_1A_m^2\,A_{m-1}A_{m+1}^2.
\]
The inductive hypothesis and the facts that $A_mA_{m+1}$ is a side while $A_{m-1}A_{m+1}$ is a diagonal give
\[
v_p(U)>v_p(V).
\tag{3}
\]
If $C$ denotes the cross term in (2), then
\[
C^2=4UV.
\]
Since $p$ is odd, (3) yields
\[
v_p(C)
=
\frac{v_p(U)+v_p(V)}2
>
v_p(V).
\tag{4}
\]
Therefore $V$ is the unique term of least $p$-adic valuation on the right side of (2). Hence
\[
\begin{aligned}
&w(A_1A_{m+1})+w(A_{m-1}A_m)\\
&\qquad=
w(A_1A_m)+w(A_{m-1}A_{m+1}).
\end{aligned}
\]
Now
\[
w(A_{m-1}A_m)\ge t
>
w(A_{m-1}A_{m+1}),
\]
so
\[
w(A_1A_{m+1})<w(A_1A_m).
\]
This proves the claim.

Applying (1) successively gives
\[
t
\le
w(A_1A_2)
>
w(A_1A_3)
>
\cdots
>
w(A_1A_k)
\ge
t,
\]
because both $A_1A_2$ and $A_1A_k$ are sides. This is impossible.

Thus our assumption that no diagonal square is divisible by $p^t$ was false. The induction step follows, and therefore
\[
p^t\mid2S
\]
for every prime power $p^t$ dividing $n$. Hence
\[
n\mid2S.
\]
