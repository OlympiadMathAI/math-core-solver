ID: 0334

SOURCE: IMO Shortlist

YEAR: 2021

TOPIC: Algebra

SUBTOPICS: Inequalities, Concavity, Induction, Fourier Integrals

STATEMENT:
Prove that, for all real numbers
\[
x_1,x_2,\ldots,x_n,
\]
we have
\[
\sum_{i=1}^{n}\sum_{j=1}^{n}\sqrt{|x_i-x_j|}
\le
\sum_{i=1}^{n}\sum_{j=1}^{n}\sqrt{|x_i+x_j|}.
\]

SOLUTION 1:
We use induction on $n$. The cases $n=0$ and $n=1$ are immediate.

Let
\[
L=
\sum_{i=1}^{n}\sum_{j=1}^{n}\sqrt{|x_i-x_j|}.
\]
If the same real number $t$ is added to every $x_i$, then $L$ does not change, while the right-hand side becomes
\[
H(t)=
\sum_{i=1}^{n}\sum_{j=1}^{n}
\sqrt{|x_i+x_j+2t|}.
\tag{1}
\]

Choose $T>0$ so large that
\[
H(-T)>L
\qquad\text{and}\qquad
H(T)>L.
\]
The numbers
\[
-\frac{x_i+x_j}{2}
\]
together with $-T$ and $T$ partition the interval $[-T,T]$ into subintervals. On each such subinterval, every summand in (1) has the form
\[
\sqrt{\pm(\lambda+2t)}
\]
with fixed sign, and is concave. Hence $H$ is concave there. Consequently, at any point inside a subinterval, $H$ is at least the smaller of its values at the two endpoints.

It therefore suffices to prove
\[
H\left(-\frac{x_i+x_j}{2}\right)\ge L
\tag{2}
\]
for every pair $i,j$. After shifting the variables by the corresponding value of $t$, condition (2) is exactly the original inequality in a situation where two of the shifted variables have sum zero.

Suppose first that one shifted variable is $0$. Removing it decreases each side of the desired inequality by
\[
2\sum_k\sqrt{|x_k|}.
\]
The result follows from the induction hypothesis for the remaining $n-1$ variables.

Suppose instead that two distinct shifted variables are opposites, say
\[
x_r=u,
\qquad
x_s=-u.
\]
Remove both. The contribution to either double sum from all ordered pairs involving $r$ or $s$ is
\[
2\sqrt{2|u|}
+
2\sum_{k\ne r,s}
\left(
\sqrt{|x_k-u|}+\sqrt{|x_k+u|}
\right).
\]
Thus both sides decrease by the same amount, and the induction hypothesis applies to the remaining $n-2$ variables.

Hence every breakpoint satisfies (2). Concavity on the interval containing $0$ now gives
\[
H(0)\ge L,
\]
which is the required inequality.

SOLUTION 2:
For real $p$, define
\[
I(p)=
\int_0^\infty
\frac{1-\cos(px)}{x^{3/2}}\,dx.
\]
The integral converges, and
\[
I(p)>0
\qquad(p\ne0).
\]
The substitution $y=|p|x$ gives
\[
I(p)=\sqrt{|p|}\,I(1).
\tag{1}
\]

For arbitrary real $a,b$, equation (1) and
\[
\cos(\alpha-\beta)-\cos(\alpha+\beta)
=
2\sin\alpha\sin\beta
\]
yield
\[
\begin{aligned}
\sqrt{|a+b|}-\sqrt{|a-b|}
&=
\frac{I(a+b)-I(a-b)}{I(1)}\\
&=
\frac{2}{I(1)}
\int_0^\infty
\frac{\sin(ax)\sin(bx)}{x^{3/2}}\,dx.
\end{aligned}
\tag{2}
\]

Sum (2) over all ordered pairs
\[
(a,b)=(x_i,x_j).
\]
We obtain
\[
\begin{aligned}
&\sum_{i,j}\sqrt{|x_i+x_j|}
-
\sum_{i,j}\sqrt{|x_i-x_j|}\\
&\qquad=
\frac{2}{I(1)}
\int_0^\infty
\frac{\left(\sum_{i=1}^{n}\sin(x_ix)\right)^2}{x^{3/2}}\,dx
\ge0.
\end{aligned}
\]
This proves the inequality.
