ID: 0174

SOURCE: IMO Shortlist

YEAR: 2015

TOPIC: Number Theory

SUBTOPICS: Prime Factorization, Divisibility Preservation, Increasing Functions, Modular Arithmetic

STATEMENT:
For every positive integer
\[
n=\prod_{i=1}^{r}p_i^{\alpha_i},
\]
define
\[
\Omega(n)=\sum_{i:\,p_i>10^{100}}\alpha_i.
\]
Thus $\Omega(n)$ is the number of prime factors of $n$ greater than $10^{100}$, counted with multiplicity.

Find all strictly increasing functions
\[
f:\mathbb Z\to\mathbb Z
\]
such that
\[
\Omega\bigl(f(a)-f(b)\bigr)\le\Omega(a-b)
\]
for all integers $a>b$.

SOLUTION 1:
The answer is
\[
\boxed{f(x)=ux+v},
\]
where $v$ is any integer and $u$ is any positive integer satisfying
\[
\Omega(u)=0.
\]
Equivalently, every prime factor of $u$ is at most $10^{100}$.

Every such function works, because
\[
f(a)-f(b)=u(a-b)
\]
and $\Omega(u)=0$.

We prove that there are no other functions. Subtracting the constant $f(0)$ does not change any difference, so we may assume
\[
f(0)=0.
\tag{1}
\]
Let
\[
N=10^{100}.
\]
Call a prime \emph{large} if it is greater than $N$, and \emph{small} otherwise. Call a positive integer large if all its prime factors are large, and small if all its prime factors are small. The number $1$ is both.

For a positive integer $q$, let $L(q)$ be its greatest large divisor and $S(q)$ its greatest small divisor. Then
\[
q=L(q)S(q).
\]

\textbf{Step 1: preservation of divisibility by large integers.}

We prove that for every large positive integer $k$ and all distinct integers $a,b$,
\[
k\mid f(a)-f(b)
\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad
k\mid a-b.
\tag{2}
\]
We use induction on $k$. The case $k=1$ is trivial.

Assume the statement is known for all large integers smaller than a fixed large integer $k_0$.

First, if
\[
0<|x-y|<k_0,
\]
then
\[
k_0\nmid f(x)-f(y).
\tag{3}
\]
Indeed, suppose the contrary and put
\[
\ell=L(|x-y|).
\]
Then $\ell<k_0$, so the induction hypothesis gives
\[
\ell\mid f(x)-f(y).
\]
Consequently
\[
\operatorname{lcm}(k_0,\ell)\mid f(x)-f(y).
\]
This least common multiple is a large proper multiple of $\ell$, so
\[
\Omega\bigl(f(x)-f(y)\bigr)
\ge
\Omega\bigl(\operatorname{lcm}(k_0,\ell)\bigr)
>
\Omega(\ell)
=
\Omega(|x-y|),
\]
contrary to the hypothesis.

For any integer $a$, the $k_0$ numbers
\[
f(a),f(a+1),\ldots,f(a+k_0-1)
\]
are pairwise distinct modulo $k_0$ by (3), so they form a complete residue system modulo $k_0$. The same is true of
\[
f(a+1),f(a+2),\ldots,f(a+k_0).
\]
These two complete residue systems have $k_0-1$ common terms, hence
\[
f(a+k_0)\equiv f(a)\pmod{k_0}.
\tag{4}
\]
Thus
\[
a\equiv b\pmod{k_0}
\quad\Longrightarrow\quad
f(a)\equiv f(b)\pmod{k_0}.
\]

Conversely, suppose $a\not\equiv b\pmod{k_0}$. Choose $b'\equiv b\pmod{k_0}$ with
\[
0<|a-b'|<k_0.
\]
By (4),
\[
f(b')\equiv f(b)\pmod{k_0},
\]
while (3) gives
\[
f(a)\not\equiv f(b')\pmod{k_0}.
\]
Hence $f(a)\not\equiv f(b)\pmod{k_0}$. This proves (2).

In particular,
\[
L\bigl(|f(a)-f(b)|\bigr)=L(|a-b|)
\tag{5}
\]
for all distinct integers $a,b$.

\textbf{Step 2: linearity on an infinite set.}

Let $\mathcal S$ be the finite set of small primes, and let
\[
d=\prod_{p\in\mathcal S}p.
\]
Choose $\alpha$ so large that
\[
2^\alpha>f(N).
\]
Because $f$ is strictly increasing and $f(0)=0$, for each small prime $p$ the numbers
\[
f(0),f(1),\ldots,f(N)
\]
are distinct modulo $p^\alpha$.

Set
\[
P=d^\alpha,
\qquad
C=P+f(N).
\]
We claim that
\[
f(t)<Ct
\qquad(t>N).
\tag{6}
\]

Fix $t>N$. For each small prime $p$, at most one index $i\in\{0,1,\ldots,N\}$ satisfies
\[
p^\alpha\mid f(t)-f(i).
\]
There are fewer than $N$ small primes, so some
\[
j\in\{0,1,\ldots,N\}
\]
works for none of them. Hence
\[
S\bigl(f(t)-f(j)\bigr)<P.
\]
By (5),
\[
L\bigl(f(t)-f(j)\bigr)=L(t-j)\le t-j.
\]
Therefore
\[
\begin{aligned}
f(t)
&=f(j)+L\bigl(f(t)-f(j)\bigr)S\bigl(f(t)-f(j)\bigr)\\
&<f(N)+(t-j)P\\
&\le Ct,
\end{aligned}
\]
proving (6).

Now let $t$ range over the large primes. From (5) with $b=0$,
\[
L(f(t))=L(t)=t,
\]
so $f(t)/t$ is a positive integer. By (6), this ratio has only finitely many possible values. Hence there exist a positive integer $u$ and infinitely many large primes $t$ such that
\[
f(t)=ut.
\tag{7}
\]
For these primes,
\[
t=L(f(t))=L(ut)=L(u)t,
\]
so
\[
L(u)=1.
\]
Thus $u$ is small, or equivalently
\[
\Omega(u)=0.
\tag{8}
\]

\textbf{Step 3: propagation to every integer.}

For an integer $r$, let
\[
R_r=\{x\in\mathbb Z:x\equiv r\pmod{N!}\}.
\]

We use the following claim.

\textbf{Claim.}
Suppose that for some $r$ there are infinitely many $n\in R_r$ satisfying
\[
f(n)=un.
\]
Then
\[
f(x)=ux
\]
for every $x\in R_{r+1}$.

\textit{Proof.}
Fix $x\in R_{r+1}$. Choose one of the infinitely many suitable $n\in R_r$ so that
\[
|n-x|>|f(x)-ux|.
\tag{9}
\]
Since
\[
n-x\equiv-1\pmod{N!},
\]
no small prime divides $|n-x|$. Thus $|n-x|$ is a large integer. By (2),
\[
|n-x|\mid f(n)-f(x).
\]
Using $f(n)=un$ and $n\equiv x\pmod{|n-x|}$, we obtain
\[
f(x)\equiv un\equiv ux\pmod{|n-x|}.
\]
Hence
\[
|n-x|\mid f(x)-ux.
\]
Together with (9), this gives $f(x)=ux$. $\square$

The infinite set in (7) contains infinitely many elements of some residue class $R_r$. Applying the claim successively gives
\[
f(x)=ux
\]
on
\[
R_{r+1},R_{r+2},\ldots,R_{r+N!}=R_r.
\]
These are all residue classes modulo $N!$, so
\[
f(x)=ux
\qquad\text{for every }x\in\mathbb Z.
\]

Restoring the subtracted constant $f(0)$ gives
\[
f(x)=ux+v,
\]
where $u>0$, $v\in\mathbb Z$, and $\Omega(u)=0$. This completes the proof.
