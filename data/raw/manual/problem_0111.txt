ID: 0111

SOURCE: IMO Shortlist

YEAR: 2013

TOPIC: Number Theory

SUBTOPICS: Largest Prime Divisors, Polynomial Factorization, Coprimality, Infinite Sequences

STATEMENT:
Prove that there exist infinitely many positive integers $n$ such that the largest prime divisor of
\[
n^4+n^2+1
\]
is equal to the largest prime divisor of
\[
(n+1)^4+(n+1)^2+1.
\]

SOLUTION 1:
For $n\ge1$, let $p_n$ be the largest prime divisor of $n^4+n^2+1$, and let $q_n$ be the largest prime divisor of $n^2+n+1$. Since
\[
n^4+n^2+1=(n^2)^2+n^2+1,
\]
we have
\[
p_n=q_{n^2}.
\tag{1}
\]
Also,
\[
\begin{aligned}
n^4+n^2+1
&=(n^2-n+1)(n^2+n+1)\\
&=\bigl((n-1)^2+(n-1)+1\bigr)(n^2+n+1).
\end{aligned}
\]
For $n\ge2$, the two factors are coprime. Indeed,
\[
\begin{aligned}
\gcd(n^2+n+1,n^2-n+1)
&=\gcd(2n,n^2-n+1)\\
&=\gcd(n,n^2-n+1)\\
&=1,
\end{aligned}
\]
where we used that $n^2-n+1$ is odd. Therefore
\[
p_n=\max\{q_{n-1},q_n\}
\qquad(n\ge2),
\tag{2}
\]
and in particular
\[
q_n\ne q_{n-1}
\qquad(n\ge2).
\tag{3}
\]

Consider the set
\[
S=\{n\ge2:q_n>q_{n-1}\text{ and }q_n>q_{n+1}\}.
\]
For every $n\in S$, equation (2) gives
\[
p_n=q_n=p_{n+1}.
\]
Thus every $n\in S$ has the required property. It remains to prove that $S$ is infinite.

We have
\[
q_2=7,
\qquad q_3=13,
\qquad q_4=7,
\]
so $3\in S$. Suppose, for contradiction, that $S$ is finite, and let $m$ be its largest element.

The sequence $q_m,q_{m+1},q_{m+2},\ldots$ cannot be strictly decreasing forever, since it consists of positive integers and adjacent terms are unequal by (3). Hence there exists $k\ge m$ such that
\[
q_k<q_{k+1}.
\]
Nor can the sequence be strictly increasing from that point onward. Indeed, if
\[
q_k<q_{k+1}<q_{k+2}<\cdots,
\]
then, because $(k+1)^2>k+1$, we would have
\[
q_{(k+1)^2}>q_{k+1}.
\]
On the other hand, by (1) and (2),
\[
q_{(k+1)^2}=p_{k+1}
=\max\{q_k,q_{k+1}\}
=q_{k+1},
\]
a contradiction.

Therefore there is a first integer $\ell\ge k+1$ such that
\[
q_\ell>q_{\ell+1}.
\]
By the minimality of $\ell$,
\[
q_{\ell-1}<q_\ell,
\]
so $\ell\in S$. But $\ell>m$, contradicting the maximality of $m$.

Hence $S$ is infinite, and so there are infinitely many required integers $n$.
