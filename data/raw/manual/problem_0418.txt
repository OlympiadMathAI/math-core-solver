ID: 0418

SOURCE: IMO Shortlist

YEAR: 2023

TOPIC: Number Theory

SUBTOPICS: Divisor Sequences, Prime Powers, Divisibility, p-Adic Valuations

STATEMENT:
Determine all positive composite integers $n$ with the following property:

If the positive divisors of $n$ are
\[
1=d_1<d_2<\cdots<d_k=n,
\]
then
\[
d_i\mid d_{i+1}+d_{i+2}
\qquad(1\le i\le k-2).
\]

SOLUTION 1:
The answer is
\[
\boxed{n=p^r},
\]
where $p$ is prime and $r\ge2$.

Every such prime power works. Indeed, its divisors are
\[
d_i=p^{i-1}
\qquad(1\le i\le r+1),
\]
and
\[
d_{i+1}+d_{i+2}=p^i+p^{i+1}
\]
is divisible by $d_i=p^{i-1}$.

Conversely, suppose that $n$ satisfies the condition and has at least two distinct prime divisors. Let
\[
p<q
\]
be the two smallest prime divisors of $n$. All divisors of $n$ smaller than $q$ are powers of $p$. Hence, for some $j\ge1$, the divisor list contains
\[
d_{j+1}=p^j,
\qquad
d_{j+2}=q.
\]
Since divisors pair symmetrically,
\[
d_i d_{k+1-i}=n.
\]
Therefore
\[
d_{k-j-1}=\frac nq,
\qquad
d_{k-j}=\frac{n}{p^j},
\qquad
d_{k-j+1}=\frac{n}{p^{j-1}}.
\]
Applying the given condition at index $k-j-1$,
\[
\frac nq
\mid
\frac{n}{p^j}+\frac{n}{p^{j-1}}
=
\frac{n(p+1)}{p^j}.
\]
After cancellation,
\[
p^j\mid q(p+1).
\]
This is impossible because
\[
p\nmid q
\qquad\text{and}\qquad
p\nmid p+1.
\]
Thus $n$ has only one prime divisor and is a prime power.

SOLUTION 2:
Again, prime powers clearly satisfy the condition. We prove that no other composite integer does.

For every $i$,
\[
d_i d_{k+1-i}=n.
\]
Apply the original condition to the divisors symmetric to
\[
d_i,\ d_{i+1},\ d_{i+2}.
\]
We obtain
\[
\frac{n}{d_{i+2}}
\mid
\frac{n}{d_{i+1}}+\frac{n}{d_i}.
\]
Multiplying by
\[
\frac{d_i d_{i+1}d_{i+2}}n
\]
gives
\[
d_i d_{i+1}
\mid
d_i d_{i+2}+d_{i+1}d_{i+2}.
\]
In particular,
\[
d_i\mid d_{i+1}d_{i+2}.
\tag{1}
\]

The given condition also gives
\[
d_i\mid d_{i+1}+d_{i+2}.
\]
Multiplying this divisibility by $d_{i+1}$ and using (1),
\[
d_i\mid d_{i+1}^2
\qquad(1\le i\le k-2).
\tag{2}
\]

Let
\[
d_2=p,
\]
the smallest prime divisor of $n$. We prove inductively that
\[
p\mid d_i
\qquad(2\le i\le k-1).
\]
The statement is clear for $i=2$. If $p\mid d_i$, then (2) gives
\[
p\mid d_i\mid d_{i+1}^2,
\]
so $p\mid d_{i+1}$.

If $n$ had another prime divisor $q\ne p$, then $q$ would occur among
\[
d_2,\ldots,d_{k-1},
\]
and the preceding conclusion would give $p\mid q$, impossible. Hence $n$ is a prime power.

SOLUTION 3:
We first prove that
\[
d_i\mid d_{i+1}
\qquad(1\le i\le k-1).
\tag{1}
\]

The case $i=1$ is immediate. Suppose $2\le i\le k-1$ and that
\[
d_{i-1}\mid d_i.
\]
The original condition at index $i-1$ gives
\[
d_{i-1}\mid d_i+d_{i+1},
\]
and hence
\[
d_{i-1}\mid d_{i+1}.
\tag{2}
\]

Apply the condition to the symmetric divisors:
\[
\frac{n}{d_{i+1}}
\mid
\frac{n}{d_i}+\frac{n}{d_{i-1}}.
\]
Dividing by $n/d_{i+1}$, we find that
\[
\frac{d_{i+1}}{d_i}
+
\frac{d_{i+1}}{d_{i-1}}
\]
is an integer. The second term is an integer by (2), so
\[
\frac{d_{i+1}}{d_i}
\]
is also an integer. This proves (1) by induction.

Now let $p$ be the smallest prime divisor of $n$. Since $p=d_2$, relation (1) shows that $p$ divides every divisor after $d_2$. If $q$ were a second prime divisor of $n$, then $p\mid q$, a contradiction. Thus $n$ is a prime power.

Conversely, if $n=p^r$ with $r\ge2$, then its divisors are $1,p,\ldots,p^r$, and
\[
p^{i-1}\mid p^i+p^{i+1}.
\]
Thus every composite prime power works.

SOLUTION 4:
Prime powers satisfy the condition, so only necessity remains.

Assume that $n$ has two distinct prime divisors, and let
\[
p<q
\]
be the two smallest ones. For some $j\ge1$, the consecutive divisors immediately before and at $q$ are
\[
p^j,\qquad q.
\]
By the symmetry of the divisor list, the condition gives
\[
\frac nq
\mid
\frac{n}{p^j}+\frac{n}{p^{j-1}}
=
\frac{n(p+1)}{p^j}.
\tag{1}
\]

Take $p$-adic valuations in (1). Since
\[
p\nmid q(p+1),
\]
the valuation of the divisor on the left is
\[
v_p\left(\frac nq\right)=v_p(n),
\]
whereas the valuation of the expression on the right is
\[
v_p\left(\frac{n(p+1)}{p^j}\right)=v_p(n)-j.
\]
This is impossible because a divisor cannot have larger $p$-adic valuation than its multiple. Therefore $n$ has only one prime divisor, and
\[
n=p^r
\qquad(r\ge2).
\]
