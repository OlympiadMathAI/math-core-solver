ID: 0379

SOURCE: IMO Shortlist

YEAR: 2022

TOPIC: Combinatorics

SUBTOPICS: Order-Preserving Bijections, Lattice Point Rankings, Parity, Extremal Constructions

STATEMENT:
Let
\[
f:\mathbb Z_{\ge0}\times\mathbb Z_{\ge0}\to\mathbb Z_{\ge0}
\]
be a bijection such that
\[
f(x_1,y_1)>f(x_2,y_2)
\]
always implies
\[
f(x_1+1,y_1)>f(x_2+1,y_2)
\]
and
\[
f(x_1,y_1+1)>f(x_2,y_2+1).
\]

Let $N$ be the number of pairs $(x,y)$ satisfying
\[
0\le x,y<100
\]
for which $f(x,y)$ is odd.

Determine the smallest and largest possible values of $N$.

SOLUTION 1:
The answer is
\[
\boxed{2500\le N\le7500},
\]
and both bounds are attainable.

We first describe the order induced by $f$.

Because $f$ is injective, translating two lattice points by the same nonnegative vector preserves their strict order in both directions. In particular,
\[
f(x+1,y)>f(x,y)
\qquad\text{and}\qquad
f(x,y+1)>f(x,y);
\]
otherwise one of the rows or columns would give an infinite strictly decreasing sequence of nonnegative integers.

For positive integers $k,\ell$, call the rational number $k/\ell$ a \emph{grade} if
\[
f(x+k,y)>f(x,y+\ell)
\tag{1}
\]
for every $x,y\ge0$. The direction of the inequality in (1) does not depend on $x,y$, because any two such comparisons can be translated to a common base point.

Needles are closed under addition. They also have the following division property: if a positive integer multiple of a vector is a needle, then the vector itself is a needle. Otherwise the reverse inequality for the smaller vector, added repeatedly, would make its multiple a non-needle.

Grades are therefore closed upwards. Indeed, suppose
\[
\frac{k_1}{\ell_1}<\frac{k_2}{\ell_2}
\]
and $k_1/\ell_1$ is a grade. Scaling gives that
\[
(k_1\ell_2,\ell_1\ell_2)
\]
has property (1). Adding the positive horizontal vector
\[
(k_2\ell_1-k_1\ell_2,0)
\]
and then dividing by $\ell_1$ shows that $k_2/\ell_2$ is also a grade.

At least one grade exists. Otherwise,
\[
f(k,0)<f(0,1)
\]
for every positive integer $k$, which is impossible because the left side assumes infinitely many distinct nonnegative values below one fixed number. Similarly, not every positive rational is a grade: if every $1/\ell$ were a grade, then
\[
f(1,0)>f(0,\ell)
\]
for every $\ell$, again impossible.

Let
\[
\alpha=\inf\{q>0:q\text{ is a grade}\}.
\]
Then the preceding monotonicity gives the key property
\[
x_1+\alpha y_1>x_2+\alpha y_2
\quad\Longrightarrow\quad
f(x_1,y_1)>f(x_2,y_2).
\tag{2}
\]
For example, if $x_1\ge x_2$ and $y_1<y_2$, the hypothesis in (2) says
\[
\frac{x_1-x_2}{y_2-y_1}>\alpha,
\]
so this ratio is a grade. The other mixed case is analogous, using a non-grade. Thus $f$ orders lattice points according to the value of
\[
x+\alpha y,
\]
with a consistent tie-breaking rule if $\alpha$ is rational.

For the finitely many values of $f$ needed on
\[
0\le x,y\le100,
\]
we may perturb $\alpha$ slightly, on the side prescribed by the tie-breaking rule, without changing their order or ranks. We may therefore assume that $\alpha$ is irrational. Then all numbers $x+\alpha y$ are distinct and
\[
f(a,b)
=
\#\bigl\{(x,y)\in\mathbb Z_{\ge0}^2:
x+\alpha y<a+\alpha b
\bigr\}.
\tag{3}
\]

We derive a local identity. The difference
\[
f(x+1,y)-f(x,y)
\]
counts lattice points whose weights lie in
\[
[x+\alpha y,\ x+1+\alpha y).
\]
Likewise,
\[
f(x+1,y+1)-f(x,y+1)
\]
counts points in the translate of this interval by $\alpha$. Among the latter points, those with positive second coordinate correspond bijectively, after decreasing that coordinate by $1$, to the points in the first interval. There is exactly one additional point with second coordinate $0$, because the interval has length $1$ and irrational endpoints. Hence
\[
f(x+1,y+1)-f(x,y+1)
=
f(x+1,y)-f(x,y)+1,
\]
or
\[
f(x,y)+f(x+1,y+1)
=
f(x+1,y)+f(x,y+1)+1.
\tag{4}
\]

Reduce (4) modulo $2$. Every unit square of lattice points has an odd number of odd $f$-values, so it has either one or three. Partition the $100\times100$ set of points into the $2500$ disjoint groups
\[
\{(2i,2j),(2i+1,2j),(2i,2j+1),(2i+1,2j+1)\},
\]
where
\[
0\le i,j<50.
\]
Each group contains between one and three odd values. Therefore
\[
2500\le N\le7500.
\tag{5}
\]

We now construct functions attaining both bounds. For any positive irrational $\alpha$, ordering all lattice points by increasing $x+\alpha y$ and assigning the ranks
\[
0,1,2,\ldots
\]
defines a bijection $f_\alpha$ satisfying the required translation property.

Choose an irrational number
\[
\alpha_+=200+\varepsilon,
\qquad
0<\varepsilon<\frac1{100}.
\]
For $0\le r\le100$,
\[
f_{\alpha_+}(r,0)=r.
\tag{6}
\]
For $0\le k\le100$, formula (3) gives
\[
\begin{aligned}
f_{\alpha_+}(0,k)
&=
\sum_{q=1}^k\#\{x\in\mathbb Z_{\ge0}:x<q\alpha_+\}\\
&=
\sum_{q=1}^k(200q+1)
\equiv k\pmod2.
\end{aligned}
\tag{7}
\]

Let
\[
p_{x,y}=f_{\alpha_+}(x,y)\pmod2
\]
and
\[
q_{x,y}=p_{x+1,y}+p_{x,y}\pmod2.
\]
Equation (4) gives
\[
q_{x,y+1}=q_{x,y}+1\pmod2.
\tag{8}
\]
By (6),
\[
q_{x,0}=1.
\]
Hence adjacent parities alternate in every even-indexed row, while every odd-indexed row is constant. Equation (7) shows that each odd row consists entirely of odd values. Thus the $50$ even rows contribute $50$ odd values each, and the $50$ odd rows contribute $100$ each:
\[
N=50\cdot50+50\cdot100=7500.
\]

For the lower bound, choose instead
\[
\alpha_-=200-\varepsilon,
\qquad
0<\varepsilon<\frac1{100},
\]
irrational. Then
\[
f_{\alpha_-}(r,0)=r
\]
and
\[
f_{\alpha_-}(0,k)
=
\sum_{q=1}^k200q
\equiv0\pmod2.
\]
Equation (8) again shows that the even rows alternate, while every odd row is constant; now all odd rows are even. Therefore
\[
N=50\cdot50=2500.
\]

Both bounds in (5) are attained, completing the solution.
