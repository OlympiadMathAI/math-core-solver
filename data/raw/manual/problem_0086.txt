ID: 0086

SOURCE: IMO Shortlist

YEAR: 2012

TOPIC: Number Theory

SUBTOPICS: Multiplicative Orders, Prime Divisors, Euler's Totient Theorem, Congruences Modulo 4

STATEMENT:
Let $x$ and $y$ be positive integers. Suppose that
\[
2^n y+1\mid x^{2^n}-1
\]
for every positive integer $n$. Prove that $x=1$.

SOLUTION 1:
We first prove an auxiliary fact.

\textbf{Lemma.} For every positive integer $y$, there are infinitely many primes $p\equiv3\pmod4$ such that $p$ divides at least one number of the form
\[
2^n y+1,
\qquad n\ge1.
\]

\textbf{Proof.} It is enough to prove the lemma when $y$ is odd. Indeed, if $y=2^s y_0$ with $y_0$ odd, the odd case supplies infinitely many relevant primes dividing numbers $2^m y_0+1$. Only finitely many primes divide the finite collection with $m\le s$, so infinitely many arise with $m>s$. For such an exponent, putting $n=m-s$ gives
\[
2^m y_0+1=2^n y+1.
\]

Now assume that $y$ is odd, and write
\[
2y+1=p_1^{e_1}p_2^{e_2}\cdots p_r^{e_r}.
\tag{1}
\]
Suppose, for a contradiction, that only finitely many primes congruent to $3$ modulo $4$ divide numbers of the form $2^n y+1$. Let
\[
p_{r+1},\ldots,p_{r+s}
\]
be all such primes that do not already divide $2y+1$.

Set
\[
M=p_1^{e_1+1}\cdots p_r^{e_r+1}p_{r+1}\cdots p_{r+s}
\]
and choose
\[
n=1+\varphi(M).
\]
Since $M$ is odd, Euler's theorem gives
\[
2^n y+1\equiv2y+1\pmod M.
\tag{2}
\]
For $1\le i\le r$, congruence (2) shows that
\[
p_i^{e_i}\parallel 2^n y+1.
\tag{3}
\]
For $r+1\le i\le r+s$, it shows that
\[
p_i\nmid2^n y+1.
\tag{4}
\]
Therefore every prime factor of $2^n y+1$ that is not among $p_1,\ldots,p_r$ is congruent to $1$ modulo $4$. From (1), (3), and (4),
\[
2^n y+1
\equiv
p_1^{e_1}\cdots p_r^{e_r}
\equiv
2y+1
\equiv3
\pmod4.
\]
But $n>1$, so $2^n y+1\equiv1\pmod4$, a contradiction. The lemma follows.

We now return to the problem. Let $p$ be a prime divisor of some $2^n y+1$. By hypothesis,
\[
x^{2^n}\equiv1\pmod p.
\tag{5}
\]
In particular $p\nmid x$. Let $d$ be the multiplicative order of $x$ modulo $p$. Equation (5) gives
\[
d\mid2^n,
\]
while Fermat's little theorem gives
\[
d\mid p-1.
\]
Hence
\[
d\mid\gcd(2^n,p-1).
\tag{6}
\]

If $p\equiv3\pmod4$, then $p-1$ is divisible by $2$ but not by $4$, and therefore
\[
\gcd(2^n,p-1)=2.
\]
By (6),
\[
x^2\equiv1\pmod p.
\]
Thus every prime $p\equiv3\pmod4$ supplied by the lemma divides the fixed integer
\[
x^2-1.
\]
There are infinitely many such primes. This is impossible unless $x^2-1=0$. Since $x$ is positive,
\[
x=1.
\]
