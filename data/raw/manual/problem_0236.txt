ID: 0236

SOURCE: IMO Shortlist

YEAR: 2017

TOPIC: Number Theory

SUBTOPICS: Rational Tuples, Vieta Jumping, Diophantine Equations, Symmetric Expressions

STATEMENT:
Find the smallest positive integer $n$, or prove that none exists, for which there are infinitely many distinct $n$-tuples of positive rational numbers
\[
(a_1,a_2,\ldots,a_n)
\]
such that both
\[
a_1+a_2+\cdots+a_n
\]
and
\[
\frac1{a_1}+\frac1{a_2}+\cdots+\frac1{a_n}
\]
are integers.

SOLUTION 1:
The answer is
\[
\boxed{n=3}.
\]

For $n=1$, positivity and integrality of both $a_1$ and $1/a_1$ force
\[
a_1=1,
\]
so there is only one tuple.

We next show that only finitely many pairs work. Write
\[
x=\frac ab,
\qquad
y=\frac cd,
\]
in lowest terms. The conditions
\[
x+y\in\mathbb Z
\qquad\text{and}\qquad
\frac1x+\frac1y\in\mathbb Z
\]
are equivalent to
\[
bd\mid ad+bc
\qquad\text{and}\qquad
ac\mid ad+bc.
\tag{1}
\]
The first divisibility gives
\[
d\mid b
\qquad\text{and}\qquad
b\mid d,
\]
so $b=d$. The second gives analogously $a=c$. Thus $x=y$. We then need
\[
2x\in\mathbb Z
\qquad\text{and}\qquad
\frac2x\in\mathbb Z.
\]
Writing $x=a/b$ in lowest terms, this forces $a,b\mid2$. Hence
\[
(x,y)\in
\left\{
\left(\frac12,\frac12\right),(1,1),(2,2)
\right\}.
\]
There are therefore only finitely many suitable pairs.

It remains to construct infinitely many triples. Seek triples of the form
\[
(x,y,z)=
\left(
\frac1{1+b+c},
\frac b{1+b+c},
\frac c{1+b+c}
\right),
\tag{2}
\]
where $b,c$ are positive integers. Their sum is $1$. Their reciprocal sum is integral whenever
\[
\frac1b+\frac1c+\frac cb+\frac bc=3,
\]
or equivalently
\[
b^2+c^2-3bc+b+c=0.
\tag{3}
\]
Indeed, under (3), the reciprocal sum of (2) is
\[
b+c+6.
\]

Equation (3) has infinitely many positive integer solutions. Starting with
\[
(b,c)=(2,3),
\]
suppose $c\ge b$ and regard (3) as a quadratic equation in $b$:
\[
b^2-(3c-1)b+(c^2+c)=0.
\]
If $b$ is one root, the other root is
\[
b'=\frac{c^2+c}{b}.
\]
It is a positive integer, and
\[
b'\ge\frac{c^2+c}{c}=c+1>c.
\]
Thus
\[
(b,c)\longmapsto(c,b')
\]
produces a new and strictly larger solution. The first pairs are
\[
(2,3),(3,6),(6,14),(14,35),(35,90),\ldots.
\]
They yield infinitely many distinct triples through (2). Hence $n=3$ works and is minimal.

SOLUTION 2:
For a positive rational $n$-tuple $\mathbf a=(a_1,\ldots,a_n)$, define
\[
F(\mathbf a)=
(a_1+\cdots+a_n)
\left(\frac1{a_1}+\cdots+\frac1{a_n}\right).
\]
Call a tuple \emph{pretty} if $F(\mathbf a)$ is an integer. Every tuple satisfying the problem is pretty. Conversely, if $(b_1,\ldots,b_n)$ is pretty, then
\[
\left(
\frac{b_1}{b_1+\cdots+b_n},\ldots,
\frac{b_n}{b_1+\cdots+b_n}
\right)
\]
has sum $1$ and reciprocal sum $F(b_1,\ldots,b_n)$, so it satisfies the problem.

The quantity $F$ is unchanged by scaling all entries by the same positive rational number. Each scaling class contains a unique primitive positive integral tuple, meaning one whose entries have greatest common divisor $1$.

A fixed scaling class contains only finitely many tuples satisfying the original two integrality conditions. Indeed, if $t(b_1,\ldots,b_n)$ is such a tuple and
\[
B=b_1+\cdots+b_n,
\qquad
R=\frac1{b_1}+\cdots+\frac1{b_n},
\]
then $tB$ and $R/t$ are positive integers whose product is the fixed integer
\[
BR=F(b_1,\ldots,b_n).
\]
There are only finitely many such factor pairs. Consequently, infinitely many tuples can exist only if there are infinitely many primitive pretty tuples.

For $n=1$ there is only one primitive tuple. For $n=2$, if $a,b$ are coprime positive integers and
\[
F(a,b)=\frac{(a+b)^2}{ab}
\]
is integral, then $a\mid(a+b)^2$ and $\gcd(a,a+b)=1$, so $a=1$; similarly $b=1$. Thus there is again only one primitive tuple.

We construct infinitely many primitive pretty triples. Fix positive integers $b,c$, and write
\[
\sigma=b+c,
\qquad
\tau=bc.
\]
The equation
\[
F(a,b,c)=k
\]
is equivalent to
\[
\sigma a^2+
\bigl(\sigma^2-(k-1)\tau\bigr)a+
\sigma\tau=0.
\tag{4}
\]
Its discriminant can be written as
\[
\Delta=
\bigl((k+1)\tau-\sigma^2\bigr)^2-4k\tau^2.
\]
It becomes a square if we impose
\[
(k+1)\tau-\sigma^2=\tau^2+k.
\]
This is equivalent to
\[
\sigma^2=(\tau-1)(k-\tau).
\tag{5}
\]
Thus, whenever
\[
\tau-1\mid\sigma^2,
\]
we obtain an integer $k$ and rational roots
\[
a=\frac{\sigma}{\tau-1}
\qquad\text{or}\qquad
a=\frac{\tau(\tau-1)}{\sigma}.
\tag{6}
\]

Now impose
\[
(b+c)^2=5(bc-1).
\tag{7}
\]
This is equivalent to
\[
b^2-3bc+c^2+5=0.
\]
For fixed $c$, if $b$ is one root, the other root is $3c-b$. Therefore
\[
(b,c)\longmapsto(c,3c-b)
\]
preserves (7). Starting from $(2,3)$ gives the infinite recurrence
\[
v_1=2,\qquad v_2=3,\qquad
v_{i+2}=3v_{i+1}-v_i,
\]
with solutions
\[
(b,c)=(v_i,v_{i+1}).
\]
The entries are strictly increasing.

For such a pair, the first value in (6) is
\[
a=\frac5{b+c}.
\]
After multiplying by $b+c$, the pretty triple is equivalent to
\[
\bigl(5,\ b(b+c),\ c(b+c)\bigr).
\]
After dividing by its common gcd if necessary, we obtain a primitive pretty triple. The ratio of its last two entries is $b:c$, and these ratios are all distinct. Hence we obtain infinitely many pairwise distinct primitive pretty triples.

Therefore the smallest possible value is $n=3$.
