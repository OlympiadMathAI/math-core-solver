ID: 0295

SOURCE: IMO Shortlist

YEAR: 2019

TOPIC: Number Theory

SUBTOPICS: Binomial Coefficients, Modular Arithmetic, p-adic Valuations, Lucas Theorem, Dirichlet's Theorem

STATEMENT:
Let $a$ be a positive integer. A positive integer $b$ is called \emph{$a$-good} if
\[
\binom{an}{b}-1
\]
is divisible by
\[
an+1
\]
for every positive integer $n$ satisfying $an\ge b$.

Suppose that $b$ is $a$-good, but $b+2$ is not $a$-good. Prove that
\[
b+1
\]
is prime.

SOLUTION 1:
We first characterize the $a$-good integers.

\textbf{Claim.}
A positive integer $b$ is $a$-good if and only if:

\begin{itemize}
\item $b$ is even; and
\item every prime $p\le b$ divides $a$.
\end{itemize}

Suppose first that there is a prime $p\le b$ with
\[
p\nmid a.
\]
Put
\[
t=v_p(b!).
\]
Since $a$ is invertible modulo $p^{t+1}$, there are arbitrarily large positive integers $c$ such that
\[
ac\equiv1\pmod{p^{t+1}}.
\]
Choose such a $c$, and put
\[
n=(p-1)c.
\]
Then
\[
an\equiv p-1\pmod{p^{t+1}},
\]
so
\[
p^{t+1}\mid an-p+1.
\]
Because $p\le b$, the factor $an-p+1$ occurs in the numerator
\[
an(an-1)\cdots(an-b+1)
\]
of $\binom{an}{b}$. Therefore
\[
p\mid\binom{an}{b}.
\]
On the other hand,
\[
p\mid an+1.
\]
Hence
\[
\binom{an}{b}-1\not\equiv0\pmod p,
\]
and $b$ is not $a$-good.

Conversely, suppose that every prime $p\le b$ divides $a$. Then
\[
\gcd(b!,an+1)=1,
\]
so $b!$ is invertible modulo $an+1$. We have
\[
\begin{aligned}
an(an-1)\cdots(an-b+1)
&\equiv
(-1)(-2)\cdots(-b)\\
&=(-1)^b b!
\pmod{an+1}.
\end{aligned}
\]
Thus
\[
\binom{an}{b}\equiv(-1)^b\pmod{an+1}.
\tag{1}
\]
If $b$ is even, (1) proves that $b$ is $a$-good.

If $b$ is odd, choose $n$ so large that
\[
an+1>2.
\]
Then
\[
\binom{an}{b}-1\equiv-2\not\equiv0\pmod{an+1},
\]
so $b$ is not $a$-good. The claim is proved.

Now assume that $b$ is $a$-good but $b+2$ is not. By the claim, $b$ is even, and every prime at most $b$ divides $a$. Since $b+2$ is even, its failure to be $a$-good means that there is a prime
\[
q\le b+2
\]
with
\[
q\nmid a.
\]
This prime cannot satisfy $q\le b$. Hence
\[
q=b+1
\qquad\text{or}\qquad
q=b+2.
\]
But $b+2>2$ is even, so it is not prime. Therefore
\[
q=b+1,
\]
and consequently
\[
\boxed{b+1\text{ is prime}}.
\]

SOLUTION 2:
We first prove, using Lucas' theorem, that if $b$ is $a$-good, then every prime $p\le b$ divides $a$.

Suppose instead that
\[
p\le b
\qquad\text{and}\qquad
p\nmid a.
\]
Write $b$ in base $p$. Since $p\le b$, some digit other than the units digit is nonzero; let the digit in the $p^t$ place be nonzero, where $t\ge1$.

Because multiplication by $a$ permutes the residue classes modulo $p^{t+1}$, we can choose a sufficiently large $n$ such that the base-$p$ expansion of $an$ has:

\begin{itemize}
\item units digit $p-1$, so $p\mid an+1$;
\item digit $0$ in the $p^t$ place.
\end{itemize}

Lucas' theorem then gives
\[
\binom{an}{b}\equiv0\pmod p,
\]
because the $p^t$ digit of $b$ is positive while the corresponding digit of $an$ is zero. Thus
\[
\binom{an}{b}-1\not\equiv0\pmod p,
\]
although $p\mid an+1$. This contradicts the assumption that $b$ is $a$-good.

Now suppose that $b$ is $a$-good but $b+2$ is not. Choose $n$ witnessing the failure of $b+2$, and write
\[
M=an+1.
\]
Since $b$ is $a$-good,
\[
\binom{an}{b}\equiv1\pmod M.
\tag{1}
\]
The identity
\[
\binom{an}{b+2}
=
\binom{an}{b}
\frac{(an-b)(an-b-1)}{(b+1)(b+2)}
\tag{2}
\]
shows that if $(b+1)(b+2)$ were invertible modulo $M$, then
\[
\frac{(an-b)(an-b-1)}{(b+1)(b+2)}
\equiv1\pmod M,
\]
because
\[
(an-b)(an-b-1)
\equiv
(b+1)(b+2)
\pmod M.
\]
Equations (1)--(2) would then give
\[
\binom{an}{b+2}\equiv1\pmod M,
\]
contrary to the choice of $n$.

Therefore some prime $q$ divides both
\[
M=an+1
\]
and
\[
(b+1)(b+2).
\]
Every prime at most $b$ divides $a$, and hence cannot divide $an+1$. Thus
\[
q=b+1
\qquad\text{or}\qquad
q=b+2,
\]
so at least one of $b+1,b+2$ is prime.

It remains to prove that $b$ is even. By Dirichlet's theorem, there are arbitrarily large $n$ for which
\[
an+1
\]
is prime. For such an $n$, every prime divisor of $b!$ divides $a$, so $b!$ is invertible modulo $an+1$. As in Solution 1,
\[
\binom{an}{b}\equiv(-1)^b\pmod{an+1}.
\]
Since $b$ is $a$-good, the left side is congruent to $1$, and therefore $b$ is even.

Hence $b+2>2$ is even and cannot be prime. The prime among $b+1,b+2$ must be
\[
\boxed{b+1}.
\]
