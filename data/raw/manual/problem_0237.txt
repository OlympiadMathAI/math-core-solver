ID: 0237

SOURCE: IMO Shortlist

YEAR: 2017

TOPIC: Number Theory

SUBTOPICS: Homogeneous Polynomials, Chinese Remainder Theorem, Euler's Theorem, Interpolation

STATEMENT:
An ordered pair $(x,y)$ of integers is called an \emph{irreducible lattice point} if
\[
\gcd(x,y)=1.
\]
For every finite set $S$ of irreducible lattice points, prove that there exists a homogeneous polynomial
\[
f(x,y)\in\mathbb Z[x,y]
\]
of degree at least $1$ such that
\[
f(x,y)=1
\]
for every $(x,y)\in S$.

A homogeneous polynomial of degree $n$ is a nonzero polynomial of the form
\[
a_0x^n+a_1x^{n-1}y+\cdots+a_{n-1}xy^{n-1}+a_ny^n.
\]

SOLUTION 1:
If $S$ is empty, the polynomial $f(x,y)=x$ works. Assume henceforth that $S$ is nonempty.

It is enough first to construct a homogeneous polynomial taking values $\pm1$ on $S$, because its square then takes the value $1$ everywhere on $S$.

If two irreducible lattice points lie on the same line through the origin, they are negatives of one another. A homogeneous polynomial takes values differing only by a sign at such a pair. We may therefore keep one representative from each such line and square the final polynomial. Write the remaining points as
\[
(x_1,y_1),\ldots,(x_n,y_n),
\]
with no two collinear with the origin.

For each $i$, define
\[
\ell_i(x,y)=y_i x-x_i y
\]
and
\[
g_i(x,y)=\prod_{j\ne i}\ell_j(x,y).
\]
Then
\[
g_i(x_j,y_j)=0\quad(j\ne i),
\qquad
a_i:=g_i(x_i,y_i)\ne0.
\tag{1}
\]
Because $\gcd(x_i,y_i)=1$, there is a linear homogeneous polynomial $L_i$ with integer coefficients such that
\[
L_i(x_i,y_i)=1.
\]
Thus, for every $N\ge n-1$,
\[
h_i(x,y)=L_i(x,y)^{N-n+1}g_i(x,y)
\]
is homogeneous of degree $N$ and satisfies the same interpolation conditions (1).

We need the following universal congruence construction.

\textbf{Claim.} For every positive integer $A$, there is a positive-degree homogeneous polynomial
\[
F_A(x,y)\in\mathbb Z[x,y]
\]
such that
\[
F_A(x,y)\equiv1\pmod A
\]
whenever $\gcd(x,y)=1$.

For $A=1$, take $F_A(x,y)=x$. For a prime power $A=p^r$, take
\[
F_A(x,y)=
\bigl(x^{p-1}+y^{p-1}\bigr)^{\varphi(A)}
\]
if $p$ is odd, and
\[
F_A(x,y)=
\bigl(x^2+xy+y^2\bigr)^{\varphi(A)}
\]
if $p=2$. In either case the expression inside the parentheses is coprime to $p$ whenever $\gcd(x,y)=1$, so Euler's theorem gives the desired congruence.

For general
\[
A=q_1q_2\cdots q_s,
\]
where the $q_i$ are pairwise coprime prime powers, raise the corresponding $F_{q_i}$ to suitable powers so that all have the same degree; call the resulting polynomials $G_i$. Choose integers $c_i$ satisfying
\[
\sum_{i=1}^s c_i\frac{A}{q_i}=1.
\]
Then
\[
F_A(x,y)=
\sum_{i=1}^s c_i\frac{A}{q_i}G_i(x,y)
\]
is homogeneous and is congruent to $1$ modulo every $q_i$, hence modulo $A$. This proves the claim.

Now take
\[
A=\operatorname{lcm}(|a_1|,\ldots,|a_n|).
\]
Choose a power $F_A^k$ whose degree is some $N\ge n-1$, and choose the polynomials $h_i$ above with the same degree. Since
\[
F_A(x_i,y_i)^k\equiv1\pmod{|a_i|},
\]
the integers
\[
\lambda_i=
\frac{F_A(x_i,y_i)^k-1}{a_i}
\]
are well defined. Set
\[
G(x,y)=F_A(x,y)^k-
\sum_{i=1}^n\lambda_i h_i(x,y).
\]
At $(x_i,y_i)$, all terms $h_j$ with $j\ne i$ vanish, while
\[
h_i(x_i,y_i)=a_i.
\]
Therefore
\[
G(x_i,y_i)=1.
\]
If opposite points were discarded at the beginning, replace $G$ by $G^2$. This gives the required polynomial on the original set $S$.

SOLUTION 2:
If $S$ is empty, take $f(x,y)=x$. Otherwise, as in Solution 1, keep one representative from each line through the origin, construct a polynomial taking the value $1$ at those representatives, and square it at the end if opposite points were removed.

We induct on the number of representatives
\[
(x_1,y_1),\ldots,(x_n,y_n).
\]
For $n=1$, Bezout's identity gives integers $c,d$ such that
\[
cx_1+dy_1=1,
\]
so $cx+dy$ works.

Assume $n\ge2$, and let $g(x,y)$ be a homogeneous polynomial of degree $d\ge1$ satisfying
\[
g(x_i,y_i)=1
\qquad(1\le i<n).
\]
Define
\[
g_n(x,y)=\prod_{i=1}^{n-1}(y_i x-x_i y)
\]
and
\[
A=g_n(x_n,y_n)\ne0.
\]
Choose integers $c,d_0$ with
\[
cx_n+d_0y_n=1.
\]
We seek a polynomial of the form
\[
f(x,y)=
g(x,y)^K-
C\,g_n(x,y)(cx+d_0y)^L,
\tag{2}
\]
where
\[
L=Kd-(n-1)>0.
\]
Then (2) is homogeneous, and its value at each of the first $n-1$ points is automatically $1$.

It remains to choose $K$ so that
\[
g(x_n,y_n)^K\equiv1\pmod A.
\tag{3}
\]
Let $p$ be a prime divisor of $A$. Then, for some $i<n$,
\[
y_i x_n-x_i y_n\equiv0\pmod p.
\tag{4}
\]
At least one of $x_ix_n$ and $y_iy_n$ is nonzero modulo $p$. Otherwise either one irreducible point would have both coordinates divisible by $p$, or the cross-divisibility would make the left side of (4) nonzero.

If $p\nmid x_ix_n$, homogeneity and (4) give
\[
\begin{aligned}
x_i^d g(x_n,y_n)
&=g(x_ix_n,x_i y_n)\\
&\equiv g(x_ix_n,y_ix_n)\\
&=x_n^d g(x_i,y_i)
=x_n^d
\pmod p.
\end{aligned}
\]
Fermat's theorem therefore gives
\[
g(x_n,y_n)^{p-1}\equiv1\pmod p.
\]
If instead $p\nmid y_i y_n$, the same argument with the $y$-coordinates gives the identical conclusion.

If $p^\alpha\mid A$, the elementary lifting fact
\[
u\equiv1\pmod p
\quad\Longrightarrow\quad
u^{p^{\alpha-1}}\equiv1\pmod{p^\alpha}
\]
shows that
\[
g(x_n,y_n)^{p^{\alpha-1}(p-1)}
\equiv1\pmod{p^\alpha}.
\]
Thus a sufficiently large multiple of $\varphi(|A|)$ satisfies (3). Choose, for example,
\[
K=n\varphi(|A|),
\]
using the convention $\varphi(1)=1$. This also guarantees $L>0$.

Now define
\[
C=\frac{g(x_n,y_n)^K-1}{A}.
\]
Then (2) also satisfies
\[
f(x_n,y_n)=1.
\]
This completes the induction. Squaring at the end, if necessary, restores any discarded opposite points.
