ID: 0078

SOURCE: IMO Shortlist

YEAR: 2012

TOPIC: Geometry

SUBTOPICS: Miquel Point, Incenter, Oriented Angles, Cyclic Quadrilaterals, Power of a Point, Locus

STATEMENT:
Let $ABC$ be a triangle with circumcenter $O$ and incenter $I$. Points $D,E,F$ lie on the sides $BC,CA,AB$, respectively, and satisfy
\[
BD+BF=CA,
\qquad
CD+CE=AB.
\]
The circumcircles of triangles $BFD$ and $CDE$ meet at $P\ne D$. Prove that
\[
OP=OI.
\]

SOLUTION 1:
Denote
\[
\omega_A=(AEF),\qquad \omega_B=(BFD),\qquad \omega_C=(CDE).
\]
By Miquel's theorem, the three circles $\omega_A,\omega_B,\omega_C$ have a common point. Since $P\ne D$ is the second common point of $\omega_B$ and $\omega_C$, we have
\[
P\in\omega_A.
\tag{1}
\]

The two given equalities imply a third one. Indeed,
\[
BD+CD=BC,
\qquad
BF+AF=AB,
\qquad
CE+AE=AC.
\]
Adding the two given conditions and substituting these identities gives
\[
AE+AF=BC.
\tag{2}
\]

Let the circles $\omega_A,\omega_B,\omega_C$ meet the angle bisectors $AI,BI,CI$ again at $A',B',C'$, respectively. We shall prove that
\[
OI=OA'=OB'=OC'.
\tag{3}
\]

We first establish a lemma.

\textbf{Lemma.} Let a circle through the vertex $A$ of an angle of measure $\alpha$ meet the two sides at $X,Y$ and the internal angle bisector again at $L$. Then
\[
AX+AY=2AL\cos\frac{\alpha}{2}.
\tag{4}
\]

\textbf{Proof of the lemma.} Since $AL$ bisects $\angle XAY$, the chords $LX$ and $LY$ are equal. Put
\[
LX=LY=u,
\qquad
XY=v.
\]
Ptolemy's theorem in the cyclic quadrilateral $AXLY$ gives
\[
AX\cdot LY+AY\cdot LX=AL\cdot XY,
\]
so
\[
(AX+AY)u=AL\,v.
\tag{5}
\]
In triangle $XLY$,
\[
\angle LXY=\frac{\alpha}{2},
\qquad
\angle XLY=180^\circ-\alpha.
\]
The sine rule yields
\[
\frac{v}{u}
=\frac{\sin\alpha}{\sin(\alpha/2)}
=2\cos\frac{\alpha}{2}.
\]
Substituting this into (5) proves (4). \hfill$\square$

Apply the lemma to the angle $BAC$, whose measure is $\alpha$, and to the circle $\omega_A$. Using (2),
\[
2AA'\cos\frac{\alpha}{2}=AE+AF=BC.
\tag{6}
\]
Similarly,
\[
2BB'\cos\frac{\beta}{2}=CA,
\qquad
2CC'\cos\frac{\gamma}{2}=AB.
\tag{7}
\]
Thus $A',B',C'$ depend only on triangle $ABC$, not on the particular points $D,E,F$.

Apply the lemma again to angle $BAC$ and to the circle with diameter $AI$. Its intersections with $AB$ and $AC$ are the two touchpoints of the incircle. Their distances from $A$ both equal
\[
\frac{AB+AC-BC}{2}.
\]
Hence
\[
2AI\cos\frac{\alpha}{2}=AB+AC-BC.
\tag{8}
\]
Finally, let $M\ne A$ be the second intersection of $AI$ with the circumcircle of $ABC$. Applying the lemma to that circumcircle gives
\[
2AM\cos\frac{\alpha}{2}=AB+AC.
\tag{9}
\]
Equations (6), (8), and (9) imply
\[
AA'+AI=AM.
\tag{10}
\]
The four points $A,I,A',M$ are collinear, and (10) says that the segments $AM$ and $IA'$ have the same midpoint. Since $A$ and $M$ lie on the circumcircle centered at $O$, the point $O$ lies on the perpendicular bisector of $AM$, and therefore also on the perpendicular bisector of $IA'$. Thus
\[
OI=OA'.
\]
By symmetry, (3) follows. Hence the points
\[
I,A',B',C'
\]
lie on the circle $\Gamma$ centered at $O$ with radius $OI$.

It remains to prove that $P\in\Gamma$. We use directed angles modulo $180^\circ$. Assume first that $P\ne F$ and that the points used below are distinct. Since $A,F,P,A'$ lie on $\omega_A$,
\[
\measuredangle(A'P,FP)
=\measuredangle(A'A,FA)
=\measuredangle(A'I,AB).
\tag{11}
\]
Since $B,F,P,B'$ lie on $\omega_B$,
\[
\measuredangle(B'P,FP)
=\measuredangle(B'B,FB)
=\measuredangle(B'I,AB).
\tag{12}
\]
Subtracting (12) from (11),
\[
\begin{aligned}
\measuredangle(A'P,B'P)
&=\measuredangle(A'P,FP)+\measuredangle(FP,B'P)\\
&=\measuredangle(A'I,AB)-\measuredangle(B'I,AB)\\
&=\measuredangle(A'I,B'I).
\end{aligned}
\]
Therefore $A',B',P,I$ are concyclic, and hence $P\in\Gamma$.

If $P=F$, use the three circles $\omega_A,\omega_B,\omega_C$ as follows:
\[
\begin{aligned}
\measuredangle(A'F,B'F)
&=\measuredangle(A'F,EF)
 +\measuredangle(EF,DF)
 +\measuredangle(DF,B'F)\\
&=\measuredangle(A'I,AC)
 +\measuredangle(AC,BC)
 +\measuredangle(BC,B'I)\\
&=\measuredangle(A'I,B'I).
\end{aligned}
\]
Thus the same cyclicity conclusion holds.

Degenerate coincidences cause no difficulty. If $P$ already equals one of $I,A',B',C'$, the conclusion is immediate. If $ABC$ is equilateral, then (6)--(9) give
\[
A'=B'=C'=I=O,
\]
and the Miquel point $P$ is also $O$.

Assume now that $ABC$ is not equilateral. At most one of $A',B',C'$ can coincide with $I$. To see how the only possible degeneracy is handled, suppose for instance that $C'=I$. Let $N\ne C$ be the second intersection of $CI$ with the circumcircle of $ABC$. The analogue of (10) at $C$ gives
\[
CC'+CI=CN.
\]
Thus $2CI=CN$, so $I$ is the midpoint of $CN$ and consequently $OI\perp CI$. If $A'=I$ as well, the analogous argument would give $OI\perp AI$, which is impossible in a non-equilateral triangle; similarly $B'\ne I$. Hence $A',B'\ne I$, and since $A'$ lies on $AI$ while $B'$ lies on $BI$, we also have $A'\ne B'$. Finally, $A',B',I$ are noncollinear because they are three distinct points of the nondegenerate circle $\Gamma$. Therefore the preceding directed-angle argument applies to $A'$ and $B'$ and proves again that $P\in\Gamma$. The other possible coincidences are symmetric.

Consequently $P$ lies on the circle centered at $O$ with radius $OI$. Therefore
\[
OP=OI.
\]
