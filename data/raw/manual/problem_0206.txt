ID: 0206

SOURCE: IMO Shortlist

YEAR: 2016

TOPIC: Number Theory

SUBTOPICS: Integer Polynomials, Rational Perfect Powers, Dirichlet's Theorem, Pigeonhole Principle

STATEMENT:
Find all polynomials $P(x)$ of odd degree $d$ with integer coefficients having the following property: for every positive integer $n$, there exist $n$ pairwise distinct positive integers
\[
x_1,x_2,\ldots,x_n
\]
such that, for every pair of indices $i,j$,
\[
\frac12<\frac{P(x_i)}{P(x_j)}<2
\]
and
\[
\frac{P(x_i)}{P(x_j)}
\]
is the $d$-th power of a rational number.

The pairwise distinctness is the intended condition used by the official solution; it is written explicitly here because without it repetitions would make the problem degenerate.

SOLUTION 1:
The answer is
\[
\boxed{P(x)=a(rx+s)^d},
\]
where
\[
a,r,s\in\mathbb Z,
\qquad
a\ne0,
\qquad
r\ge1,
\qquad
\gcd(r,s)=1.
\]

We first prove necessity.

Write
\[
P(x)=a_dx^d+a_{d-1}x^{d-1}+\cdots+a_0,
\qquad a_d\ne0.
\]
Make the affine substitution
\[
y=da_dx+a_{d-1}
\]
and define
\[
Q(y)=P\left(\frac{y-a_{d-1}}{da_d}\right).
\]
Then $Q$ has rational coefficients and no $y^{d-1}$ term:
\[
Q(y)=b_dy^d+b_{d-2}y^{d-2}+\cdots+b_0,
\qquad b_d\ne0.
\tag{1}
\]

If $d=1$, equation (1) already says that $Q$ is a monomial, and the conclusion follows immediately. We therefore assume
\[
d\ge3.
\]

The given condition transfers from the $x_i$ to the corresponding distinct integers
\[
y_i=da_dx_i+a_{d-1}.
\]
Because arbitrarily many distinct positive $x_i$ are available, after discarding finitely many small values we may take all $|y_i|$ arbitrarily large. Replacing $y$ by $-y$ if necessary, assume below that all selected $y_i$ are large and positive.

Let $c$ be a positive common denominator of the coefficients of $Q$, so
\[
cQ(t)\in\mathbb Z
\qquad(t\in\mathbb Z).
\]
Choose, by Dirichlet's theorem, a sufficiently large prime
\[
p\equiv2\pmod d
\]
with
\[
p\nmid c.
\]
Since $d$ is odd,
\[
\gcd(p-1,d)=1.
\tag{2}
\]

Choose sufficiently many of the integers $y_i$. By the pigeonhole principle, some
\[
d+1
\]
of them are congruent modulo $p$. Relabel them as
\[
y_1,y_2,\ldots,y_{d+1}.
\]

We prove the following claim.

\textbf{Claim.}
For every $2\le i\le d+1$,
\[
\frac{Q(y_i)}{Q(y_1)}
=
\frac{y_i^d}{y_1^d}.
\tag{3}
\]

\textit{Proof.}
Write
\[
\frac{Q(y_i)}{Q(y_1)}
=
\frac{\ell^d}{m^d},
\qquad
\ell,m\in\mathbb Z_{>0},
\qquad
\gcd(\ell,m)=1.
\tag{4}
\]
Using (1), equation (4) becomes
\[
b_d(m^dy_i^d-\ell^dy_1^d)
=
-\sum_{j=0}^{d-2}
b_j(m^dy_i^j-\ell^dy_1^j).
\tag{5}
\]

For all sufficiently large selected $y_i,y_1$, the bounds on the ratios of $Q$ imply that there is a constant $C$, depending only on $Q$, such that
\[
C^{-1}\le\frac{y_i}{y_1}\le C,
\qquad
C^{-1}\le\frac{\ell}{m}\le C.
\tag{6}
\]
Moreover, because the reduced denominator $m^d$ divides
\[
|cQ(y_1)|,
\]
we have
\[
m\le Cy_1\le C^2y_i.
\tag{7}
\]

Put
\[
\Delta=my_i-\ell y_1.
\]
Factoring the left side of (5) and using (6), while bounding the lower-degree right side, gives
\[
|\Delta|
\le C\frac{m}{y_i},
\tag{8}
\]
after increasing $C$ if necessary. Indeed, the factor multiplying $\Delta$ on the left is at least
\[
|b_d|m^{d-1}y_i^{d-1},
\]
whereas the absolute value of the right side is at most
\[
Cm^dy_i^{d-2}.
\]

We distinguish two cases.

\textbf{Case 1:}
\[
p\mid cQ(y_1).
\]
Since
\[
y_i\equiv y_1\pmod p,
\]
we also have
\[
cQ(y_i)\equiv cQ(y_1)\equiv0\pmod p.
\]
At least one factor $p$ is therefore cancelled when the fraction
\[
\frac{cQ(y_i)}{cQ(y_1)}
\]
is reduced to $\ell^d/m^d$. Hence
\[
m^d\le\frac{|cQ(y_1)|}{p},
\]
and consequently
\[
m\le Cp^{-1/d}y_i.
\]
If $\Delta\ne0$, then $|\Delta|\ge1$, while (8) gives
\[
1\le Cp^{-1/d},
\]
impossible for sufficiently large $p$. Thus
\[
\Delta=0.
\]

\textbf{Case 2:}
\[
p\nmid cQ(y_1).
\]
Modulo $p$, we have
\[
cQ(y_i)\equiv cQ(y_1)\ne0.
\]
Using (4),
\[
\ell^d\equiv m^d\pmod p.
\]
By (2), the map $z\mapsto z^d$ is a bijection on the nonzero residues modulo $p$, so
\[
\ell\equiv m\pmod p.
\]
Together with
\[
y_i\equiv y_1\pmod p,
\]
this gives
\[
p\mid\Delta.
\]
If $\Delta\ne0$, then $|\Delta|\ge p$. But (7) and (8) give
\[
|\Delta|\le C,
\]
which is impossible for sufficiently large $p$. Hence again
\[
\Delta=0.
\]

Thus
\[
my_i=\ell y_1,
\]
and (4) gives exactly (3). This proves the claim. $\square$

Consider the polynomial
\[
F(y)=Q(y_1)y^d-y_1^dQ(y).
\]
By the claim, its $d+1$ distinct roots include
\[
y_1,y_2,\ldots,y_{d+1}.
\]
Since $\deg F\le d$, we must have
\[
F\equiv0.
\]
Therefore
\[
Q(y)=b_dy^d.
\]
Undoing the substitution,
\[
P(x)
=
a_d\left(
x+\frac{a_{d-1}}{da_d}
\right)^d.
\tag{9}
\]

Write the rational number in (9) in lowest terms:
\[
\frac{a_{d-1}}{da_d}=\frac sr,
\qquad
r\ge1,
\qquad
\gcd(r,s)=1.
\]
Then
\[
P(x)=\frac{a_d}{r^d}(rx+s)^d.
\]
Because $P$ has integer coefficients and $\gcd(r,s)=1$, we have
\[
r^d\mid a_d.
\]
Writing
\[
a_d=ar^d
\]
gives
\[
P(x)=a(rx+s)^d.
\]

It remains to prove sufficiency. Let
\[
P(x)=a(rx+s)^d
\]
with the stated conditions. For a given $n$, choose a sufficiently large positive integer $X$ and set
\[
x_i=X+i
\qquad(1\le i\le n).
\]
Choose $X$ so large that all $rx_i+s$ are positive and
\[
\left(
\frac{r(X+n)+s}{r(X+1)+s}
\right)^d<2.
\]
Then, for every $i,j$,
\[
\frac{P(x_i)}{P(x_j)}
=
\left(
\frac{rx_i+s}{rx_j+s}
\right)^d
\]
is the $d$-th power of a rational number, and its value lies strictly between $1/2$ and $2$. Thus every polynomial in the displayed family satisfies the condition.
