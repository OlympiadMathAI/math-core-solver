ID: 0203

SOURCE: IMO Shortlist

YEAR: 2016

TOPIC: Number Theory

SUBTOPICS: Pell-Type Equations, Vieta Jumping, Descent, Diophantine Transformations

STATEMENT:
Let $a$ be a positive integer that is not a perfect square. Let $A$ be the set of positive integers $k$ for which
\[
k=\frac{x^2-a}{x^2-y^2}
\tag{1}
\]
for some integers $x,y$ with
\[
x>\sqrt a.
\]
Let $B$ be the set of positive integers $k$ for which (1) holds for some integers $x,y$ with
\[
0\le x<\sqrt a.
\]
Prove that
\[
A=B.
\]

SOLUTION 1:
We first establish a transformation of solutions.

\textbf{Claim.}
Fix $k$, and suppose that integers $x,y$ satisfy (1). Define
\[
X=\frac12\left(
x-y+\frac{(x-y)^2-4a}{x+y}
\right),
\]
\[
Y=\frac12\left(
x-y-\frac{(x-y)^2-4a}{x+y}
\right).
\tag{2}
\]
Then $X,Y$ are integers and satisfy (1).

\textit{Proof.}
The denominator $x+y$ is nonzero, since otherwise $x^2-y^2=0$. We have
\[
X+Y=x-y
\]
and
\[
\begin{aligned}
X
&=\frac{x^2-xy-2a}{x+y}\\
&=-x+\frac{2(x^2-a)}{x+y}\\
&=-x+2k(x-y).
\end{aligned}
\]
Thus $X$ and $Y$ are integers.

Put
\[
u=x+y,
\qquad
v=x-y.
\]
Equation (1) is equivalent to
\[
u^2-(4k-2)uv+v^2-4a=0.
\tag{3}
\]
Regarding this as a quadratic equation in $u$, its other root is
\[
z=\frac{v^2-4a}{u}.
\]
By Vieta's theorem,
\[
v^2-(4k-2)vz+z^2-4a=0.
\]
Since
\[
v=X+Y,
\qquad
z=X-Y,
\]
this is exactly equation (1) for $X,Y$. $\square$

We first prove
\[
B\subseteq A.
\]
Take $k\in B$, represented by integers $x,y$ with
\[
0\le x<\sqrt a.
\]
Replacing $y$ by $|y|$, assume $y>0$. The case $k=1$ would give $y^2=a$, impossible because $a$ is not a square. Hence $k>1$.

Since the numerator and denominator in (1) are both negative,
\[
0\le x<y.
\]
The equivalent equation
\[
ky^2-(k-1)x^2=a
\tag{4}
\]
gives
\[
a-y^2=(k-1)(y^2-x^2)>0.
\]
Thus
\[
0\le x<y<\sqrt a.
\]

Let $X,Y$ be given by (2), and replace $X$ by $|X|$, which does not affect equation (1). Then
\[
\begin{aligned}
|X|
&\ge -X\\
&=\frac{2a+x(y-x)}{x+y}\\
&\ge\frac{2a}{x+y}\\
&>\sqrt a.
\end{aligned}
\]
Therefore the same $k$ has a representation with first coordinate greater than $\sqrt a$, and hence
\[
k\in A.
\]

We now prove
\[
A\subseteq B.
\]
Take $k\in A$. Choose a representation with $x>\sqrt a$, replace $y$ by $|y|$, and among all such representations choose one for which
\[
x+y
\]
is minimal. Again $k>1$. The numerator and denominator in (1) are positive, so $x>y\ge0$. From (4),
\[
y^2-a=(k-1)(x^2-y^2)>0,
\]
and hence
\[
x>y>\sqrt a.
\tag{5}
\]

Let $X,Y$ be as in (2), and put
\[
x_1=|X|,
\qquad
y_1=Y.
\]
The claim shows that $x_1,y_1$ still satisfy (1). Moreover,
\[
Y=\frac{y(x-y)+2a}{x+y}>0.
\]
Writing
\[
v=x-y,
\qquad
z=\frac{v^2-4a}{x+y},
\]
we obtain
\[
x_1+y_1
=
\begin{cases}
v,&X\ge0,\\[2mm]
-z=\dfrac{4a-v^2}{x+y},&X<0.
\end{cases}
\]
Thus
\[
x_1+y_1
\le
\max\left\{
x-y,\,
\frac{4a-(x-y)^2}{x+y}
\right\}.
\tag{6}
\]
The first quantity in (6) is smaller than $x+y$. The second one is also smaller, because (5) gives
\[
4a<(x+y)^2+(x-y)^2.
\]
Hence
\[
x_1+y_1<x+y.
\]

If $x_1>\sqrt a$, this contradicts the minimality of $x+y$. Therefore
\[
0\le x_1\le\sqrt a.
\]
Since $a$ is not a square and $x_1$ is an integer,
\[
0\le x_1<\sqrt a.
\]
Thus $k\in B$, proving $A\subseteq B$.

The two inclusions give
\[
A=B.
\]

SOLUTION 2:
Equation (1) is equivalent to
\[
ky^2-(k-1)x^2=a.
\tag{2}
\]
Again, $k=1$ is impossible because it would imply that $a$ is a square, so throughout we have $k>1$.

We use the following Pell-type transformation.

\textbf{Claim.}
If $(x_0,y_0)$ satisfies (2), then both pairs
\[
\left(
(2k-1)x_0\pm2ky_0,\,
(2k-1)y_0\pm2(k-1)x_0
\right)
\tag{3}
\]
also satisfy (2).

\textit{Proof.}
A direct expansion gives
\[
\begin{aligned}
&k\bigl((2k-1)y_0\pm2(k-1)x_0\bigr)^2\\
&\qquad -(k-1)\bigl((2k-1)x_0\pm2ky_0\bigr)^2\\
&=
\bigl(k(2k-1)^2-(k-1)(2k)^2\bigr)y_0^2\\
&\qquad+
\bigl(k(2k-2)^2-(k-1)(2k-1)^2\bigr)x_0^2\\
&=ky_0^2-(k-1)x_0^2\\
&=a.
\end{aligned}
\]
$\square$

Take first a solution with
\[
0\le x<\sqrt a
\]
and $y\ge0$. Equation (2) gives
\[
a-x^2=k(y^2-x^2)>0,
\]
so
\[
y>x.
\]
Since $x,y$ are integers,
\[
y\ge x+1.
\]
Using the plus signs in (3), define
\[
x_1=(2k-1)x+2ky.
\]
Then
\[
x_1
\ge
(2k-1)x+2k(x+1)
=(4k-1)x+2k>x.
\]
Repeatedly applying this transformation produces solutions with arbitrarily large first coordinate. In particular, eventually
\[
x>\sqrt a.
\]
Thus
\[
B\subseteq A.
\]

Conversely, take a solution with
\[
x>\sqrt a,
\qquad y\ge0.
\]
Then (1) gives
\[
x>y.
\tag{4}
\]
From (2),
\[
\sqrt{k}\,y>\sqrt{k-1}\,x.
\]
In particular,
\[
ky>(k-1)x.
\tag{5}
\]
Using the minus signs in (3), put
\[
t=(2k-1)x-2ky
\]
and
\[
y_1=(2k-1)y-2(k-1)x.
\]
The inequality
\[
\sqrt{\frac{k-1}{k}}
>
\frac{2(k-1)}{2k-1}
\]
shows that $y_1>0$.

By (5),
\[
t<x,
\]
while (4) gives
\[
t>-x.
\]
Hence
\[
|t|<x.
\]
The pair
\[
(|t|,y_1)
\]
still satisfies (2). Repeating the transformation decreases the nonnegative integer first coordinate until it is no longer greater than $\sqrt a$. Because $a$ is not a square, the final value satisfies
\[
0\le x<\sqrt a.
\]
Thus
\[
A\subseteq B.
\]

Therefore
\[
A=B.
\]

SOLUTION 3:
It is enough to prove that every $k\in A\cup B$ has both a representation with first coordinate greater than $\sqrt a$ and one with first coordinate between $0$ and $\sqrt a$.

Take $k\in A\cup B$, and choose integers $x,y$ satisfying (1). Replacing signs if necessary, assume
\[
x,y\ge0.
\]
As before, $k\ne1$, so
\[
k\ge2.
\]

Put
\[
u=x+y,
\qquad
v=x-y.
\]
Then
\[
k=\frac{(u+v)^2-4a}{4uv},
\tag{2}
\]
or equivalently,
\[
v^2+(2u-4ku)v+u^2-4a=0.
\tag{3}
\]

Let
\[
v_1=4ku-2u-v.
\]
By Vieta's theorem, $v_1$ is the other root of (3). Furthermore,
\[
u+v_1=4ku-u-v>u+v.
\]
The numbers $u$ and $v_1$ have the same parity, so
\[
x_1=\frac{u+v_1}{2},
\qquad
y_1=\frac{v_1-u}{2}
\]
are integers. Reversing the substitution shows that $(x_1,y_1)$ satisfies (1), and
\[
x_1>x.
\]
Repeating this construction gives a representation with
\[
x>\sqrt a.
\]

Now suppose that the current representation already satisfies
\[
x>\sqrt a.
\]
Then the numerator and denominator of (1) are positive, so
\[
x>y\ge0.
\]
Thus
\[
u>v>0.
\]
Regard (2) instead as a quadratic equation in $u$:
\[
u^2+(2v-4kv)u+v^2-4a=0.
\]
Let
\[
u_2=4kv-2v-u
\]
be its other root. By Vieta's theorem,
\[
uu_2=v^2-4a.
\tag{4}
\]

If $u_2\ge0$, then
\[
vu_2\le uu_2=v^2-4a<v^2,
\]
so
\[
0<u_2+v<u+v.
\]
If $u_2<0$, then
\[
(u_2+v)+(u+v)=4kv>0
\]
and
\[
u_2+v<u+v,
\]
which together imply
\[
|u_2+v|<u+v.
\]
The number $u_2+v$ is even, and therefore
\[
x_2=\frac{u_2+v}{2},
\qquad
y_2=\frac{u_2-v}{2}
\]
are integers satisfying (1). Replacing $x_2$ by $|x_2|$ if necessary,
\[
|x_2|<x.
\]

Repeated descent eventually produces a representation with
\[
0\le x<\sqrt a,
\]
because $a$ is not a square. Thus every $k\in A\cup B$ lies in both $A$ and $B$, and hence
\[
A=B.
\]
