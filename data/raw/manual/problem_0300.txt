ID: 0300

SOURCE: IMO Shortlist

YEAR: 2020

TOPIC: Algebra

SUBTOPICS: Polynomial Ideals, Symmetric Polynomials, Homogeneous Components, Monomials

STATEMENT:
Let $\mathcal A$ be the set of all polynomials in three variables $x,y,z$ with integer coefficients. Let $\mathcal B$ be the subset of $\mathcal A$ consisting of all polynomials expressible in the form
\[
(x+y+z)P(x,y,z)+(xy+yz+zx)Q(x,y,z)+xyzR(x,y,z),
\]
where $P,Q,R\in\mathcal A$.

Find the smallest nonnegative integer $n$ such that
\[
x^iy^jz^k\in\mathcal B
\]
for all nonnegative integers $i,j,k$ satisfying
\[
i+j+k\ge n.
\]

SOLUTION 1:
The answer is
\[
\boxed{4}.
\]

Put
\[
p=x+y+z,
\qquad
q=xy+yz+zx,
\qquad
r=xyz.
\]
We first show that every monomial of total degree at least $4$ belongs to $\mathcal B$.

Because $x,y,z$ are roots of
\[
t^3-pt^2+qt-r,
\]
we have
\[
x^3=px^2-qx+r\in\mathcal B.
\tag{1}
\]
Also,
\[
x^2y^2=xyq-(x+y)r\in\mathcal B.
\tag{2}
\]
Now consider a monomial $x^iy^jz^k$ of degree at least $4$. By symmetry, assume
\[
i\ge j\ge k.
\]
If $k\ge1$, the monomial is divisible by $r$. If $k=0$ and $j\ge2$, it is divisible by $x^2y^2$, which belongs to $\mathcal B$ by (2). Finally, if $k=0$ and $j\le1$, then $i\ge3$, so the monomial is divisible by $x^3$, which belongs to $\mathcal B$ by (1). Thus
\[
n\le4.
\]

It remains to show that $x^2y\notin\mathcal B$. Suppose otherwise. Taking only homogeneous components of the relevant degrees, we could write
\[
x^2y=pP_2+qQ_1+rR_0,
\tag{3}
\]
where $P_2,Q_1,R_0$ are homogeneous of degrees $2,1,0$, respectively.

The coefficient of $x^3$ on the right of (3) is the coefficient of $x^2$ in $P_2$, so that coefficient must vanish. The same applies to $y^2$ and $z^2$. Hence write
\[
P_2=Axy+Byz+Czx,
\]
\[
Q_1=Dx+Ey+Fz,
\qquad
R_0=G.
\]
Comparing the coefficients of
\[
xy^2,\quad y^2z,\quad yz^2,\quad xz^2,\quad x^2z
\]
in (3) gives
\[
A+E=0,
\quad
B+E=0,
\quad
B+F=0,
\quad
C+F=0,
\quad
C+D=0.
\]
Thus
\[
A=B=C
\qquad\text{and}\qquad
D=E=F=-A.
\]
But the coefficient of $x^2y$ on the right of (3) is then
\[
A+D=0,
\]
whereas on the left it is $1$. This contradiction proves that $x^2y\notin\mathcal B$.

Therefore the smallest possible value is
\[
\boxed{n=4}.
\]
