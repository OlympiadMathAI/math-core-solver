ID: 0299

SOURCE: IMO Shortlist

YEAR: 2020

TOPIC: Algebra

SUBTOPICS: Inequalities, Chebyshev Polynomials, Polynomial Coefficients, Calculus

STATEMENT:
\textbf{Version 1.} Let $n$ be a positive integer and set
\[
N=2^n.
\]
Determine the smallest real number $a_n$ such that, for every real number $x$,
\[
\sqrt[N]{\frac{x^{2N}+1}{2}}
\le a_n(x-1)^2+x.
\tag{1}
\]

\textbf{Version 2.} For every positive integer $N$, determine the smallest real number $b_N$ such that, for every real number $x$,
\[
\sqrt[N]{\frac{x^{2N}+1}{2}}
\le b_N(x-1)^2+x.
\tag{2}
\]

SOLUTION 1:
For both versions, the answer is
\[
\boxed{\frac N2}.
\]
This solution proves Version 1, where $N$ is a power of $2$.

We first prove the lower bound. Suppose that a constant $c<N/2$ satisfied the required inequality. Put
\[
x=1+t,
\qquad t>0.
\]
The right-hand side is positive for all sufficiently small $t$, so after raising the inequality to the $N$th power we would have
\[
\left(1+t+ct^2\right)^N
\ge
\frac{(1+t)^{2N}+1}{2}.
\]
The difference between the two sides has expansion
\[
\left(Nc-\frac{N^2}{2}\right)t^2+O(t^3).
\]
Its quadratic coefficient is negative, so the difference is negative for sufficiently small positive $t$, a contradiction. Hence
\[
c\ge\frac N2.
\tag{3}
\]

It remains to prove
\[
\sqrt[N]{\frac{x^{2N}+1}{2}}
\le x+\frac N2(x-1)^2
\qquad(x\in\mathbb R)
\tag{$I_N$}
\]
when $N$ is a power of $2$. We prove this by induction under the doubling $N\mapsto2N$.

For $N=1$,
\[
\frac{x^2+1}{2}=x+\frac12(x-1)^2,
\]
so equality holds.

Assume that $(I_N)$ holds for every real argument. We prove $(I_{2N})$. A direct calculation gives
\[
\begin{aligned}
\left(x+N(x-1)^2\right)^2
&=x^2+N^2(x-1)^4+2Nx(x-1)^2\\
&=x^2+\frac N2(x^2-1)^2
 +\left(N^2-\frac N2\right)(x-1)^4\\
&\ge x^2+\frac N2(x^2-1)^2.
\end{aligned}
\]
By $(I_N)$ applied to $x^2$,
\[
x^2+\frac N2(x^2-1)^2
\ge
\sqrt[N]{\frac{x^{4N}+1}{2}}.
\]
Moreover,
\[
x+N(x-1)^2
\ge x+\frac12(x-1)^2
=\frac{x^2+1}{2}\ge0.
\]
Taking square roots therefore yields
\[
x+N(x-1)^2
\ge
\sqrt[2N]{\frac{x^{4N}+1}{2}},
\]
which is $(I_{2N})$.

Thus equality is possible with $c=N/2$, and (3) proves that this is the smallest value.

SOLUTION 2:
We prove Version 2 for an arbitrary positive integer $N$ by comparing polynomial coefficients.

The lower bound $b_N\ge N/2$ follows from the expansion at $x=1$ given in Solution 1. We prove that $b_N=N/2$ works.

It is enough first to consider $x>0$. Put
\[
t=\frac{(x-1)^2}{x}=x+\frac1x-2\ge0
\]
and
\[
F_N(t)=\frac{x^N+x^{-N}}2.
\]
Then the desired inequality with $b_N=N/2$, after division by $x$, is equivalent to
\[
F_N(t)\le\left(1+\frac N2t\right)^N.
\tag{4}
\]

We use the identity
\[
F_N(t)
=N\sum_{r=0}^{N}
\frac1{N+r}\binom{N+r}{2r}t^r.
\tag{5}
\]
It follows by induction from
\[
F_{N+1}+F_{N-1}=(2+t)F_N,
\]
with $F_0=1$ and $F_1=1+t/2$.

For $r\ge1$, compare the coefficient of $t^r$ in (5) with the corresponding coefficient in the right-hand side of (4). Their ratio is
\[
\begin{aligned}
&\frac{\displaystyle \frac{N}{N+r}\binom{N+r}{2r}}
{\displaystyle \left(\frac N2\right)^r\binom Nr}\\
&\qquad=
\frac{2^r r!}{(2r)!}
\prod_{j=1}^{r-1}\left(1+\frac jN\right)\\
&\qquad\le
\frac{2^r(r!)^2}{(2r)!}
=
\prod_{j=1}^{r}\frac{2j}{r+j}
\le1.
\end{aligned}
\]
The constant coefficients are both $1$. Hence every coefficient of the right-hand side of (4) is at least the corresponding coefficient of $F_N$, and (4) follows for $t\ge0$.

For $x=0$, the required inequality is immediate. For $x<0$, write $u=-x>0$. The left-hand side is the same for $x=-u$ and $x=u$, while
\[
\left[-u+\frac N2(u+1)^2\right]
-
\left[u+\frac N2(u-1)^2\right]
=2(N-1)u\ge0.
\]
Thus the result for $u>0$ implies the result for $-u$ as well.

Therefore $b_N=N/2$ for every positive integer $N$.

SOLUTION 3:
We give a second proof of Version 2 using the roots of a Chebyshev-type polynomial.

As before, the expansion at $x=1$ gives the lower bound $b_N\ge N/2$. For $x>0$, put
\[
t=x+\frac1x-2\ge0,
\qquad
F_N(t)=\frac{x^N+x^{-N}}2.
\]
It is enough to prove
\[
F_N(t)\le\left(1+\frac N2t\right)^N.
\tag{6}
\]

The recurrence
\[
F_{N+1}=(2+t)F_N-F_{N-1}
\]
shows that $F_N$ is a polynomial of degree $N$ in $t$. For
\[
\theta_j=\frac{(2j-1)\pi}{2N},
\qquad
s_j=4\sin^2\frac{\theta_j}{2}
\qquad(1\le j\le N),
\]
take $x=e^{i\theta_j}$. Then
\[
t=x+x^{-1}-2=-s_j
\]
and
\[
F_N(-s_j)=\cos(N\theta_j)=0.
\]
Since $F_N(0)=1$, we obtain
\[
F_N(t)=\prod_{j=1}^{N}\left(1+\frac{t}{s_j}\right).
\tag{7}
\]
Furthermore,
\[
F_N'(0)=\frac{N^2}{2},
\]
which follows, for example, by writing $x=e^u$ and using
\[
t=2\cosh u-2=u^2+O(u^4),
\qquad
F_N(t)=\cosh(Nu)=1+\frac{N^2u^2}{2}+O(u^4).
\]
Comparing with (7),
\[
\sum_{j=1}^{N}\frac1{s_j}=\frac{N^2}{2}.
\]
The arithmetic-geometric mean inequality now gives
\[
\begin{aligned}
F_N(t)
&=\prod_{j=1}^{N}\left(1+\frac{t}{s_j}\right)\\
&\le
\left(1+\frac tN\sum_{j=1}^{N}\frac1{s_j}\right)^N\\
&=
\left(1+\frac N2t\right)^N.
\end{aligned}
\]
This proves (6). The cases $x=0$ and $x<0$ are handled exactly as in Solution 2. Hence the smallest constant is $N/2$.

SOLUTION 4:
We give a calculus proof of Version 2. For a real constant $c$, define on $x>0$
\[
h_c(x)=
\left(\frac{x^{2N}+1}{2}\right)^{1/N}
-c(x-1)^2-x.
\]
The desired inequality is $h_c(x)\le0$.

At $x=1$,
\[
h_c(1)=h_c'(1)=0,
\qquad
h_c''(1)=N-2c.
\]
If $c<N/2$, then $x=1$ is a strict local minimum of $h_c$, so $h_c(x)>0$ for some $x$ near $1$. Thus $c\ge N/2$.

Now put $c=N/2$. If $N=1$, then $h_c\equiv0$. Assume $N>1$. Direct differentiation gives
\[
h_c'''(x)=
\frac12(1-N)(1-2N)
\left(\frac{x^{2N}+1}{2}\right)^{1/N-3}
 x^{2N-3}(1-x^{2N}).
\tag{8}
\]
The coefficient in front of $1-x^{2N}$ is positive. Therefore
\[
h_c'''(x)>0\quad(0<x<1),
\qquad
h_c'''(x)<0\quad(x>1).
\]
Since $h_c''(1)=0$, it follows that
\[
h_c''(x)<0
\qquad(x>0,\,x\ne1).
\]
Thus $h_c'$ is strictly decreasing, and because $h_c'(1)=0$,
\[
h_c'(x)>0\quad(0<x<1),
\qquad
h_c'(x)<0\quad(x>1).
\]
Hence $h_c$ attains its maximum at $x=1$, where it equals $0$. This proves the inequality for $x>0$. The cases $x=0$ and $x<0$ follow as in Solution 2.

Therefore the smallest constant is again
\[
\boxed{\frac N2}.
\]
