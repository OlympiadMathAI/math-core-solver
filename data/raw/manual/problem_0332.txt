ID: 0332

SOURCE: IMO Shortlist

YEAR: 2021

TOPIC: Algebra

SUBTOPICS: Floor Sums, Greatest Common Divisors, Modular Arithmetic, Primality Characterization

STATEMENT:
For an integer $n\ge1$, consider the $n\times n$ table whose entry in row $i$ and column $j$ is
\[
\left\lfloor\frac{ij}{n+1}\right\rfloor
\qquad(1\le i,j\le n).
\]
Determine all $n$ for which the sum of the $n^2$ entries is
\[
\frac14n^2(n-1).
\]

SOLUTION 1:
The answer is
\[
\boxed{n+1\text{ is prime}.}
\]

Let
\[
S=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\sum_{j=1}^{n}
\left\lfloor\frac{ij}{n+1}\right\rfloor.
\]
If two real numbers $x,y$ have integral sum, then
\[
\lfloor x\rfloor+\lfloor y\rfloor\ge x+y-1,
\tag{1}
\]
with equality unless both $x$ and $y$ are integers.

Replacing $i$ by $n+1-i$ in one copy of the sum gives
\[
\begin{aligned}
2S
&=
\sum_{i=1}^{n}\sum_{j=1}^{n}
\left(
\left\lfloor\frac{ij}{n+1}\right\rfloor+
\left\lfloor\frac{(n+1-i)j}{n+1}\right\rfloor
\right)\\
&\ge
\sum_{i=1}^{n}\sum_{j=1}^{n}(j-1)\\
&=
\frac12n^2(n-1).
\end{aligned}
\tag{2}
\]
Thus
\[
S\ge\frac14n^2(n-1).
\tag{3}
\]

Equality in (3) holds exactly when none of the numbers
\[
\frac{ij}{n+1},
\qquad 1\le i,j\le n,
\]
is an integer.

If $n+1$ is composite, write
\[
n+1=ab
\]
with
\[
2\le a,b\le n.
\]
Then $ij/(n+1)$ is integral for $i=a,j=b$, so the inequality in (2) is strict.

Conversely, if $n+1$ is prime, it cannot divide any product $ij$ with
\[
1\le i,j\le n.
\]
Hence equality holds in every application of (1), and
\[
S=\frac14n^2(n-1).
\]

Therefore the required integers are precisely those for which $n+1$ is prime.

SOLUTION 2:
Again let $S_i$ be the sum of row $i$, and add a phantom column $j=0$, whose entry is $0$.

Fix $i\in\{1,\ldots,n\}$, and put
\[
d=\gcd(i,n+1),
\qquad
q=\frac{n+1}{d}.
\]
For $j=0,1,\ldots,n$, let $r_j$ be the least nonnegative residue of $ij$ modulo $n+1$.

For each
\[
g=1,2,\ldots,d,
\]
the $q$ residues corresponding to
\[
(g-1)q\le j\le gq-1
\]
are a permutation of
\[
0,d,2d,\ldots,(q-1)d.
\tag{1}
\]
Indeed, all of them are multiples of $d$. If two were equal, then
\[
n+1\mid i(j_1-j_2),
\]
so
\[
q\mid j_1-j_2.
\]
Within one block of $q$ consecutive indices this forces $j_1=j_2$, proving (1).

It follows that
\[
\begin{aligned}
\sum_{j=0}^{n}r_j
&=
d\sum_{\ell=0}^{q-1}\ell d\\
&=
\frac{(n+1-d)(n+1)}2.
\end{aligned}
\tag{2}
\]
Therefore
\[
\begin{aligned}
S_i
&=
\sum_{j=0}^{n}\left\lfloor\frac{ij}{n+1}\right\rfloor\\
&=
\sum_{j=0}^{n}\frac{ij-r_j}{n+1}\\
&=
\frac{in-n-1+d}{2}.
\end{aligned}
\tag{3}
\]
Since $d\ge1$,
\[
S_i\ge\frac{n(i-1)}2,
\tag{4}
\]
and equality holds exactly when
\[
\gcd(i,n+1)=1.
\]

Summing (4) over all rows gives
\[
S\ge
\sum_{i=1}^{n}\frac{n(i-1)}2
=
\frac14n^2(n-1).
\]
Equality holds exactly when every integer
\[
1\le i\le n
\]
is coprime to $n+1$, which is equivalent to $n+1$ being prime.
