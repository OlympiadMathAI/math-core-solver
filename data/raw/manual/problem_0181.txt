ID: 0181

SOURCE: IMO Shortlist

YEAR: 2016

TOPIC: Algebra

SUBTOPICS: Functional Equations, Cauchy Equation, Growth Estimates, Symmetry

STATEMENT:
Find all functions $f:\mathbb R\to\mathbb R$ such that $f(0)\ne0$ and
\[
f(x+y)^2
=
2f(x)f(y)
+
\max\bigl\{f(x^2)+f(y^2),\ f(x^2+y^2)\bigr\}
\tag{1}
\]
for all real numbers $x,y$.

SOLUTION 1:
The solutions are
\[
\boxed{f(x)=-1\quad\text{for all }x}
\]
and
\[
\boxed{f(x)=x-1\quad\text{for all }x}.
\]

Putting $x=y=0$ in (1), and using $f(0)\ne0$, gives
\[
f(0)=-1.
\]
Putting $y=0$ then yields
\[
(f(x)+1)^2=f(x^2)+1.
\]
Define
\[
g(x)=f(x)+1.
\]
Then
\[
g(x^2)=g(x)^2\ge0.
\tag{2}
\]
Since the maximum in (1) is at least its first entry, (1) and (2) imply
\[
(g(x+y)-1)^2
\ge
(g(x)+g(y)-1)^2-1.
\tag{3}
\]
From (2), $g(1)$ is either $0$ or $1$.

\textbf{Case 1: $g(1)=0$.}
Then $f(1)=-1$. Substituting $(-1,0)$ and then $(-1,1)$ into (1) gives
\[
f(-1)=-1,
\qquad
f(2)=-1,
\]
so
\[
g(-1)=g(2)=0.
\]
By (2),
\[
g\left(2^{2^m}\right)=0
\qquad(m\ge0).
\]
Put $y=2^{2^m}-x$ in (3). For any fixed $x\ge0$ and all sufficiently large $m$, both $x$ and $2^{2^m}-x$ are nonnegative, so (2) gives
\[
0\le g(x)\le1+\sqrt2.
\]
Applying (2) repeatedly,
\[
g(x)^{2^m}=g(x^{2^m})\le1+\sqrt2,
\]
and therefore
\[
0\le g(x)\le1
\qquad(x\ge0).
\tag{4}
\]
Also, (2) gives
\[
|g(-x)|=g(x)
\qquad(x\ge0).
\tag{5}
\]

Suppose $g(a)\ne0$ for some real $a$. Then $g(a^2)=g(a)^2>0$. For sufficiently large $m$, with
\[
t=(a^2)^{1/2^m}>0,
\]
we have
\[
u=g(t)=g(a^2)^{1/2^m}>\frac1{\sqrt2}.
\]
By (4), $u\le1$, and by (5), $g(-t)=u$ or $g(-t)=-u$. In the first case,
\[
2f(t)f(-t)=2(u-1)^2<1;
\]
in the second,
\[
2f(t)f(-t)=2(1-u^2)<1.
\]
Moreover, by (4),
\[
\max\{2f(t^2),f(2t^2)\}\le0.
\]
Putting $x=t$, $y=-t$ in (1) would therefore make its right-hand side strictly smaller than its left-hand side $f(0)^2=1$, a contradiction. Hence $g\equiv0$, and
\[
f\equiv-1.
\]

\textbf{Case 2: $g(1)=1$.}
Then $f(1)=0$. Substituting $(-1,1)$ into (1) gives $f(2)=1$, so
\[
g(2)=2.
\]
Using (3) with $x=2m$, $y=2$, we prove inductively that
\[
g(2m)\ge m+1
\qquad(m\ge1).
\tag{6}
\]
The base case is $g(2)=2$. If $g(2m)\ge m+1$, then
\[
(g(2m+2)-1)^2
\ge(g(2m)+1)^2-1>(m+1)^2.
\]
Since $g(2m+2)\ge0$, the negative square-root alternative would make $g(2m+2)<0$; hence $g(2m+2)>m+2$.

Fix $a>1$. We first prove $g(a)\ge a$. Choose a large integer $m$ and an integer $k$ such that
\[
2k\le a^{2^m}<2k+2.
\]
Since $g$ is nonnegative on nonnegative arguments, (2), (3), and (6) give
\[
\begin{aligned}
(g(a)^{2^m}-1)^2+1
&=(g(a^{2^m})-1)^2+1\\
&\ge
\bigl(g(2k)+g(a^{2^m}-2k)-1\bigr)^2\\
&\ge k^2\\
&>\frac14(a^{2^m}-2)^2.
\end{aligned}
\]
For large $m$ this implies $g(a)^{2^m}>1$, and consequently
\[
g(a)^{2^m}>\frac12(a^{2^m}-2).
\]
Taking $2^m$-th roots and letting $m\to\infty$ gives
\[
g(a)\ge a.
\tag{7}
\]

Next we prove $g(a)\le a+1$. From (3) and (7), an induction on $r$ gives
\[
g(ra)\ge(r-1)(g(a)-1)+a
\qquad(r\ge1).
\tag{8}
\]
Indeed, the case $r=1$ is (7). If (8) holds for $r$, then $g(ra),g(a)>1$, and (3) gives
\[
g((r+1)a)-1
\ge\sqrt{(g(ra)+g(a)-1)^2-1}
\ge g(ra)+g(a)-2,
\]
which is the next case of (8).
Choose a large $m$ and $k$ such that
\[
ka\le2^{2^m}<(k+1)a.
\]
Since $g(2^{2^m})=2^{2^m}$, inequalities (3) and (8) yield
\[
2^{2^m}>(k-1)(g(a)-1)+a-1.
\]
Also $k>2^{2^m}/a-1$. Hence
\[
2^{2^m}>
\left(\frac{2^{2^m}}a-2\right)(g(a)-1)+a-1.
\]
Divide by $2^{2^m}$ and let $m\to\infty$. We get
\[
\frac{g(a)-1}{a}\le1,
\]
so
\[
g(a)\le a+1.
\tag{9}
\]

Finally put $z=a^{2^m}$. From (9),
\[
|g(2z)-1|\le2z.
\]
Using (3) with $x=y=z$ gives
\[
(2g(z)-1)^2\le4z^2+1.
\]
Thus
\[
g(a)^{2^m}=g(z)
\le\frac{1+\sqrt{4z^2+1}}2<2z
\]
after $m$ is large. Taking $2^m$-th roots and letting $m\to\infty$ gives $g(a)\le a$. Together with (7),
\[
g(a)=a
\qquad(a>1).
\]
Therefore
\[
f(a)=a-1
\qquad(a>1).
\]

For an arbitrary real $x$, choose $y$ so large that both $y$ and $x+y$ exceed $1$. Substituting the known formulas for these arguments into (1) gives
\[
2(x-1-f(x))y
=
-x^2+2x-2-2f(x)+\max\{f(x^2),x^2\}.
\]
The right side is independent of $y$. Since the identity holds for all sufficiently large $y$, its coefficient of $y$ must vanish. Hence
\[
f(x)=x-1
\]
for every real $x$.

Both obtained functions are readily checked in (1).

SOLUTION 2:
As in Solution 1,
\[
f(0)=-1
\tag{1}
\]
and
\[
f(x)^2=-2f(x)+f(x^2).
\tag{2}
\]
Replacing $x$ by $-x$ in (2) and comparing gives
\[
\bigl(f(x)-f(-x)\bigr)\bigl(f(x)+f(-x)+2\bigr)=0.
\]
Thus, for every $x$,
\[
f(x)=f(-x)
\quad\text{or}\quad
f(x)+f(-x)=-2.
\tag{3}
\]

Compare the original functional equation for the pairs $(x,x)$ and $(x,-x)$. The maximum terms are identical, and we obtain
\[
f(2x)^2-2f(x)^2=1-2f(x)f(-x).
\tag{4}
\]
Consequently,
\[
\begin{cases}
f(2x)=\pm1,&\text{if }f(x)=f(-x),\\
f(2x)=\pm(2f(x)+1),&\text{if }f(x)+f(-x)=-2.
\end{cases}
\tag{5}
\]

We claim that the second alternative in (3) always holds. Suppose instead that for some $a$,
\[
f(a)=f(-a)\ne-1.
\]
We may take $a>0$. First, $f(a)\ne1$: otherwise (4) gives $f(2a)^2=1$, while (2) gives $f(a^2)=3$, and substituting $(a,a)$ into the original equation makes its right side at least $8$.
Thus $f(a)\notin\{-1,1\}$.

Apply (3) and (5) to $a/2$. If $f(a/2)=f(-a/2)$, then (5) would give $f(a)^2=1$, impossible. Hence
\[
f(a/2)+f(-a/2)=-2.
\]
Write $u=f(a/2)$. Then $f(-a/2)=-2-u$, and (5) gives signs $\varepsilon,\delta\in\{-1,1\}$ such that
\[
f(a)=\varepsilon(2u+1),
\qquad
f(-a)=\delta(-2u-3).
\]
Since $f(a)=f(-a)$, either the two signs are equal, which forces $u=-1$ and then $f(a)=\pm1$, or the signs are opposite, which gives the impossible equation $2u+1=2u+3$. This proves the claim:
\[
f(x)+f(-x)=-2
\qquad(x\in\mathbb R).
\tag{6}
\]

Replace $(x,y)$ by $(-x,-y)$ in the original equation and compare the result with the original one. Using (6), a direct simplification gives
\[
f(x+y)=f(x)+f(y)+1.
\tag{7}
\]
Define
\[
g(x)=f(x)+1.
\]
Then (7) says that $g$ is additive. Equation (2) becomes
\[
g(x^2)=g(x)^2.
\tag{8}
\]
In particular, $g(t)\ge0$ for every $t\ge0$. An additive function nonnegative on the positive reals is increasing, hence linear:
\[
g(x)=cx.
\]
Substituting this into (8) gives $c^2=c$, so $c=0$ or $c=1$. Therefore
\[
f(x)=-1
\quad\text{or}\quad
f(x)=x-1.
\]
Both functions satisfy the equation.

SOLUTION 3:
We again have
\[
f(0)=-1,
\qquad
(f(x)+1)^2=f(x^2)+1,
\tag{1}
\]
and, for every $x$,
\[
f(x)=f(-x)
\quad\text{or}\quad
f(x)+f(-x)=-2.
\tag{2}
\]
We show first that the same alternative in (2) must hold for every real $x$.

Suppose, to the contrary, that there are nonzero $a,b$ such that
\[
f(a)=f(-a)\ne-1,
\]
while
\[
f(b)\ne f(-b),
\qquad
f(b)+f(-b)=-2.
\]
Replace $a$ by $-a$ if necessary so that $a>0$.

Comparing the original equation for $(x,a)$ and $(x,-a)$ gives
\[
f(x+a)^2=f(x-a)^2.
\]
Hence
\[
f(x+2a)=\pm f(x)
\qquad(x\in\mathbb R).
\tag{3}
\]
Taking $x=b$ and $x=-2a-b$ in (3) shows that
\[
|f(2a+b)|=|f(b)|,
\qquad
|f(-2a-b)|=|f(-b)|.
\]
The two absolute values on the right are different: otherwise $f(b)=\pm f(-b)$, and together with $f(b)+f(-b)=-2$ this would force either equality or the impossible sum $0$. Hence $f(2a+b)\ne f(-2a-b)$, so the second alternative of (2) applies to this pair. The only choices of signs compatible with a sum of $-2$ are
\[
f(2a+b)=f(b),
\qquad
f(-2a-b)=f(-b).
\]
Repeating the argument with $a$ and $-a$ gives, for every integer $k$,
\[
f(2ka+b)=f(b),
\qquad
f(2ka-b)=f(-b).
\tag{4}
\]
Since the two numbers $f(b),f(-b)$ are unequal and have sum $-2$, one of them is less than $-1$. Without loss of generality, suppose $f(b)<-1$. Choose $k$ so large that
\[
2ka+b>0
\]
and set $x=\sqrt{2ka+b}$. By (1) and (4),
\[
(f(x)+1)^2=f(x^2)+1=f(b)+1<0,
\]
a contradiction.

Thus one alternative in (2) holds globally.

If
\[
f(x)=f(-x)
\qquad\text{for all }x,
\]
compare the equation for $(a/2,a/2)$ and $(a/2,-a/2)$. This gives
\[
f(a)^2=f(0)^2=1
\]
for every $a$. If $f(a)=1$ for some $a$, we may take $a>0$ and substitute $x=y=\sqrt a$; the right side is then at least $4$, while the left side equals $1$. Hence
\[
f(x)=-1
\]
for all $x$.

If instead
\[
f(x)+f(-x)=-2
\qquad\text{for all }x,
\]
the additive argument from Solution 2 applies and yields
\[
f(x)=x-1.
\]
Thus the two stated functions are the only solutions.
