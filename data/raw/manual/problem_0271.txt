ID: 0271

SOURCE: IMO Shortlist

YEAR: 2019

TOPIC: Algebra

SUBTOPICS: Lagrange Interpolation, Symmetric Polynomials, Roots of Unity, Rational Identities

STATEMENT:
Let $x_1,x_2,\ldots,x_n$ be distinct real numbers. Prove that
\[
\sum_{i=1}^{n}
\prod_{\substack{1\le j\le n\\j\ne i}}
\frac{1-x_i x_j}{x_i-x_j}
=
\begin{cases}
0,&n\text{ is even},\\
1,&n\text{ is odd}.
\end{cases}
\]

SOLUTION 1:
Denote the expression on the left by $G$. Since both sides are rational functions of the variables, it is enough to prove the identity when none of the $x_i$ equals $1$ or $-1$.

Define
\[
f(t)=\prod_{i=1}^{n}(1-x_i t).
\]
Using the $n+2$ interpolation nodes
\[
1,-1,x_1,x_2,\ldots,x_n,
\]
the Lagrange interpolation formula gives
\[
\begin{aligned}
f(t)
={}&\sum_{i=1}^{n}
f(x_i)
\frac{(t-1)(t+1)\prod_{j\ne i}(t-x_j)}
{(x_i-1)(x_i+1)\prod_{j\ne i}(x_i-x_j)}\\
&+f(1)\frac{(t+1)\prod_{j=1}^{n}(t-x_j)}
{2\prod_{j=1}^{n}(1-x_j)}\\
&+f(-1)\frac{(t-1)\prod_{j=1}^{n}(t-x_j)}
{-2\prod_{j=1}^{n}(-1-x_j)}.
\end{aligned}
\tag{1}
\]
Although the right side has apparent degree $n+1$, it equals the degree-$n$ polynomial $f$.

Compare the coefficients of $t^{n+1}$ in (1). Since
\[
f(x_i)=(1-x_i^2)\prod_{j\ne i}(1-x_i x_j),
\]
the contribution of the $i$th term is
\[
-\prod_{j\ne i}\frac{1-x_i x_j}{x_i-x_j}.
\]
The node $1$ contributes $1/2$, while the node $-1$ contributes $(-1)^{n+1}/2$. Therefore
\[
0=-G+\frac12+\frac{(-1)^{n+1}}2.
\]
Hence
\[
G=\frac{1+(-1)^{n+1}}2,
\]
which is $0$ for even $n$ and $1$ for odd $n$.

SOLUTION 2:
Let
\[
G(x_1,\ldots,x_n)=
\sum_{i=1}^{n}
\prod_{j\ne i}\frac{1-x_i x_j}{x_i-x_j}
\]
and let
\[
V=\prod_{1\le i<j\le n}(x_j-x_i).
\]
Then $F=GV$ is a polynomial. It is alternating, because $G$ is symmetric and $V$ is alternating. Hence $F$ vanishes whenever two variables are equal, so every factor $x_j-x_i$ divides $F$. Their product $V$ therefore divides $F$, and consequently $G$ is itself a symmetric polynomial.

For nonzero variables, set $y_i=1/x_i$. Then
\[
\frac{1-y_i y_j}{y_i-y_j}
=
\frac{1-x_i x_j}{x_i-x_j},
\]
so
\[
G\left(\frac1{x_1},\ldots,\frac1{x_n}\right)
=G(x_1,\ldots,x_n).
\tag{1}
\]
This forces the polynomial $G$ to be constant. Indeed, if its highest nonzero homogeneous component had positive degree, choose pairwise distinct nonzero numbers $c_i$ at which that component does not vanish. Then $G(tc_1,\ldots,tc_n)$ would be unbounded as $t\to\infty$, whereas by (1) it equals
\[
G\left(\frac1{tc_1},\ldots,\frac1{tc_n}\right),
\]
which tends to $G(0,\ldots,0)$.

It remains to identify the constant. The polynomial identity extends to complex values. Let $\zeta$ be a primitive $n$th root of unity and take
\[
x_i=\zeta^{i-1}
\qquad(1\le i\le n).
\]
For a fixed $i$, the numerator of the corresponding term vanishes unless $x_i=x_i^{-1}$.

The term with $x_i=1$ equals $1$. If $n$ is odd, it is the only nonzero term, so $G=1$. If $n$ is even, there is one additional term with $x_i=-1$. Each of its $n-1$ factors equals $-1$, so that term is
\[
(-1)^{n-1}=-1.
\]
Thus $G=0$ when $n$ is even.

SOLUTION 3:
Regard the expression
\[
G=G(x_1,\ldots,x_n)
\]
as a rational function of $x_n$, with the other variables fixed. Put
\[
D(t)=\prod_{j=1}^{n-1}(t-x_j).
\]
Then
\[
G(x_1,\ldots,x_{n-1},t)=\frac{P(t)}{D(t)},
\]
where
\[
\begin{aligned}
P(t)
={}&\prod_{j=1}^{n-1}(1-tx_j)\\
&+\sum_{i=1}^{n-1}(x_i t-1)
\prod_{\substack{1\le j\le n-1\\j\ne i}}(t-x_j)
\prod_{\substack{1\le j\le n-1\\j\ne i}}
\frac{1-x_i x_j}{x_i-x_j}.
\end{aligned}
\tag{1}
\]
The polynomial $P$ has degree at most $n-1$.

For any $k<n$, substitute $t=x_k$ into (1). In the sum, every term except the one with $i=k$ vanishes. Hence
\[
\begin{aligned}
P(x_k)
={}&(1-x_k^2)
\prod_{\substack{j<n\\j\ne k}}(1-x_kx_j)\\
&+(x_k^2-1)
\prod_{\substack{j<n\\j\ne k}}(1-x_kx_j)=0.
\end{aligned}
\]
Thus $P$ has the same $n-1$ distinct roots as $D$, and both have degree at most $n-1$. Therefore
\[
P(t)=cD(t)
\]
for some constant $c$ independent of $t$. Hence $G$ is independent of $x_n$. Since $G$ is symmetric in all its variables, it is independent of every variable and is therefore a constant depending only on $n$.

Evaluating this constant at the $n$th roots of unity exactly as in Solution 2 gives $1$ for odd $n$ and $0$ for even $n$.
