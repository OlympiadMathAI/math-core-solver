ID: 0098

SOURCE: IMO Shortlist

YEAR: 2013

TOPIC: Combinatorics

SUBTOPICS: Integer Partitions, Pigeonhole Principle, Extremal Combinatorics, Minimal Counterexample

STATEMENT:
Let $n$ be a positive integer, and let $A\subseteq\{1,2,\ldots,n\}$. An $A$-partition of $n$ into $k$ parts is a representation
\[
n=a_1+a_2+\cdots+a_k,
\]
where every $a_i$ belongs to $A$ and repetitions are allowed. The number of different parts in the partition is
\[
\bigl|\{a_1,a_2,\ldots,a_k\}\bigr|.
\]
An $A$-partition is called optimal if no $A$-partition of $n$ uses fewer parts.

Prove that every optimal $A$-partition of $n$ contains at most
\[
\sqrt[3]{6n}
\]
different parts.

SOLUTION 1:
Assume an $A$-partition exists, and let
\[
n=a_1+\cdots+a_{k_{\min}}
\]
be optimal. Let its distinct parts be
\[
S=\{b_1,b_2,\ldots,b_s\},
\qquad
b_1<b_2<\cdots<b_s.
\]
Suppose for a contradiction that
\[
s>\sqrt[3]{6n}.
\tag{1}
\]
We will find subsets $X,Y\subseteq S$ such that
\[
|X|<|Y|
\qquad\text{and}\qquad
\sum_{x\in X}x=\sum_{y\in Y}y.
\tag{2}
\]
Then one occurrence of every element of $Y$ can be removed from the original partition and one occurrence of every element of $X$ inserted. This preserves the total sum but decreases the number of parts, contradicting optimality.

Fix $k\in\{1,2,\ldots,s\}$. Starting with the $k$-subset
\[
\{b_1,b_2,\ldots,b_k\},
\]
repeatedly move the rightmost selected element that can be moved one position to the right, until the subset
\[
\{b_{s-k+1},\ldots,b_s\}
\]
is reached. Every move replaces some $b_j$ by $b_{j+1}$, so the subset sum strictly increases.

The potential given by the sum of the selected indices starts at
\[
1+2+\cdots+k
\]
and ends at
\[
(s-k+1)+\cdots+s.
\]
Each move increases this potential by $1$, so the list contains exactly
\[
k(s-k)+1
\]
distinct $k$-subsets with pairwise distinct sums. Every such sum is an integer between $1$ and $n$, because all selected parts occur in the original partition.

As $k$ ranges from $1$ to $s$, the total number of sums produced is
\[
\begin{aligned}
\sum_{k=1}^s\bigl(k(s-k)+1\bigr)
&=\frac{s(s^2+5)}6\\
&>\frac{s^3}{6}\\
&>n,
\end{aligned}
\]
where the last inequality follows from (1). By the pigeonhole principle, two produced sums are equal. Within a fixed value of $k$ the sums were distinct, so the equal sums come from subsets of different sizes. Calling the smaller one $X$ and the larger one $Y$ gives (2), a contradiction.

Therefore
\[
s\le\sqrt[3]{6n}.
\]

SOLUTION 2:
Assume the assertion is false, and choose the smallest positive integer $n$ for which a counterexample exists. Let
\[
n=a_1+\cdots+a_{k_{\min}}
\]
be an optimal $A$-partition with distinct parts
\[
b_1<b_2<\cdots<b_s,
\]
where
\[
s>\sqrt[3]{6n}>1.
\tag{1}
\]
Choose the ordering so that $a_{k_{\min}}=b_s$.

\textbf{Case 1.} Suppose
\[
b_s\ge\frac{s(s-1)}2+1.
\tag{2}
\]
Then
\[
n-b_s=a_1+\cdots+a_{k_{\min}-1}
\]
is an optimal partition of $n-b_s$: otherwise, adding one part $b_s$ to a shorter partition of $n-b_s$ would give a shorter partition of $n$. Every part used here is at most $n-b_s$, so this is an instance of the problem for the set $A\cap\{1,\ldots,n-b_s\}$. It contains at least $s-1$ distinct parts.

From $n<s^3/6$ and (2),
\[
\begin{aligned}
n-b_s
&\le n-\frac{s(s-1)}2-1\\
&<\frac{s^3}{6}-\frac{s(s-1)}2-1\\
&<\frac{(s-1)^3}{6}.
\end{aligned}
\]
Thus
\[
s-1>\sqrt[3]{6(n-b_s)},
\]
contradicting the minimal choice of $n$.

\textbf{Case 2.} Suppose
\[
b_s\le\frac{s(s-1)}2.
\tag{3}
\]
Set
\[
b_0=0,
\qquad
\Sigma_{0,0}=0,
\]
and for $1\le i\le j<s$ define
\[
\Sigma_{i,j}=b_1+b_2+\cdots+b_{i-1}+b_j.
\]
There are
\[
1+\frac{s(s-1)}2>b_s
\]
such sums. Hence two distinct sums, say $\Sigma_{i,j}$ and $\Sigma_{i',j'}$, are congruent modulo $b_s$.

For a fixed $i$, the residues are distinct, because if $i\le j<k<s$, then
\[
0<\Sigma_{i,k}-\Sigma_{i,j}=b_k-b_j<b_s.
\]
Thus $i\ne i'$. Assume $i>i'$. Write
\[
\Sigma_{i,j}-\Sigma_{i',j'}=r b_s
\]
for some integer $r$. Since $b_{i'}\le b_{j'}$,
\[
\Sigma_{i,j}-\Sigma_{i',j'}
=(b_{i'}-b_{j'})+b_j+b_{i'+1}+\cdots+b_{i-1},
\]
and every displayed positive term is less than $b_s$. Therefore
\[
-b_s<\Sigma_{i,j}-\Sigma_{i',j'}<(i-i')b_s.
\]
It follows that
\[
0\le r\le i-i'-1.
\tag{4}
\]

The sum $\Sigma_{i,j}$ consists of $i$ distinct parts from the original partition. Remove one occurrence of each of them. Replace them by the $i'$ parts occurring in $\Sigma_{i',j'}$ and by $r$ copies of $b_s$. The total sum is unchanged, while by (4) the number of inserted parts is
\[
i'+r<i.
\]
This contradicts optimality.

Both cases are impossible, so the claimed bound holds.
