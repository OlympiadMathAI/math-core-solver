SOURCE: IMO Shortlist

YEAR: 2015

TOPIC: Combinatorics

SUBTOPICS: Additive Combinatorics, Subset Sums, Parity, Uniqueness Arguments

STATEMENT:
Let $S$ be a nonempty set of positive integers. A positive integer $n$ is called \emph{clean} if it has exactly one representation as a sum of an odd number of distinct elements of $S$.

Prove that infinitely many positive integers are not clean.

SOLUTION 1:
Call a representation \emph{odd} or \emph{even} according to the parity of its number of summands. Suppose, for contradiction, that only finitely many positive integers are not clean. Then there exists $N$ such that every integer greater than $N$ has exactly one odd representation.

The set $S$ must be infinite, since otherwise all sufficiently large integers would have no representation at all.

\textbf{Property 1.} Every positive integer has at most one odd representation and at most one even representation.

\textit{Proof.}
Fix $n$. Choose $x\in S$ with $x>\max\{n,N\}$. No representation of $n$ can contain $x$. If $n$ had two even representations, adjoining $x$ to each would give two distinct odd representations of the clean integer $n+x$, impossible.

Similarly, choose distinct $y,z\in S$ with $y,z>\max\{n,N\}$. If $n$ had two odd representations, adjoining both $y$ and $z$ to each would produce two distinct odd representations of the clean integer $n+y+z$. $\square$

\textbf{Property 2.} Fix $s\in S$. If $n>N$ has no even representation, then for every integer $a\ge1$, the number $n+2as$ has an even representation containing $s$.

\textit{Proof.}
It is enough to show the following iterative step: if $n$ has no even representation avoiding $s$, then $n+2s$ has an even representation containing $s$.

The clean integer $n+s$ has an odd representation. That representation cannot contain $s$, because removing $s$ would give an even representation of $n$ avoiding $s$. Adding $s$ to it gives an even representation of $n+2s$ containing $s$. By Property 1 this is its only even representation, so in particular there is no even representation avoiding $s$. The argument may now be repeated. $\square$

\textbf{Property 3.} Every sufficiently large positive integer has an even representation.

\textit{Proof.}
Fix $s\in S$. For each residue representative $r\in\{1,2,\ldots,2s\}$, consider
\[
Z_r=\{r+2as:a\ge0\}.
\]
By Property 2, each progression $Z_r$ contains at most one integer greater than $N$ having no even representation. Hence only finitely many positive integers have no even representation. $\square$

Increasing $N$ if necessary, we may therefore assume that every integer greater than $N$ has exactly one odd and exactly one even representation.

\textbf{Property 4.} If $s,t\in S$ and $N<s<t$, then the even representation of $t$ contains $s$.

\textit{Proof.}
Suppose it does not. Adjoining $s$ to the even representation of $t$ gives an odd representation of $s+t$. Adjoining $t$ to the even representation of $s$ gives another odd representation of $s+t$. These are distinct: an even representation of $t$ cannot contain $t$, and an even representation of $s$ cannot contain $s$ or $t$. This contradicts the cleanliness of $s+t$. $\square$

List the elements of $S$ increasingly:
\[
s_1<s_2<s_3<\cdots,
\]
and put
\[
\sigma_i=s_1+s_2+\cdots+s_i,
\qquad
\sigma_0=0.
\]
Choose $k$ with $s_k>N$. For every $i>k$, Property 4 says that the even representation of $s_i$ contains
\[
s_k,s_{k+1},\ldots,s_{i-1}.
\]
All its remaining terms belong to $\{s_1,\ldots,s_{k-1}\}$. Thus
\[
s_i=\sigma_{i-1}-\sigma_{k-1}+R_i,
\tag{1}
\]
where $R_i$ is a subset sum of $s_1,\ldots,s_{k-1}$ and therefore
\[
0\le R_i\le\sigma_{k-1}.
\]

Choose $j_0>k$ so that
\[
\sigma_{j_0}>2\sigma_{k-1}.
\]
Then for every $j\ge j_0$, equation (1) gives
\[
s_{j+1}\ge\sigma_j-\sigma_{k-1}>\frac{\sigma_j}{2}.
\tag{2}
\]
Choose an index $p>j_0$ for which $R_p$ is minimal among all $R_i$ with $i>j_0$. Using (1) twice,
\[
\begin{aligned}
s_{p+1}
&=s_k+s_{k+1}+\cdots+s_p+R_{p+1}\\
&=(s_p-R_p)+s_p+R_{p+1}\\
&\ge2s_p.
\end{aligned}
\]
Hence no element of $S$ lies strictly between $s_p$ and $2s_p$.

The integer $2s_p$ is greater than $N$, so it has an even representation. That representation cannot contain an element of $S$ larger than $s_p$. On the other hand, applying (2) with $j=p-1$ gives
\[
2s_p>\sigma_{p-1},
\]
so the representation cannot use only $s_1,\ldots,s_{p-1}$. It must therefore contain $s_p$. Removing $s_p$ leaves an odd representation of $s_p$ not containing $s_p$. This is different from the one-term representation $s_p$, contradicting Property 1.

The contradiction proves that infinitely many positive integers are not clean.

SOLUTION 2:
We retain Property 1 from Solution 1: every positive integer has at most one odd and at most one even representation.

Assume again, for contradiction, that only finitely many positive integers are not clean. Let their number be $m-1$. List the elements of $S$ as
\[
s_1<s_2<\cdots
\]
and define
\[
\sigma_n=s_1+s_2+\cdots+s_n.
\]
Let $O_n$ and $E_n$ be the sets of integers representable as odd and even sums, respectively, of distinct elements of $\{s_1,\ldots,s_n\}$. We include the empty sum, so $0\in E_n$. Put
\[
O=\bigcup_{n\ge1}O_n,
\qquad
E=\bigcup_{n\ge1}E_n.
\]

\textbf{Step 1: estimates for $s_{n+1}$ and $\sigma_{n+1}$.}

Property 1 gives
\[
|O_n|=|E_n|=2^{n-1}.
\]
Among the $2^{n-1}+m$ integers in
\[
[1,2^{n-1}+m],
\]
at least $m$ do not belong to $O_n$. Since only $m-1$ positive integers are non-clean, one of them, say $x_n$, is clean. Its unique odd representation uses an element larger than $s_n$, so
\[
s_{n+1}\le x_n\le2^{n-1}+m.
\tag{3}
\]
The integer $s_1$ is clean, and all positive integers below $s_1$ are non-clean, so $s_1\le m$. Summing (3) gives
\[
\sigma_{n+1}\le2^n-1+(n+1)m.
\tag{4}
\]

Since $O_{n+1}\subseteq[1,\sigma_{n+1}]$ and $|O_{n+1}|=2^n$, we also have
\[
\sigma_{n+1}\ge2^n.
\tag{5}
\]
Subtracting the upper estimate for $\sigma_n$ from (5), we obtain
\[
s_{n+1}\ge2^{n-1}+1-nm.
\tag{6}
\]
Thus, for every $n\ge1$,
\[
2^{n-1}+1-nm\le s_{n+1}\le2^{n-1}+m,
\]
and
\[
2^n\le\sigma_{n+1}\le2^n-1+(n+1)m.
\tag{7}
\]

\textbf{Step 2: sufficiently large integers have even representations.}

Because representations of a fixed parity are unique, we have disjoint unions
\[
E_{n+1}=E_n\sqcup(s_{n+1}+O_n),
\qquad
O_{n+1}=O_n\sqcup(s_{n+1}+E_n).
\tag{8}
\]
For sufficiently large $n$, estimates (6) and (7) give
\[
s_{n+2}>\sigma_n.
\]

We claim that, for all sufficiently large $n$,
\[
(\sigma_n-s_{n+1},\ s_{n+2}-s_{n+1})\subseteq E_n,
\tag{9}
\]
where intervals contain the integers lying strictly between their endpoints. Indeed, every integer in $(\sigma_n,s_{n+2})$ is then clean. It is too large to lie in $O_n$, and too small for an odd representation using $s_{n+2}$ or a later element. Hence it belongs to
\[
O_{n+1}\setminus O_n=s_{n+1}+E_n.
\]
Subtracting $s_{n+1}$ proves (9).

Using (7), the interval in (9) contains
\[
(2nm,\ 2^{n-1}-(n+2)m)
\]
for all sufficiently large $n$. These intervals eventually overlap and cover all sufficiently large integers. Therefore $\mathbb Z_{\ge0}\setminus E$ is finite. Since all sufficiently large integers are clean, $\mathbb Z_{\ge0}\setminus O$ is finite as well. We may henceforth choose $N$ such that every integer greater than $N$ has exactly one odd and exactly one even representation.

\textbf{Step 3: the finite subset-sum sets are almost intervals.}

Taking complements of subsets of $\{s_1,\ldots,s_n\}$ gives
\[
\begin{aligned}
E_{2n}&=\sigma_{2n}-E_{2n},
& O_{2n}&=\sigma_{2n}-O_{2n},\\
E_{2n+1}&=\sigma_{2n+1}-O_{2n+1},
& O_{2n+1}&=\sigma_{2n+1}-E_{2n+1}.
\end{aligned}
\tag{10}
\]
We claim that for every sufficiently large $n$,
\[
(N,\sigma_n-N)\subseteq O_n\cap E_n.
\tag{11}
\]
Indeed, by (6) and (7), for sufficiently large $n$,
\[
s_{n+1}>\frac{\sigma_n}{2}.
\]
Thus the odd representation of an integer in $(N,\sigma_n/2]$ cannot use any element beyond $s_n$, so that interval lies in $O_n$. An even representation using an element beyond $s_n$ would contain at least $s_{n+1}+s_1>\sigma_n/2$, so the same interval lies in $E_n$. The complement relations (10) extend this conclusion to the upper half, proving (11).

\textbf{Step 4: the contradiction.}

The sets $\mathbb Z_{\ge0}\setminus O$ and $\mathbb Z_{\ge0}\setminus E$ are finite and nonempty: $0\notin O$, and $1\notin E$. Let
\[
o=\max(\mathbb Z_{\ge0}\setminus O),
\qquad
e=\max(\mathbb Z_{\ge0}\setminus E).
\]
Take $k$ so large that $e,o\le N$, $\sigma_{2k}>2N$, and (11) holds for all indices at least $2k$.

For $2k$ elements, the complement relations preserve parity. Hence the least integer greater than $N$ which is absent from $E_{2k}$ is $\sigma_{2k}-e$, while the least new even sum requiring an element beyond $s_{2k}$ is $s_{2k+1}+s_1$. Likewise, the least integer greater than $N$ absent from $O_{2k}$ is $\sigma_{2k}-o$, and the least new odd sum is $s_{2k+1}$. Therefore
\[
\sigma_{2k}-e=s_{2k+1}+s_1,
\qquad
\sigma_{2k}-o=s_{2k+1}.
\tag{12}
\]

For $2k+1$ elements, taking complements reverses parity. The analogous comparison gives
\[
\sigma_{2k+1}-e=s_{2k+2},
\qquad
\sigma_{2k+1}-o=s_{2k+2}+s_1.
\tag{13}
\]
Subtracting the two equations in (12) gives
\[
s_1=o-e,
\]
whereas subtracting the two equations in (13) gives
\[
-s_1=o-e.
\]
Thus $s_1=-s_1$, impossible because $s_1>0$.

This final contradiction completes the proof.
