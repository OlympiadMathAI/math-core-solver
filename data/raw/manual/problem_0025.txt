ID: 0025

SOURCE: IMO Shortlist

YEAR: 2010

TOPIC: Number Theory

SUBTOPICS: Sums of Squares, Rational Polynomials, Infinite Descent, Four-Square Identity

STATEMENT:
Find the smallest positive integer $n$ for which there exist polynomials
\[
f_1,f_2,\ldots,f_n\in\mathbb{Q}[x]
\]
such that
\[
x^2+7
=
f_1(x)^2+f_2(x)^2+\cdots+f_n(x)^2.
\]

SOLUTION 1:
The smallest value is
\[
\boxed{5}.
\]
Indeed,
\[
x^2+7=x^2+2^2+1^2+1^2+1^2,
\]
so five squares suffice.

Suppose, for a contradiction, that four squares suffice:
\[
x^2+7
=
f_1(x)^2+f_2(x)^2+f_3(x)^2+f_4(x)^2.
\tag{1}
\]
Every $f_i$ has degree at most $1$, since a term of larger degree could not be cancelled in a sum of squares over the reals. Write
\[
f_i(x)=a_ix+b_i,
\qquad
a_i,b_i\in\mathbb Q.
\]
Comparing coefficients in (1),
\[
\sum_{i=1}^4a_i^2=1,
\qquad
\sum_{i=1}^4a_ib_i=0,
\qquad
\sum_{i=1}^4b_i^2=7.
\tag{2}
\]

Put
\[
p_i=a_i+b_i,
\qquad
q_i=a_i-b_i.
\]
Then (2) gives
\[
\sum_{i=1}^4p_i^2=8,
\qquad
\sum_{i=1}^4q_i^2=8,
\qquad
\sum_{i=1}^4p_iq_i=-6.
\tag{3}
\]

Clear denominators. There exist integers
\[
x_1,\ldots,x_4,\ y_1,\ldots,y_4
\]
and a positive integer $m$ such that
\[
p_i=\frac{x_i}{m},
\qquad
q_i=\frac{y_i}{m},
\]
and hence
\[
\sum_{i=1}^4x_i^2=8m^2,
\tag{4}
\]
\[
\sum_{i=1}^4y_i^2=8m^2,
\tag{5}
\]
\[
\sum_{i=1}^4x_iy_i=-6m^2.
\tag{6}
\]
Choose such a solution with $m$ minimal.

Squares modulo $8$ are $0,1,4$. From (4), the sum of four squares is divisible by $8$, which forces all $x_i$ to be even. Similarly, all $y_i$ are even. Then the left side of (6) is divisible by $4$, so $m$ is even as well. Dividing every $x_i,y_i,m$ by $2$ gives another solution of (4)--(6) with a smaller positive denominator, contradicting the minimality of $m$.

Thus four squares are impossible, while five squares are possible. Hence the minimum is $5$.

SOLUTION 2:
As in Solution 1, suppose that
\[
x^2+7=\sum_{i=1}^4(a_ix+b_i)^2
\]
with rational $a_i,b_i$. Then
\[
\sum a_i^2=1,
\qquad
\sum a_ib_i=0,
\qquad
\sum b_i^2=7.
\tag{1}
\]

Euler's four-square identity states that
\[
\begin{aligned}
\left(\sum_{i=1}^4a_i^2\right)
\left(\sum_{i=1}^4b_i^2\right)
&=
(a_1b_1+a_2b_2+a_3b_3+a_4b_4)^2\\
&\quad+
(a_1b_2-a_2b_1+a_3b_4-a_4b_3)^2\\
&\quad+
(a_1b_3-a_3b_1+a_4b_2-a_2b_4)^2\\
&\quad+
(a_1b_4-a_4b_1+a_2b_3-a_3b_2)^2.
\end{aligned}
\]
Using (1), the first square on the right vanishes, so
\[
7=r_1^2+r_2^2+r_3^2
\tag{2}
\]
for three rational numbers $r_1,r_2,r_3$.

Choose integers $m_1,m_2,m_3$ and a positive integer $m$, with $m$ minimal, such that
\[
r_i=\frac{m_i}{m}.
\]
Equation (2) becomes
\[
7m^2=m_1^2+m_2^2+m_3^2,
\]
or equivalently
\[
8m^2=m_1^2+m_2^2+m_3^2+m^2.
\tag{3}
\]

The right side of (3) is a sum of four squares divisible by $8$. Therefore
\[
m_1,m_2,m_3,m
\]
are all even. Dividing them all by $2$ produces a representation of the same form with a smaller positive denominator, contradicting the minimality of $m$.

Hence $7$ is not a sum of three rational squares, so four polynomial squares are impossible. The five-square representation from Solution 1 proves that the answer is $5$.
