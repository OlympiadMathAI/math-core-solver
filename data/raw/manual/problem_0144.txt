ID: 0144

SOURCE: IMO Shortlist

YEAR: 2014

TOPIC: Number Theory

SUBTOPICS: Recursive Sequences, Primitive Prime Divisors, Modular Periodicity, p-adic Valuations

STATEMENT:
Let $c\ge1$ be an integer. Define a sequence of positive integers by
\[
a_1=c
\]
and
\[
a_{n+1}=a_n^3-4ca_n^2+5c^2a_n+c
\qquad(n\ge1).
\]
Prove that for every integer $n\ge2$ there exists a prime $p$ dividing $a_n$ but none of $a_1,\ldots,a_{n-1}$.

SOLUTION 1:
Define
\[
x_0=0,
\qquad
x_n=\frac{a_n}{c}\quad(n\ge1).
\]
Induction from the recurrence shows that every $x_n$ is a positive integer and
\[
x_{n+1}=c^2(x_n^3-4x_n^2+5x_n)+1
\qquad(n\ge0).
\tag{1}
\]
In particular,
\[
x_1=1,
\qquad
x_2=2c^2+1.
\]
Moreover,
\[
x_{n+1}
=c^2x_n(x_n-2)^2+c^2x_n+1>x_n,
\tag{2}
\]
so the sequence is strictly increasing. Equation (1) also gives
\[
\gcd(x_{n+1},c)=1.
\tag{3}
\]
Thus it is enough to find, for each $n\ge2$, a prime dividing $x_n$ but no earlier $x_i$.

We establish three claims.

\textbf{Claim 1.} If $i\equiv j\pmod m$, where $i,j\ge0$ and $m\ge1$, then
\[
x_i\equiv x_j\pmod{x_m}.
\tag{4}
\]

It suffices to prove $x_{i+m}\equiv x_i\pmod{x_m}$ for all $i\ge0$. For $i=0$ this is clear. The induction step follows by applying the polynomial recurrence (1) to congruent arguments.

\textbf{Claim 2.} If $i\equiv j\pmod m$, where $i,j\ge2$ and $m\ge1$, then
\[
x_i\equiv x_j\pmod{x_m^2}.
\tag{5}
\]

Again it is enough to prove $x_{i+m}\equiv x_i\pmod{x_m^2}$ for $i\ge2$. Put $L=5c^2$. From (1),
\[
x_{m+1}\equiv Lx_m+1\pmod{x_m^2}.
\]
Hence
\[
\begin{aligned}
x_{m+2}
&=c^2(x_{m+1}^3-4x_{m+1}^2+5x_{m+1})+1\\
&\equiv c^2\bigl((1+Lx_m)^3-4(1+Lx_m)^2+5(1+Lx_m)\bigr)+1\\
&\equiv2c^2+1=x_2
\pmod{x_m^2}.
\end{aligned}
\]
The induction step again follows from (1).

\textbf{Claim 3.} For every $n\ge2$,
\[
x_n>x_1x_2\cdots x_{n-2},
\tag{6}
\]
where the empty product is $1$.

The cases $n=2,3$ are immediate. Suppose (6) holds for some $n\ge3$. Since $x_2\ge3$, relation (2) gives $x_n\ge x_3\ge7$. Therefore
\[
x_{n+1}
>x_n^3-4x_n^2+5x_n
>x_n^2
>x_nx_{n-1}.
\]
Using the induction hypothesis in the last expression proves (6) for $n+1$.

Now fix $n\ge2$. By Claim 3, some prime $p$ occurs in $x_n$ to a higher exponent than it occurs in
\[
x_1x_2\cdots x_{n-2}.
\tag{7}
\]
In particular $p\mid x_n$. Suppose that $p$ also divides an earlier term, and let $k<n$ be the smallest index with $p\mid x_k$. Since
\[
x_n\equiv1\pmod{x_{n-1}}
\]
by (1), we have $k\le n-2$; also $k\ge2$ because $x_1=1$.

Write
\[
n=qk+r,
\qquad0\le r<k.
\]
Claim 1 gives
\[
x_n\equiv x_r\pmod{x_k}.
\]
Thus $p\mid x_r$. By minimality of $k$, this forces $r=0$, so $k\mid n$.

Claim 2 now yields
\[
x_n\equiv x_k\pmod{x_k^2}.
\tag{8}
\]
Let $\alpha=v_p(x_k)$. Since $x_k$ is among the factors in (7), the choice of $p$ gives
\[
p^{\alpha+1}\mid x_n.
\]
Also $p^{\alpha+1}\mid x_k^2$. Reducing (8) modulo $p^{\alpha+1}$ would then imply
\[
p^{\alpha+1}\mid x_k,
\]
contradicting the definition of $\alpha$.

Therefore $p$ divides no earlier $x_i$. By (3), it also does not divide $c$, so from $a_i=cx_i$ it follows that $p$ divides $a_n$ and none of $a_1,\ldots,a_{n-1}$.
