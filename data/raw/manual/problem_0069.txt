ID: 0069

SOURCE: IMO Shortlist

YEAR: 2012

TOPIC: Combinatorics

SUBTOPICS: Combinatorial Games, Strategy, Invariants, Descent, Circular Arrangements

STATEMENT:
Players $A$ and $B$ play a game with $N\ge 2012$ coins and $2012$ boxes arranged around a circle. Initially, $A$ distributes the coins among the boxes so that there is at least one coin in each box. Then they make moves in the order
\[
B,A,B,A,\ldots
\]
according to the following rules.

On every move, $B$ passes one coin from every box to an adjacent box.

On every move, $A$ chooses several coins that were not involved in $B$'s previous move and are in different boxes. She passes every chosen coin to an adjacent box.

Player $A$ wants to ensure that there is at least one coin in each box after every move of hers, regardless of how $B$ plays and regardless of how many moves are made. Find the least $N$ for which she can succeed.

SOLUTION 1:
We solve the problem for a circle of $n\ge 7$ boxes. We prove that the least possible number of coins is
\[
2n-2.
\]
For $n=2012$, this gives
\[
\boxed{N_{\min}=4022}.
\]

\textbf{Part 1: $2n-2$ coins are sufficient.}

Call a distribution regular if exactly $n-2$ boxes contain two coins and exactly two boxes contain one coin. Call these boxes red and white, respectively. Player $A$ starts with any regular distribution.

Suppose $B$ makes a move $M$. We show that $A$ can make a legal move that restores a regular distribution.

Consider the following exceptional situation:

\emph{There is a red box $R$ whose two neighbors are white, and neither white neighbor sends a coin to $R$ during $M$.}

There can be at most one such red box.

First suppose the exceptional situation does not occur. For each red box $X$, exactly one of its two original coins is moved by $B$. Player $A$ moves the other original coin from $X$ in the direction opposite to the direction in which $B$ moved the first coin. She does this for every red box. This is legal because the chosen coins were not involved in $M$ and lie in different boxes.

Together, the moves of $B$ and $A$ send the two original coins of each red box to its two different neighbors. Therefore every box having a red neighbor is nonempty after $A$'s move. The only possible box with no red neighbor is a red box lying between the two white boxes. If such a box exists, the exceptional situation does not occur, so it receives a coin from at least one white neighbor during $M$; that coin was involved in $M$ and is not moved by $A$. Thus every box is nonempty.

Every box gives away all of its original coins during the two moves: a red box gives one coin to $B$ and its other coin to $A$, while a white box gives its unique coin to $B$. Each neighboring red box contributes exactly one coin, and each neighboring white box contributes at most one coin. Hence no box contains more than two coins. Since the total number of coins is $2n-2$, the resulting distribution is regular.

Now suppose the exceptional situation occurs at a red box $R$. Player $A$ leaves the unmoved original coin in $R$ and applies the preceding rule to every other red box. Box $R$ then contains exactly one coin. Every other box has a red neighbor different from $R$, and that neighbor sends it one coin during the two moves, so every other box is nonempty. As before, no box other than $R$ can contain more than two coins. The total is $2n-2$, so the resulting distribution is again regular.

Thus $A$ can restore a regular distribution after every move of $B$ and can continue forever.

\textbf{Part 2: $2n-3$ coins are not sufficient.}

Assume that
\[
N\le 2n-3.
\]
A set $\alpha$ of $\ell$ consecutive boxes will be called an arc if
\[
\ell\le n-2
\qquad\text{and}\qquad
N(\alpha)\le 2\ell-3,
\tag{1}
\]
where $N(\alpha)$ is the number of coins in those boxes.

Whenever all boxes are nonempty, every arc contains a possibly shorter arc whose two end boxes contain exactly one coin. Indeed, if an end box contains at least two coins, remove it. The new length is $\ell-1$, while the number of coins decreases by at least two, so condition (1) is preserved. Repeating this operation gives the claim.

We first justify that an arc always exists in any nonempty distribution with at most $2n-3$ coins. Let the box contents be $c_1,\ldots,c_n$, and put
\[
d_i=2-c_i,
\qquad
D=\sum_{i=1}^n d_i=2n-N\ge 3.
\]
Suppose no arc exists. For every adjacent pair of boxes $i,i+1$, the complementary block of $n-2$ consecutive boxes is not an arc. Its total deficit is therefore at most $2$, so
\[
D-d_i-d_{i+1}\le 2,
\]
or
\[
d_i+d_{i+1}\ge D-2
\qquad\text{for every }i.
\tag{2}
\]
Since every box is nonempty, $d_i\le 1$.

If $D\ge 5$, (2) is impossible because its left-hand side is at most $2$. If $D=4$, (2) forces every $d_i=1$, giving $D=n\ge 7$, a contradiction. If $D=3$, (2) forces every $d_i$ to be $0$ or $1$ and forbids two consecutive zeros. Since their sum is $3$, there are only three ones, so on a circle there can be at most three zeros; hence $n\le 6$, again a contradiction. Thus an arc exists.

Take an arc $\alpha$ whose end boxes contain exactly one coin, and label its boxes consecutively as
\[
1,2,\ldots,\ell,
\qquad 3\le \ell\le n-2.
\]
Player $B$ moves one coin counterclockwise from boxes $1$ and $n$, and one coin clockwise from every other box. No coin enters $\alpha$ during this move, while the unique coins in boxes $1$ and $\ell$ leave it. Hence the boxes of $\alpha$ contain
\[
N(\alpha)-2
\]
coins immediately after $B$'s move. Moreover, box $1$ is empty, while box $\ell$ contains exactly one coin $c$, received from box $\ell-1$; the coin $c$ was involved in $B$'s move.

During $A$'s response, at most two coins can enter $\alpha$ from outside: one through box $1$ and one through box $\ell$. Let $k\le 2$ be the number that enter.

If $k<2$, then after $A$'s move the same $\ell$ boxes contain at most
\[
N(\alpha)-2+k<N(\alpha)
\]
coins. They still satisfy (1), so they form an arc with a strictly smaller number of coins.

If $k=2$, one coin enters through each end. If $A$ moves any coin from $\alpha$ to a box outside $\alpha$, then the total in $\alpha$ is already strictly less than $N(\alpha)$, and the same arc works. We may therefore assume that no coin leaves $\alpha$.

Player $A$ cannot move a coin out through box $1$, because it was empty immediately after $B$'s move, and she cannot move the coin $c$ out through box $\ell$, because $c$ was involved in $B$'s move. After the two incoming moves, box $\ell$ contains at least two coins. The total number of coins in $\alpha$ is at most $N(\alpha)$, so removing box $\ell$ leaves an arc of length $\ell-1$ with at most
\[
N(\alpha)-2< N(\alpha)
\]
coins.

Thus, unless an empty box has already appeared after $A$'s move, player $B$ can produce a new arc with strictly fewer coins than the previous one. Repeating the argument would make the positive integer $N(\alpha)$ decrease indefinitely, which is impossible. Hence an empty box must eventually occur after a move of $A$.

Together with Part 1, this proves that the least sufficient number of coins is $2n-2$. For $n=2012$, the least value is $4022$.
