ID: 0099

SOURCE: IMO Shortlist

YEAR: 2013

TOPIC: Combinatorics

SUBTOPICS: Periodic Sequences, Pigeonhole Principle, Sliding Sums, Finite-State Arguments

STATEMENT:
Let $r$ be a positive integer, and let $a_0,a_1,\ldots$ be an infinite sequence of real numbers. Assume that for all nonnegative integers $m$ and $s$, there exists an integer
\[
n\in\{m+1,m+2,\ldots,m+r\}
\]
such that
\[
a_m+a_{m+1}+\cdots+a_{m+s}
=
a_n+a_{n+1}+\cdots+a_{n+s}.
\]
Prove that the sequence is periodic: there exists an integer $p\ge1$ such that
\[
a_{j+p}=a_j
\]
for every $j\ge0$.

SOLUTION 1:
For $u\le v$, write
\[
S(u,v)=a_u+a_{u+1}+\cdots+a_{v-1},
\]
so $S(v,v)=0$.

We begin with a lemma.

\textbf{Lemma.} Let $b_0,b_1,\ldots$ be a sequence such that for every $m\ge0$ there is an index
\[
n\in\{m+1,\ldots,m+r\}
\]
with $b_n=b_m$. Then:

\begin{enumerate}
\item for every $k\le \ell$, some $t\in\{\ell,\ell+1,\ldots,\ell+r-1\}$ satisfies $b_t=b_k$;
\item the sequence $(b_i)$ takes at most $r$ distinct values.
\end{enumerate}

\textbf{Proof.} Starting from $k_1=k$, repeatedly choose
\[
k_i<k_{i+1}\le k_i+r
\]
with $b_{k_{i+1}}=b_{k_i}$. The indices are unbounded. If $k_j$ is the first one at least $\ell$, then
\[
\ell\le k_j\le\ell+r-1,
\]
which proves the first assertion.

If $r+1$ distinct values occurred at indices $i_1,\ldots,i_{r+1}$, apply the first assertion with
\[
\ell=\max(i_1,\ldots,i_{r+1}).
\]
Each of those $r+1$ values would then occur among the $r$ positions
\[
\ell,\ell+1,\ldots,\ell+r-1,
\]
a contradiction.

Set $s=0$ in the hypothesis of the problem. The lemma shows that the sequence $(a_i)$ takes at most $r$ distinct values. Define the length-$r$ blocks
\[
A_i=(a_i,a_{i+1},\ldots,a_{i+r-1}).
\]
There are at most $r^r$ possible blocks. Consequently, for every $K\ge0$, two of
\[
A_K,A_{K+1},\ldots,A_{K+r^r}
\]
are equal. Thus there exist integers $d\ge K$ and $1\le p\le r^r$ such that
\[
A_d=A_{d+p}.
\]
Since only finitely many values of $p$ are possible, one fixed $p$ occurs for arbitrarily large $d$. Let
\[
D=\{d\ge0:A_d=A_{d+p}\}.
\]
Then $D$ is unbounded.

We prove that $D$ contains every nonnegative integer. Since it is unbounded, it is enough to prove the downward implication
\[
d+1\in D\quad\Longrightarrow\quad d\in D.
\tag{1}
\]
Assume $d+1\in D$. Define
\[
b_k=S(k,k+p).
\]
The original hypothesis with $s=p-1$ shows that the sequence $(b_k)$ satisfies the lemma. Hence some
\[
t\in\{d+1,d+2,\ldots,d+r\}
\]
satisfies
\[
S(t,t+p)=S(d,d+p).
\]
Equivalently,
\[
S(d,t)=S(d+p,t+p).
\tag{2}
\]
Because $d+1\in D$, the blocks $A_{d+1}$ and $A_{d+p+1}$ are equal, so
\[
S(d+1,t)=S(d+p+1,t+p).
\tag{3}
\]
Subtracting (3) from (2) gives
\[
a_d=a_{d+p}.
\]
Together with $A_{d+1}=A_{d+p+1}$, this yields
\[
A_d=A_{d+p},
\]
so $d\in D$. This proves (1).

Therefore $A_d=A_{d+p}$ for every $d\ge0$, and in particular
\[
a_{d+p}=a_d
\]
for every $d\ge0$. The sequence is periodic.
