ID: 0425

SOURCE: IMO Shortlist

YEAR: 2023

TOPIC: Number Theory

SUBTOPICS: Functional Equations, Iteration, Injectivity, Divisibility Posets, Arithmetic Progressions

STATEMENT:
Let $\mathbb Z_{>0}$ be the set of positive integers. Determine all functions
\[
f:\mathbb Z_{>0}\to\mathbb Z_{>0}
\]
such that
\[
f^{\,b f(a)}(a+1)=(a+1)f(b)
\tag{1}
\]
for all positive integers $a,b$, where $f^k$ denotes the $k$-fold iterate of $f$ and
\[
f^0(x)=x.
\]

SOLUTION 1:
The only solution is
\[
\boxed{f(n)=n+1}.
\]

For convenience, write $P(a,b)$ for equation (1).

\textbf{Step 1: $f$ is injective.}

We first prove that, for every $a\ge2$, the forward orbit
\[
a,f(a),f^2(a),\ldots
\]
is infinite.

From $P(a,1)$,
\[
f^{f(a)}(a+1)=(a+1)f(1).
\]
As $a$ varies, the right-hand side takes infinitely many values. Hence the range of $f$ is infinite.

Now fix $a\ge2$. From $P(a-1,b)$,
\[
f^{\,b f(a-1)}(a)=a f(b).
\tag{2}
\]
As $b$ varies, the right-hand side takes infinitely many values, so the orbit of $a$ is infinite. In particular,
\[
f^r(a)\ne a
\qquad(a\ge2,\ r\ge1).
\tag{3}
\]

Suppose
\[
f(b)=f(c)
\]
for some $b<c$. For every $a$,
\[
\begin{aligned}
(a+1)f(c)
&=f^{\,c f(a)}(a+1)\\
&=
f^{(c-b)f(a)}
\left(f^{\,b f(a)}(a+1)\right)\\
&=
f^{(c-b)f(a)}\bigl((a+1)f(b)\bigr)\\
&=
f^{(c-b)f(a)}\bigl((a+1)f(c)\bigr).
\end{aligned}
\]
Thus the integer $(a+1)f(c)\ge2$ is periodic under $f$, contradicting (3). Hence $f$ is injective.

\textbf{Step 2: the range of $f$ is exactly $\{2,3,4,\ldots\}$.}

First,
\[
1\notin f(\mathbb Z_{>0}).
\tag{4}
\]
Indeed, if $f(b)=1$, then $P(a,b)$ gives
\[
f^{\,b f(a)}(a+1)=a+1,
\]
contradicting (3).

Call $x$ a \emph{descendant} of $y$ if
\[
x=f^r(y)
\]
for some $r\ge1$. Two distinct integers at least $2$ cannot be descendants of one another in both directions, by (3).

We claim that for any $a,b\ge2$, either $a=b$, or one is a descendant of the other. Fix $c\ge2$ and put
\[
u=f^{\,c f(a-1)-1}(a),
\qquad
v=f^{\,c f(b-1)-1}(b).
\]
By (2),
\[
f(u)=a f(c),
\qquad
f(v)=b f(c).
\]
Applying (2) once more,
\[
\begin{aligned}
f^{\,v f(a-1)}(a)
&=a f(v)
=abf(c),\\
f^{\,u f(b-1)}(b)
&=b f(u)
=abf(c).
\end{aligned}
\]
After cancelling equal iterates by injectivity, either $a=b$, or one of $a,b$ is a positive iterate of the other.

Let
\[
q=f(1).
\]
By (4), $q\ge2$. For any $a\ge2$, comparability applies to $a$ and $q$. The point $q$ cannot be a descendant of $a$: if
\[
f^r(a)=f(1),
\]
then injectivity gives $a=1$ when $r=1$, while for $r\ge2$ it gives $1$ in the range of $f$. Therefore $a$ is a descendant of $q$. Hence every $a\ge2$ lies in the range of $f$.

Together with (4),
\[
f(\mathbb Z_{>0})=\{2,3,4,\ldots\}.
\tag{5}
\]

\textbf{Step 3: $f(1)=2$.}

From (2) and (5), for all $a,n\ge2$ the number
\[
na
\]
is a descendant of $a$: choose $m$ with $f(m)=n$, and use
\[
f^{\,m f(a-1)}(a)=af(m)=an.
\tag{6}
\]

In particular, every even integer at least $4$ is a descendant of $2$. By (5), there is some $t$ with
\[
f(t)=2.
\]
The integer $t$ cannot be even, because then $t$ and $2$ would be descendants of one another, contradicting (3). Hence
\[
t=2k+1
\]
for some $k\ge0$.

We show that
\[
f(2k+1)\ge f(1).
\tag{7}
\]
For $k=0$ this is immediate. Suppose $k\ge1$. Choose $r$ such that
\[
f^r(2)=2k+2,
\]
which is possible by (6). For every sufficiently large $b$, equations $P(1,b)$ and $P(2k+1,b)$ give
\[
f^{\,b f(1)-r}(2k+2)=2f(b)
\]
and
\[
f^{\,b f(2k+1)}(2k+2)=(2k+2)f(b).
\]
The second target is a proper descendant of the first by (6). Since an orbit has no repetitions,
\[
b f(2k+1)>b f(1)-r.
\]
Letting $b$ grow proves (7).

But
\[
f(2k+1)=2,
\]
while (4) gives $f(1)\ge2$. Therefore
\[
f(1)=2.
\tag{8}
\]

\textbf{Step 4: $f(2)=3$ and $f(3)=4$.}

From $P(1,b)$ and (8),
\[
f^{2b}(2)=2f(b).
\tag{9}
\]
Taking $b=1$,
\[
f^2(2)=4.
\tag{10}
\]
Put
\[
p=f(2).
\]
Taking $b=p$ in (9),
\[
f^{2p}(2)=2f(p)=2f^2(2)=8.
\]
Together with (10),
\[
f^{2p-2}(4)=8.
\tag{11}
\]

On the other hand, $P(3,1)$ gives
\[
f^{f(3)}(4)=4f(1)=8.
\]
The orbit of $4$ has no repetitions, so
\[
f(3)=2p-2.
\tag{12}
\]

We need one divisibility observation.

\textbf{Claim.}
If
\[
f(m)\mid f(n),
\]
then
\[
m\le n.
\]

If $f(m)=f(n)$, this follows from injectivity. Otherwise, write
\[
f(n)=qf(m)
\qquad(q\ge2).
\]
For any $a$, the values given by $P(a,m)$ and $P(a,n)$ are
\[
(a+1)f(m)
\qquad\text{and}\qquad
q(a+1)f(m).
\]
By (6), the second is a descendant of the first. Hence
\[
n f(a)>m f(a),
\]
so $n>m$.

Every divisor of $p=f(2)$ which is at least $2$ belongs to the range of $f$, and the claim shows that it must be either
\[
f(1)=2
\qquad\text{or}\qquad
f(2)=p.
\]
Therefore $p$ is either an odd prime or $4$. The case $p=4$ is impossible because (10) would give
\[
f(4)=4,
\]
contradicting (3). Thus $p$ is an odd prime.

By (12),
\[
f(3)=2(p-1).
\]
The divisor $p-1\ge2$ of $f(3)$ must equal one of
\[
f(1)=2,\qquad f(2)=p,\qquad f(3)=2(p-1).
\]
Only the first is possible, so
\[
p-1=2.
\]
Hence
\[
f(2)=3,
\qquad
f(3)=4.
\tag{13}
\]

\textbf{Step 5: determination of all values.}

We first prove
\[
f\bigl(2f(b)-1\bigr)=2b+2
\qquad(b\ge1).
\tag{14}
\]
By (9) and (10),
\[
f^{2b-2}(4)=2f(b).
\]
Then $P(2f(b)-1,1)$ gives
\[
f^{\,f(2f(b)-1)}(2f(b))=4f(b),
\]
whereas $P(3,b)$ and (13) give
\[
f^{4b}(4)=4f(b).
\]
Comparing the two iterate counts along the orbit of $4$,
\[
2b-2+f(2f(b)-1)=4b,
\]
which proves (14).

We now use induction. Suppose
\[
f(n)=n+1
\qquad(1\le n\le2b+1).
\]
Replacing $b$ by $b+1$ in (14),
\[
f(2b+3)
=
f\bigl(2f(b+1)-1\bigr)
=
2b+4.
\tag{15}
\]

The induction hypothesis gives
\[
f^b(b+2)=2b+2.
\]
Therefore
\[
f(f(2b+2))
=
f^{b+2}(b+2).
\]
By $P(b+1,1)$,
\[
f^{b+2}(b+2)=2(b+2)=2b+4=f(2b+3).
\]
Injectivity and (15) yield
\[
f(2b+2)=2b+3.
\]
Starting from (8) and (13), induction proves
\[
f(n)=n+1
\qquad(n\ge1).
\]

Finally, this function works because
\[
f^r(x)=x+r,
\]
so
\[
f^{\,b f(a)}(a+1)
=
a+1+b(a+1)
=
(a+1)(b+1)
=
(a+1)f(b).
\]

SOLUTION 2:
We first establish three foundational facts.

For every $a\ge2$, the orbit of $a$ is infinite. Indeed, $P(a,1)$ shows that the range of $f$ is infinite, and then
\[
P(a-1,b):
\qquad
f^{\,b f(a-1)}(a)=af(b)
\]
shows that the orbit of $a$ contains infinitely many values. Hence no positive iterate fixes an integer at least $2$.

If $f(b)=f(c)$ with $b<c$, comparing $P(a,b)$ and $P(a,c)$ would make $(a+1)f(c)$ periodic. Thus $f$ is injective.

Also, $1$ is not in the range of $f$, because $f(b)=1$ would make $a+1$ periodic through $P(a,b)$. For $a,b\ge2$, fix $c\ge2$ and set
\[
u=f^{\,c f(a-1)-1}(a),
\qquad
v=f^{\,c f(b-1)-1}(b).
\]
Then
\[
f(u)=af(c),\qquad f(v)=bf(c),
\]
and
\[
f^{\,v f(a-1)}(a)=abf(c)=f^{\,u f(b-1)}(b).
\]
Injectivity shows that one of $a,b$ is an iterate of the other, unless they are equal.

Take one of the two integers to be $f(1)$. The value $f(1)$ cannot be a descendant of an integer $a\ge2$: if
\[
f^r(a)=f(1),
\]
then injectivity gives $a=1$ when $r=1$, while for $r\ge2$ it gives $1$ in the range of $f$. Therefore every integer at least $2$ is a descendant of $f(1)$ and hence of $1$. Consequently,
\[
f:\mathbb Z_{>0}\longrightarrow\{2,3,4,\ldots\}
\]
is a bijection, and no positive iterate fixes any positive integer.

Define
\[
g(n)=f(f(n)-1).
\]
Using $P(f(a)-1,b)$,
\[
f^{\,b g(a)+1}(a)=f(a)f(b).
\]
By symmetry in $a,b$ and injectivity,
\[
f^{\,b g(a)}(a)=f^{\,a g(b)}(b).
\tag{1}
\]

Because every positive integer lies on the orbit of $1$, define $r(a)$ by
\[
f^{r(a)}(1)=a.
\]
Equation (1) gives
\[
r(a)+bg(a)=r(b)+ag(b).
\]
Adding the three corresponding identities cyclically for $a,b,c$,
\[
(b-c)g(a)+(c-a)g(b)+(a-b)g(c)=0.
\tag{2}
\]

Take
\[
(a,b,c)=(n,n+1,n+2).
\]
Then (2) becomes
\[
g(n+2)-g(n+1)=g(n+1)-g(n).
\]
Thus
\[
g(n)=Cn+D
\]
for integers $C,D$.

The map
\[
n\longmapsto f(n)-1
\]
is a bijection of the positive integers, and $f$ is a bijection onto $\{2,3,\ldots\}$. Hence $g$ is also a bijection
\[
\mathbb Z_{>0}\longrightarrow\{2,3,4,\ldots\}.
\]
The only arithmetic progression with this image is
\[
g(n)=n+1.
\tag{3}
\]

Substituting (3) into (1),
\[
f^{\,b(a+1)}(a)=f^{\,a(b+1)}(b).
\]
Cancel the common $ab$ iterates:
\[
f^b(a)=f^a(b).
\tag{4}
\]
Taking $a=1$ and $b=n$,
\[
f^n(1)=f(n).
\]
Injectivity gives
\[
f^{n-1}(1)=n,
\]
and therefore
\[
f(n)=n+1.
\]
For this function, $f^r(x)=x+r$, so
\[
f^{\,b f(a)}(a+1)
=
a+1+b(a+1)
=
(a+1)f(b).
\]

SOLUTION 3:
We begin with the same elementary foundation, proved here for completeness.

The equations
\[
P(a,1)
\qquad\text{and}\qquad
P(a-1,b)
\]
show that the range of $f$ and every orbit beginning at an integer at least $2$ are infinite. Thus no positive iterate fixes such an integer.

If $f(b)=f(c)$ for $b<c$, comparison of $P(a,b)$ and $P(a,c)$ produces a periodic integer at least $2$, so $f$ is injective. Also, $1$ is not in the range: if $f(b)=1$, then $P(a,b)$ makes $a+1$ periodic.

For $a,b\ge2$, define
\[
u=f^{\,c f(a-1)-1}(a),
\qquad
v=f^{\,c f(b-1)-1}(b)
\]
with any fixed $c\ge2$. Then
\[
f(u)=af(c),\qquad f(v)=bf(c),
\]
and
\[
f^{\,v f(a-1)}(a)=abf(c)=f^{\,u f(b-1)}(b).
\]
Injectivity implies that $a=b$ or one is a forward iterate of the other.

Take $b=f(1)$. The value $f(1)$ cannot be a descendant of $a\ge2$, because injectivity would then force either $a=1$ or the value $1$ to lie in the range of $f$. Hence every integer at least $2$ is a descendant of $f(1)$ and therefore of $1$. All positive integers lie in the single nonperiodic orbit of $1$.

Define
\[
g(n)=f(f(n)-1).
\]
The substitution $P(f(a)-1,b)$ gives
\[
f^{\,b g(a)}(a)=f^{\,a g(b)}(b).
\tag{1}
\]

Suppose
\[
b=f^k(a)
\qquad(k\ge1).
\]
Then (1) becomes
\[
f^{\,b g(a)}(a)
=
f^{\,a g(b)+k}(a).
\]
The orbit has no repetitions, so
\[
b\,g(a)=a\,g(b)+k.
\tag{2}
\]

Put
\[
A_n=f^n(1)
\qquad(n\ge0).
\]
Applying (2) to the pairs
\[
(A_n,A_{n+1}),\qquad
(A_{n+1},A_{n+2}),\qquad
(A_n,A_{n+2}),
\]
gives
\[
A_{n+1}g(A_n)=A_ng(A_{n+1})+1,
\tag{3}
\]
\[
A_{n+2}g(A_{n+1})=A_{n+1}g(A_{n+2})+1,
\tag{4}
\]
and
\[
A_{n+2}g(A_n)=A_ng(A_{n+2})+2.
\tag{5}
\]

Multiply (3) by $A_{n+2}$ and (4) by $A_n$, then add. Using (5), we get
\[
2A_{n+1}=A_n+A_{n+2}.
\]
Thus $(A_n)$ is an arithmetic progression. Since
\[
A_0=1
\]
and the orbit contains every positive integer, its common difference must be $1$. Therefore
\[
f^n(1)=n+1.
\]
In particular,
\[
f(n)=f\bigl(f^{n-1}(1)\bigr)=f^n(1)=n+1.
\]
Again $f^r(x)=x+r$, and direct substitution gives
\[
f^{\,b f(a)}(a+1)=(a+1)f(b).
\]

SOLUTION 4:
We first record the basic consequences of the equation.

The range of $f$ is infinite by $P(a,1)$, and then $P(a-1,b)$ shows that every orbit beginning at an integer at least $2$ is infinite. Hence such an integer is never periodic.

If $f(b)=f(c)$ for $b<c$, comparison of $P(a,b)$ and $P(a,c)$ produces a periodic value, so $f$ is injective. The value $1$ is not in the range, since $f(b)=1$ would make $a+1$ periodic through $P(a,b)$.

For $a,b\ge2$, choose
\[
u=f^{\,c f(a-1)-1}(a),
\qquad
v=f^{\,c f(b-1)-1}(b).
\]
Then
\[
f(u)=af(c),\qquad f(v)=bf(c),
\]
and
\[
f^{\,v f(a-1)}(a)=abf(c)=f^{\,u f(b-1)}(b).
\]
Thus one of $a,b$ is a descendant of the other. For $a\ge2$, the value $f(1)$ cannot be a descendant of $a$, because injectivity would force either $a=1$ or $1$ to belong to the range of $f$. Therefore $a$ is a descendant of $f(1)$ and hence of $1$. Consequently,
\[
f:\mathbb Z_{>0}\to\{2,3,4,\ldots\}
\]
is a bijection.

For $a\ge2$, define $G(a)$ and $H(a)$ by
\[
f^{G(a)}(1)=a,
\qquad
H(a)=f(a-1).
\]
Then
\[
G:\{2,3,\ldots\}\to\mathbb Z_{>0}
\]
and
\[
H:\{2,3,\ldots\}\to\{2,3,\ldots\}
\]
are bijections.

Apply $P(a-1,b-1)$ for $a,b\ge2$. Since
\[
f^{G(a)}(1)=a,
\]
we obtain
\[
G\bigl(aH(b)\bigr)
=
G(a)+(b-1)H(a).
\tag{1}
\]

For fixed $a\ge2$, consider
\[
S_a=\{G(at):t\ge1\}.
\]
As $H(b)$ runs through all integers at least $2$, equation (1) gives
\[
S_a=G(a)+H(a)\mathbb Z_{\ge0}.
\tag{2}
\]

Since the positive common multiples of $a$ and $b$ are precisely the multiples of $\operatorname{lcm}(a,b)$,
\[
S_a\cap S_b=S_{\operatorname{lcm}(a,b)}.
\]
Comparing common differences in (2),
\[
H(\operatorname{lcm}(a,b))
=
\operatorname{lcm}(H(a),H(b)).
\tag{3}
\]
It follows that
\[
a\mid b
\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad
H(a)\mid H(b).
\tag{4}
\]

Extend $H$ by $H(1)=1$. Relation (4) shows that $H$ maps primes bijectively to primes. It also maps the divisor chain
\[
1,p,p^2,\ldots,p^e
\]
onto the full divisor chain of $H(p^e)$, so
\[
H(p^e)=H(p)^e.
\]
Therefore, if
\[
n=\prod_i p_i^{e_i},
\]
then
\[
H(n)=\prod_i H(p_i)^{e_i}.
\tag{5}
\]
In particular, $H$ is completely multiplicative and preserves gcd and lcm.

We next prove
\[
H(H(n))=n
\qquad(n\ge2).
\tag{6}
\]
It is enough to prove this for primes. Let $p\ne q$ be primes. Since $H(p)$ and $H(q)$ are distinct primes, they are coprime. The corresponding linear Diophantine equation has integer solutions, and by shifting a solution we may choose integers $x,y\ge2$ such that
\[
G(p)+(x-1)H(p)
=
G(q)+(y-1)H(q).
\tag{7}
\]
For every $k\ge0$, equation (1) and (7) give
\[
G\bigl(pH(x+kH(q))\bigr)
=
G\bigl(qH(y+kH(p))\bigr).
\]
Since $G$ is injective,
\[
pH(x+kH(q))
=
qH(y+kH(p)).
\tag{8}
\]
Thus $p$ divides
\[
H(y+kH(p))
\]
for $k=0,1$. Let $r$ be the prime with
\[
H(r)=p.
\]
By (4),
\[
r\mid y
\qquad\text{and}\qquad
r\mid y+H(p).
\]
Hence
\[
r\mid H(p).
\]
Both numbers are prime, so
\[
r=H(p),
\]
which means
\[
H(H(p))=p.
\]
Equation (6) follows from complete multiplicativity.

Replace $b$ by $H(b)$ in (1). Using (6),
\[
G(ab)=G(a)+(H(b)-1)H(a).
\tag{9}
\]
Interchanging $a$ and $b$ in (9),
\[
G(a)-H(a)=G(b)-H(b).
\]
Thus $G-H$ is constant. The image of $G$ is
\[
\{1,2,3,\ldots\},
\]
whereas the image of $H$ is
\[
\{2,3,4,\ldots\}.
\]
Therefore
\[
G(a)=H(a)-1.
\tag{10}
\]

Using (6) and (10),
\[
G(H(a))=H(H(a))-1=a-1.
\]
By the definitions of $G$ and $H$,
\[
f(a-1)
=
H(a)
=
f^{G(H(a))}(1)
=
f^{a-1}(1).
\]
Injectivity gives
\[
f^{a-2}(1)=a-1
\qquad(a\ge2).
\]
Hence
\[
f^n(1)=n+1
\qquad(n\ge0),
\]
and therefore
\[
f(n)=n+1
\]
for every positive integer $n$.

For this function,
\[
f^r(x)=x+r,
\]
and therefore
\[
f^{\,b f(a)}(a+1)
=
a+1+b(a+1)
=
(a+1)(b+1)
=
(a+1)f(b).
\]
Thus the function indeed satisfies the equation.
